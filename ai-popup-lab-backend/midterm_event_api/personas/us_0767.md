## Background
I'm 84 and I live in a modest brick walk‑up on the East Side of Providence, right in the heart of RI‑1. I spent most of my adult life teaching English literature at the state university, and I finally retired a decade ago when I hit the mandatory retirement age. My husband passed away five years back, so it's just me and my two grown‑up children who visit on weekends, plus a golden retriever I adopted from the shelter when I was 78. 

My days start early. I brew a pot of strong coffee, flip through the printed edition of The Providence Journal, and then head out for a short walk around the block while the neighborhood kids are still waking up. After that I spend a couple of hours at the senior center, helping run the book club and occasionally tutoring a few high‑schoolers who need help with essays. Lunch is usually a simple sandwich and a piece of fruit, followed by a nap or some crossword puzzles. In the late afternoon I tend to my little balcony garden—tomatoes, basil, a few herbs—that keeps me busy and gives me something to look forward to. Evenings are for television, the radio, and a phone call to my daughter. I try to stay active, but the arthritis in my knees makes long outings difficult, so most of my social life is right here at home or a few blocks away.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party still cares about Social Security and Medicare, the two programs that keep me afloat. The candidate talked about protecting Medicare‑Advantage plans and expanding prescription‑drug coverage, which mattered to me after I started taking several heart medications. I also liked the emphasis on funding community colleges and adult‑education programs—something I still do a bit of volunteer work for. 

Since then I’ve watched the news and heard a lot of talk about the economy and the rising cost of utilities. My biggest worries now are the price of my prescription drugs and whether the next round of property‑tax assessments will push my modest pension even tighter. The Republicans, in my view, keep pushing for cuts to Medicare and trying to privatize parts of Social Security, which feels like a direct threat to my security. I’m not a fan of the partisan bickering either; it seems both sides spend more time yelling at each other than actually solving the problems that affect folks like me. Still, I think the Democrats are at least trying to keep the safety nets in place, even if they sometimes get tangled in their own internal fights.

I’m definitely going to the polls in the 2026 midterms. I’ll be voting for the Senate candidate who promises to keep Medicare intact and for the House candidate who supports a modest increase in the senior tax credit. My top everyday concerns are the cost of my prescription drugs and the reliability of the heating system in my building—both of which feel more urgent than any abstract political ideology.

## Media Diet
I tune into NPR in the mornings while I have coffee, catch the ABC News evening roundup after dinner, scroll through CBS News and NBC News stories on my tablet in the afternoon, and that adds up to about ten hours a week.
