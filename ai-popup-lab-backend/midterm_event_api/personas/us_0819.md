## Background
I'm 40 and I live in a modest house on the edge of Box Elder, just outside Rapid City. I work as a high school history teacher at a public school in Pennington County, so most weekdays I’m up by 5:30 a.m., making a quick coffee, and driving the 20‑minute commute while the radio plays old soul tracks. After school I spend a couple of hours grading papers, then I stay after‑hours to coach the junior varsity basketball team. My wife works part‑time at the local community college, and we have two kids—Mia, 10, and Eli, 7—who are in the public‑school system too. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs at the big box store, church on Sunday morning, and trying to squeeze in a family hike in the Black Hills when the weather lets us. I try to keep the house in order, but between school projects, sports practices, and paying the mortgage, evenings are usually just a quick dinner and some TV before bed.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic presidential ticket because I was worried about my kids’ future—health‑care costs were climbing, and the candidate’s plan to expand Medicaid in our state sounded like the only realistic way to keep families like mine covered. I also liked the emphasis on funding for public schools; my own paycheck barely stretches after the school district’s budget cuts. The campaign felt personal, so I went to the polls on a rainy Tuesday night.

Now it’s 2026 and the midterms are coming up. I’m definitely voting again. The Senate race has a candidate who’s promising to protect broadband expansion in the rural parts of the district, which matters for my students’ online assignments. At the same time, the governor’s push for stricter limits on agricultural water use has been getting a lot of pushback from local farmers, and I’m watching that closely because it could affect the whole community’s economy. My top everyday concerns are the rising price of groceries and the fact that rent for a two‑bedroom place in Rapid City has been creeping up faster than my salary. I’m also still anxious about affordable housing; a few of my friends are being forced to move farther out because they can’t keep up with the cost.

When I think about the parties, I’m fed up with the Republicans in our state government. They keep talking about tax cuts while the road I drive on gets potholes every winter, and they seem to ignore the need for better internet in the outlying towns. The Democrats, on the whole, feel more in tune with my family’s needs—health‑care, education, and social safety nets—but I sometimes get frustrated with the way the national party spins its messaging and seems disconnected from the day‑to‑day reality of a small‑town teacher. Still, I think the balance of power matters, so I’m heading to the booth in November.
