## Background
I'm 20 and I work the late‑shift at a fast‑food restaurant near the I‑80 corridor. I live in a small duplex in Bellevue, just a few minutes from the river, sharing the place with my older sister who’s trying to finish a GED program. Our mom moved out a few years ago and sends money when she can, so we split rent and utilities between us. My day starts around 2 p.m. after I finish a quick shift at the grill, then I head home to catch the 6 p.m. bus to the grocery store where I pick up a part‑time stocker gig. The job is mostly stocking shelves and bagging, but it gives me enough cash to cover my phone bill and a little extra for weekend outings with friends.

When I’m not working, I hang out at the local skate park or stream video games in my room. I don’t have a driver’s license yet, so everything depends on the bus schedule. On Sundays I sometimes help my aunt with cleaning jobs around her rental properties; that’s how I pick up a few extra dollars for gas. I’m not in school, but I’m thinking about taking a community‑college class in basic computer skills if I can scrape together the tuition. My routine is pretty straightforward: work, eat, watch a few videos, and try to stay out of trouble.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. To be honest, I felt like none of the candidates cared about someone like me—just a kid working two minimum‑wage jobs and trying to keep a roof over my head. The whole process seemed like a big, boring ceremony that never changed anything for the people on the bottom. I also missed the registration deadline because I was busy covering a shift and didn’t get the reminder until after the cut‑off. It just reinforced the idea that voting is a hassle I can’t fit into my schedule.

I’m sitting this 2026 midterm out, too. Unless something blows up locally—like a sudden rent hike that forces us out of our duplex or a scandal that directly hits the jobs I rely on—I don’t see a reason to make the trip to the polling place. My top everyday worries are keeping up with rent and making sure grocery prices don’t keep climbing. When I think about the two big parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats sound like they care about “the little guy,” but their policies look like they’ll raise taxes or push more regulation that could shut down places like the fast‑food joint I work at. The Republicans keep talking about “law and order” and “cutting taxes,” but most of the time they seem to ignore the fact that a lot of us are just trying to get a paycheck that stretches to the end of the month. I end up feeling like the parties are just two sides of the same coin, both promising change that never lands in my world.
