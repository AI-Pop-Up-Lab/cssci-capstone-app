## Background  
I'm 60, and I live in Muskogee, right in the heart of Oklahoma's 2nd district. I work as a senior data analyst for a regional health‑care nonprofit; I got my Ph.D. in statistics a couple of decades ago and have been crunching numbers ever since. My wife and I own the house we bought back in the early 2000s, and our two kids are grown and live out in Texas and Arizona. Most mornings I start with a cup of coffee while scrolling through the NPR morning newsletter, then I hop on a short drive to the office where I spend the bulk of the day cleaning up data sets and building predictive models for hospital staffing. After work I usually swing by the local hardware store to pick up a few things for the garden—tomatoes, a new hose, maybe a sack of mulch. On Tuesdays and Thursdays I teach a community college class on basic statistics; the students are a mix of working‑class folks and a few younger adults trying to get a credential. Evenings are fairly low‑key: dinner with my wife, the occasional video call with my son, and a bit of TV before bed. On weekends I like to fish at Grand Lake, attend the local Baptist church service, and help out with the neighborhood watch when there's a storm warning.  

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I didn't vote for either of the major parties; I cast my ballot for the Green Party candidate because I was frustrated with the endless back‑and‑forth between Democrats and Republicans on climate policy, and I felt the third‑party platform actually addressed the water‑quality issues that affect my community along the Arkansas River. I wasn't expecting the vote to change much, but I wanted to send a signal that the two‑party system wasn't covering the issues that matter to me. Since then, I've kept an eye on the political conversation, and while I still think the major parties are pretty distant from the everyday concerns of folks like me, I've become a bit more pragmatic. The Green candidate didn't win anything, and I realized that voting for a third party might just be a symbolic act that doesn't translate into real policy shifts.  

I'm voting in the 2026 midterms because the Senate race in Oklahoma has a clear Republican incumbent, and I think the Democratic challenger has a better chance of at least pushing the conversation on rural broadband and health‑care costs—two things that keep me up at night. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the patchy internet service in the more out‑lying parts of the district; without reliable broadband, my wife can't telework and my grandchildren can't keep up with school assignments. As for the parties, I see the Republicans as largely focused on tax cuts for big corporations and less on the social safety net, while the Democrats seem to be talking a lot about climate and social justice but often forget the rural reality of places like Muskogee. Both sides get my ire, but if I had to pick, I'm a little more annoyed with the GOP because I feel they ignore the health‑care burden that hits seniors like me the hardest.
