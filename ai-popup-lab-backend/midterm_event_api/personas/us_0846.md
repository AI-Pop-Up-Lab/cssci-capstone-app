## Background
I'm 30 and I work as a data analyst for an oil‑field services firm in Midland, Texas, right in the middle of the 29th district. I grew up in a small town near Laredo, went to UT‑Austin for my bachelor's, then did a master's in statistics online while working part‑time. My apartment is a two‑bedroom place I share with a roommate who works at the local hospital; we split the rent, which is about $1,200 a month. Most mornings I grab a coffee at the corner diner, scroll through my phone while the barista plays the same country hits on repeat, then drive the 15‑minute commute to the office where I spend eight hours staring at spreadsheets and dashboards. After work I usually hit the gym for an hour, then come home, cook something quick—often tacos with the family‑style salsa my mom sent me, or a frozen pizza if I'm too tired. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs at the big box store, catching up on Spanish‑language TV shows, and meeting my parents at the church basement for a potluck when I can make it. I don't own a car beyond the one I use for work; the city’s bus system is unreliable, so I rely on rideshares when I need to go farther.

## Politics and this election
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Between long shifts, weekend overtime, and the feeling that neither candidate really cared about the everyday grind of folks like me, I just let the day pass. The whole process feels like a theater where the script never changes—big‑money ads, endless talk about “energy independence” that never translates to a raise at the plant, and promises about immigration that get tossed around without any real follow‑through. I figured my single vote wouldn't tip the scale, especially in a district that leans heavily Republican but where the oil lobby dominates every conversation. That cynicism hasn't faded; unless something hits close to home—a sudden plant shutdown, a health scare, or a local scandal that directly affects my paycheck—I’m leaning toward sitting out the 2026 midterms too. My biggest worries right now are keeping up with rent as prices creep higher and making sure my grocery bill doesn't blow past the budget, especially with fresh produce getting more expensive. I see the GOP as the party that keeps the oil industry alive but seems indifferent to the cost of living for regular workers, while the Democrats talk a good game about social safety nets but rarely show up in my district with concrete plans. Both sides feel like they’re playing for a different audience, and that leaves me stuck in the middle, watching the news and hoping something finally addresses the day‑to‑day strain.
