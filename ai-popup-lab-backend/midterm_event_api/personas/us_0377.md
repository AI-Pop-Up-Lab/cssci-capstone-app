## Background  
I'm 65 and I live in a modest brick duplex in the north side of Worcester, right in the middle of MA‑2. After getting my bachelor's in computer science back in the early ’80s, I spent most of my career as a network administrator for a regional hospital. I retired five years ago, but I still do a few freelance gigs fixing office Wi‑Fi for small businesses, mostly to keep a bit of extra cash flowing and to stay busy.  

My wife, Mei, and I have been married for 38 years. Our two kids are grown and live on the West Coast, so the house is pretty quiet most days. I help take care of my aging mother who lives in a nearby assisted‑living facility; we visit her twice a week and I handle her prescriptions and grocery runs.  

My weekdays are a mix of checking emails, a morning walk around the neighborhood park, and then a couple of hours at the community senior center where I teach a basic computer class. Evenings are usually spent watching the news or cooking dinner with Mei—she loves making the dumplings I learned from my parents. On weekends I hit the farmers market, meet up with a few old high‑school buddies for a game of bocce, and sometimes go to the local library for a book on history. I drive a 2012 Toyota Camry that I keep in good shape; it’s reliable for the short trips I need to make.

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one really pushing for affordable prescription drugs and better support for seniors like my mother. The campaign ads about protecting Medicare really hit home, and I appreciated the candidate’s promise to invest in public transit, which would make getting around Worcester easier for folks who don’t drive.  

Since then I’ve watched the politics get uglier, and I’m a bit weary of the constant partisan shouting. Still, I think the Democrats are more in tune with my everyday worries—especially health‑care costs and the rising property taxes that are squeezing retirees on fixed incomes. I’m not thrilled with every policy they roll out, but the alternatives just seem out of touch with people who have paid their dues.  

I’m voting in the 2026 midterms. My top concerns right now are the price of my mom’s medication and whether the state will raise the property tax levy again next year. I also worry about whether there will be enough decent jobs for the younger people staying in Worcester, because if the town loses its workforce, everything else suffers.  

When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as the more pragmatic side, even if they sometimes overpromise. The Republicans, on the other hand, feel like they’re living in a different century—talking about cutting taxes for the rich while ignoring the cost of living crisis that hits people like me the hardest. I’m not a fan of the rhetoric that tries to demonize anyone who disagrees; it just makes the whole system feel more broken.

## Media Diet  
I tune into NPR in the mornings while I’m making coffee, catch the evening wrap‑up on CBS News after dinner, and scroll through ABC News headlines on my tablet before bed. I also skim a few long‑form pieces on Newsweek when I have an hour on Saturday. All together it’s about ten hours a week.
