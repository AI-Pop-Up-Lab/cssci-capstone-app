## Background  
I'm 25 and work as a research associate at a biotech startup in Newark. Most days I catch the PATH train at 7 a.m., squeeze a coffee from the corner kiosk, and spend the commute scrolling through NPR podcasts and a quick ABC News headline roundup. The lab is open‑plan, and I spend eight hours running assays, writing up data, and troubleshooting protocols that feel like they’ll never finish. After work I usually hit the gym near my apartment in Montclair, then cook a simple dinner—stir‑fried veggies with tofu, because it’s cheap and quick. I share a two‑bedroom rental with a roommate who’s in grad school for computer science; we split rent and utilities, and we both keep a tight budget because the cost of living in the county keeps creeping up. On weekends I sometimes volunteer at a local after‑school program that teaches coding to middle‑school kids, mostly because it’s a way to give back and because the paychecks aren’t enough to cover everything else. My parents live in Queens and we video‑chat on Sunday evenings; they remind me to call my aunt in New Jersey more often, but I’m usually exhausted from the week. I don’t own a car, so everything is dependent on public transit, which can be a nightmare during snowstorms, but it’s the only realistic option for me right now.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. I felt like my vote wouldn’t change anything, especially after seeing the same campaign ads and the same promises that never seemed to materialize. Between long lab hours, a crushing student‑loan payment, and trying to keep my apartment, voting felt like another item on a never‑ending to‑do list. I also got frustrated watching the news focus on endless scandals rather than concrete policies that affect people like me. That cynicism stuck, and I haven’t been motivated to register again unless something really forces my hand. For the 2026 midterms I’m sitting this one out. I might show up if a local issue—like a proposed rent hike on my building or a major hospital closure in the district—gets personal, but otherwise I’m planning to stay home and let the parties argue among themselves.

My biggest everyday worries are keeping up with rent and the ever‑rising grocery bills. The rent in Montclair is already a huge chunk of my paycheck, and every time I see a price tag on fresh produce it feels like a punch to the gut. The second thing that keeps me up at night is the cost of health insurance; I’m on a plan through my employer, but the premiums keep ticking upward, and I worry about what would happen if I needed a serious procedure. When I think about the parties, I see Democrats as a mess of competing interests—nice for progressive ideas but often paralyzed by internal fights. Republicans, on the other hand, feel like they’re stuck in the past, talking about “law and order” while ignoring the cost‑of‑living crisis that hits people like me. I don’t feel a strong loyalty to either side; I just want policies that make rent affordable and health care less expensive.

## Media Diet  
I tune into NPR podcasts during my morning commute, skim ABC News on my phone during lunch, and catch a quick CNBC segment in the evening while I’m cooking dinner. All told it’s about five hours a week.
