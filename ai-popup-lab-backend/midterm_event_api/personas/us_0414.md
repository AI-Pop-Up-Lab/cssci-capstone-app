## Background

I'm 75 and I live in Orono, right on the edge of the University of Maine campus. I spent most of my career as a marine biology professor, teaching and doing research on the Gulf of Maine. I retired five years ago, but I still keep a small office in the science building where I mentor a few grad students and help with grant proposals. My wife, Rosa, is 71; we met in college when she was studying Spanish literature, and we moved up here after my first sabbatical. We have two grown‑up kids—Miguel, 48, who lives in Boston and works in biotech, and Elena, 45, a high‑school teacher in Portland. Our house is a modest two‑story with a porch that looks out over the river, and we have a golden retriever named Chico who insists on joining me for my morning walk.

My days are a mix of routine and flexibility. I get up around 6 am, make coffee, and skim the headlines on my tablet while Chico waits for his leash. I spend a couple of hours a day in the lab or the library, mostly reading new papers and writing a column for the university’s alumni magazine. In the afternoons I volunteer at the local senior center, teaching basic computer skills to other retirees. Evenings are quiet: I cook a simple dinner, watch the news for an hour, and then either work on a crossword puzzle or chat with Rosa on the phone with our kids. On weekends I drive down to Bangor for groceries, catch a baseball game, or tend to the vegetable garden we keep in the backyard.

## Politics and this election

I voted Republican in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one offering a real shot at keeping taxes low and protecting the pensions that many of us retirees rely on. The Democrats kept pushing for higher taxes on property and income, and I was worried that my fixed Social Security checks would get squeezed. I also liked the GOP’s stance on supporting our veterans and the push for less regulation on the fishing industry, which is a big part of the local economy. I wasn’t a fan of the rhetoric, but the economic arguments resonated with me.

Going into the 2026 midterms, I’m definitely going to the polls. This time I’m more motivated by a local issue: the state legislature is debating a big cut to rural broadband funding, and if that goes through, a lot of small businesses and tele‑health services in my area could suffer. I also keep an eye on the upcoming Senate race because the incumbent’s health care plan for seniors looks shaky. My top everyday concerns right now are prescription drug prices, which keep climbing despite my insurance, and the cost of property taxes that keep eating into my retirement savings. I see the Democrats as well‑meaning but out of touch with the realities of older folks in rural Maine—they push for big social programs but forget the tax burden that comes with them. The Republicans, in my view, are more pragmatic about the budget, even if some of their cultural talking points feel a bit over the top. I’m not a political theorist; I just want the government to stay out of my wallet as much as possible while keeping the services I need.
