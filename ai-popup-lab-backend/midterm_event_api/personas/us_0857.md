## Background
I'm 70, and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Midland, right in the middle of Texas 19. I spent most of my working life as a roughneck on the oil rigs that dot the Permian Basin, a job that never required a diploma—just stamina and a willingness to get dirty. I retired five years ago after a back injury forced me out, so now I spend my days fixing things around the house, helping my neighbor with his fence, and driving my old pickup to the local grocery store. My wife passed away three years back, and I have two grown children who live in Austin and Dallas; they call once a week, and my grandson visits in the summer and helps me with the new smartphone I finally got. I’m a member of the local Native American heritage association, which meets monthly to celebrate our traditions and discuss land issues. Most mornings I sit on the porch with a cup of black coffee, watch the sunrise, and listen to the radio for traffic updates before heading out to the senior center for bingo on Wednesdays.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Between the long wait at the polling place, the feeling that the candidates were all the same, and my doctor’s appointment that cut into voting hours, I just skipped it. I’ve always thought the system is set up for folks like me—older, less formally educated, working‑class—to feel ignored. The campaign ads never talked about the rising cost of my prescription meds or the fact that property taxes keep creeping up on my modest home. I figured my single vote wouldn’t change anything, and I was too tired to make a big deal out of it.

I'm sitting this one out again in 2026. Unless there’s a scandal that hits my town directly—like a refinery spill that threatens our water—or a candidate who actually promises to lower the premiums on my Medicare Part D, I don’t see a reason to stand in line. My biggest worries right now are the price of my insulin and the rising property tax bill that’s making it hard to keep the house in good shape. I also keep an eye on gas prices because even though I drive a pickup, I can’t afford big spikes.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty fed up with the Democrats. They talk about climate change and social programs, but they never bring jobs back to West Texas or address the health‑care gaps that hit people like me. The Republicans seem more in tune with the oil industry, but they keep cutting funding for senior services and pushing for higher taxes on the little stuff we actually need. It feels like a lose‑lose, so I stay home and hope the next round of candidates will finally notice the folks who work the rigs and live in the dust.
