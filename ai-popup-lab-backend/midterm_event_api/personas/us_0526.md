## Background
I'm 55 and work as a senior analyst for a nonprofit that focuses on workforce development in Roanoke Rapids, the biggest town in my part of NC‑1. I have a master's in public administration, so most of my day is spent pulling data, writing grant reports, and meeting with local employers about apprenticeship programs. I live in a modest two‑bedroom house with my sister, who moved back after a divorce. We each have a room, share the kitchen, and take turns cooking dinner—mostly something quick like fried catfish and collard greens because the kids are gone and we’re both tired after work. My sister’s teenage son visits on weekends, so the house gets a bit noisy when we’re trying to watch the news.

My routine starts early: I’m up by 5 a.m., make coffee, and check my phone for any urgent emails before the kids leave for school. I drive the 20‑minute commute to the nonprofit office, sit at a desk with a dual monitor setup, and spend most of the day on spreadsheets. Lunch is usually a sandwich I bring from home, eaten at a small break‑room table with a couple of coworkers. After work I stop at the grocery store, pick up a few things for dinner, and then head home to unwind with a bit of TV or a podcast while my sister does the dishes. On Saturdays I’m at the local church for the morning service, then help out with the community garden. Sundays are quiet; I read a novel or catch up on the news before the week starts again.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my ballot didn’t matter—there was a lot of talk about the same old promises, and the candidates both seemed to be talking past each other on issues that affect my community. Between long work hours, caring for my sister’s son, and trying to keep the house in order, voting felt like another chore I could skip. I also got discouraged by the endless stream of negative ads and the feeling that the big parties were just playing a game with my neighborhood’s needs. That cynicism hasn’t gone away, so I’m sitting this 2026 midterm out too, unless something really forces me to show up, like a local ballot measure that could directly affect our community garden funding or a scandal that makes the race suddenly matter to me.

My biggest everyday worries are the rising cost of groceries and the stability of my sister’s job. Prices at the market keep creeping up, and even though my salary is decent for the area, every extra dollar counts when we’re trying to keep the house in good shape and pay for the kid’s school supplies. The other concern is the local job market—if the nonprofit loses funding, we both could be out of work, and that would hit us hard.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with the Republicans. Their rhetoric on taxes and social programs feels out of touch with people like me who rely on community services. The Democrats seem more aligned with my values on education and health care, but I’m also wary of the same old “talk the talk, walk the walk” routine. I don’t feel a strong loyalty to either side; I just want policies that actually help working families and keep the neighborhood safe.

## Media Diet
I spend about four hours a week scrolling through NPR podcasts in the mornings and catching the evening headlines on CBS News and ABC News. Most of it is while I’m cooking or driving home from work.
