## Background
I'm 84, and I live in a modest ranch‑style house on the outskirts of Midland, right in Texas’ 38th district. My husband passed away three years ago, so I’m mostly on my own now, though my daughter visits on Sundays and helps with grocery runs. I earned an associate’s degree in business administration from a community college back in the early ’70s, and after a long career as a clerk at the local bank I retired at 68. These days I volunteer a few mornings a week at the senior center, signing people up for the bingo nights and handing out flyers for the health‑screening clinics. I still drive my old sedan to the pharmacy and the grocery store, usually leaving around 9 a.m. to avoid the heat. My mornings are quiet: I make a cup of tea, read the newspaper (the print edition, because I like the feel of paper), and check my blood pressure. In the afternoons I tend to my little garden of tomatoes and peppers, and in the evenings I sit on the porch watching the sky while the TV is on low. I keep a small stash of cash for emergencies and pay my property taxes and utility bills on time, though every year the numbers seem to creep up a little more.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. By the time the primaries rolled around, I felt the whole process was just a circus—candidates shouting over each other, ads promising everything, and none of it seemed to touch my daily worries. I was also dealing with a bout of pneumonia that month, and the recovery left me exhausted and skeptical about spending another weekend at a polling place that felt crowded and chaotic. I’ve always thought my single vote wouldn’t change anything, so I stayed home. That feeling hasn’t really shifted. I’m sitting this one out too, unless something dramatic hits our community—like a major hospital closing or a sudden spike in property taxes that forces me to sell the house. My top concerns right now are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the ever‑increasing property tax bill that keeps eating into my fixed income. I’m also watching grocery prices; a loaf of bread now costs more than it did when I was a teenager.

When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as a distant, idealistic crowd that talks about climate change and student debt while ignoring the realities of seniors who need affordable health care. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep promising lower taxes and “small government,” but I feel they forget about the seniors who rely on Medicare and need reliable public services. I’m not a die‑hard partisan; I just get annoyed when either side seems to think they have all the answers without listening to folks like me who are trying to make ends meet on a limited pension.
