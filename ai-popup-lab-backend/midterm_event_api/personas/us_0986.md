## Background
I'm 45 and I live on the Wind River Reservation, just outside the small town of Fort Washakie. I work as a case manager for the tribal health clinic, helping families navigate Medicaid and the occasional specialty referral. The job is a 9‑to‑5 grind, but I usually stay late when there’s a crisis or a language barrier to sort out. My husband runs a cattle operation a half‑hour away, and we have two kids: a 16‑year‑old son who’s thinking about joining the Air Force, and a 12‑year‑old daughter who loves skateboarding and spends most weekends at the community center.

Mornings start with a quick coffee, a glance at the weather app, and a drive to the clinic where I spend the first half‑hour checking electronic records. After work I pick up the kids from school, swing by the grocery store for milk and beans, then we all eat dinner together while the TV is off and someone is scrolling on their phone. Weekends are a mix of helping the church with the food pantry, taking the kids to the high‑school basketball game, and sometimes helping my husband fix a fence or a broken gate on the ranch. I’m proud of my heritage, but I also feel the pull of the broader world, especially when it comes to health care and education for our community.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between the long drives to a polling place that’s over an hour away, the feeling that none of the candidates really cared about tribal health issues, and a work schedule that left me exhausted, I just stayed home. I’ve always thought the system was set up for people in the cities, not for folks like me who spend most of the day in a clinic or on a ranch. The whole thing felt like a circus—ads promising change, then the same old policies that barely touch our water rights or the funding for our schools.

I’m sitting this one out too, unless something dramatic happens—like a major cut to the Indian Health Service or a local scandal that hits my community directly. My top everyday worries are keeping the clinic funded enough to cover the rising cost of medicines and making sure the ranch can stay afloat when the price of feed spikes. I also worry about the next school year’s budget because my son’s future depends on good teachers who actually stay.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats talk a lot about social programs, but when it comes to tribal sovereignty they’re vague, and the funding they promise never seems to materialize. The Republicans push for less regulation and more drilling, which sometimes means more jobs, but they also ignore the environmental damage that threatens our water sources and sacred sites. I wish someone would actually sit down and listen to what our reservation needs instead of tossing out sound bites.
