## Background
I'm 45, and I live in a modest three‑bedroom house on the edge of Pittsfield, right where the old mill buildings give way to the newer strip malls. I work the night shift loading trucks at the regional distribution center that handles everything from canned beans to winter tires. The job is mostly standing, lifting, and moving pallets, and I get paid hourly with a little overtime when the holiday season rolls around. I never finished high school; I dropped out at 16 to help my mom cover the rent after my dad left. I picked up a GED later, but I never went back to school.

My partner, Lisa, works part‑time at a local grocery store, and we have two kids: a 12‑year‑old daughter who just started middle school and a 9‑year‑old son who loves basketball. Mornings are a blur of getting the kids ready, catching the early bus to the warehouse, and grabbing a coffee from the corner diner. Evenings are the only time I get to sit down with the family; we eat dinner, help with homework, and sometimes watch whatever is on the TV when I’m too tired to read. On weekends I try to do some yard work around the house, but more often I’m just sitting on the porch, listening to the radio, and waiting for the next paycheck.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus and none of the candidates really cared about folks like me who are just trying to keep a roof over our heads and food on the table. The campaign ads were all about big‑ticket issues that never touch the daily grind—tax cuts for the rich, foreign policy, climate talks that feel a million miles away from my truck route. I also work nights, so the polling places are closed when I’m free, and I never got the time to request a mail‑in ballot. The whole process just seemed like a waste of my limited energy.

I’m sitting this one out again in 2026. Unless something blows up locally—like a factory closing or a big price hike on utilities that hits us hard—I don’t see a reason to bother. My biggest worries right now are the rising cost of groceries and the fact that my kids’ school is underfunded; the district keeps talking about upgrades while we’re still waiting for a decent heating system in the house. The other thing is job security. The warehouse has been cutting shifts whenever demand dips, and there’s always the fear that automation will take my spot.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats promise more social programs, but the money never seems to trickle down to the neighborhoods that need it most, and I hear a lot of talk about “systemic change” that never translates into a stable paycheck. The Republicans talk about “law and order” and “lower taxes,” but most of that feels like a fantasy for people who already have a safety net. I guess I’m just fed up with the rhetoric and the feeling that politicians are playing chess while we’re stuck moving the same old pieces around.

## Media Diet
I usually start my day with NPR on the radio while I’m loading the trucks, then scroll ABC News on my phone in the evenings after the kids are in bed. On weekends I catch up on a couple of CBS News stories, adding up to about five hours a week.
