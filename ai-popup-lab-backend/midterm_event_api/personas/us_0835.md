## Background
I'm 65 and I live in a modest ranch‑style house on the edge of Jackson, Tennessee, right in the 4th congressional district. I spent most of my career as a civil engineer for the state Department of Transportation, and I earned a master's in structural engineering in the late 1990s, so I retired with a post‑grad credential. My wife of 38 years still works part‑time at the local library, and our two grown‑up kids live in Nashville and Memphis but come back for holidays. I also help my sister, who has early‑stage dementia, with appointments and grocery runs a few times a week. My days usually start with a cup of black coffee while I skim the headlines on my tablet, then a short walk around the cul‑de‑sac to check on the neighborhood kids playing hopscotch. By mid‑morning I’m either on a Zoom call with the volunteer board of the senior center or fixing something around the house—usually a leaky faucet or a loose railing. After lunch I tend to my garden, mostly tomatoes and peppers, and in the evenings I watch the news, read a mystery novel, or play chess with a neighbor at the community center. On Sundays I attend a non‑denominational service and then head to a family brunch that’s become a weekly ritual.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted Republican because I felt the party best protected the things I care about: low taxes, a strong national defense, and keeping regulations from choking the small‑business owners in my town. I was also reassured by the candidate’s promises to keep Medicare Part B premiums from rising, which matters a lot at my age. Since then I’ve watched the current administration’s handling of the budget and the fallout from the recent federal infrastructure bill, and I’m still convinced that the GOP’s focus on fiscal restraint aligns with my own worries about the growing national debt. I’m definitely going to the polls in the 2026 midterms; I don’t have the energy to sit this one out unless something really crazy happens locally. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax hikes the county commission has been pushing to fund school upgrades. I’m annoyed by the Democrats’ tendency to promise big programs without showing how they’ll pay for them, and I think they’re out of touch with rural voters who just want reliable roads and affordable healthcare. The Republicans, for all their flaws, at least talk about cutting wasteful spending and give me hope that my retirement savings won’t be eroded by runaway inflation. I’m not a policy wonk, but I know when a candidate’s message feels like it’s aimed at people like me.
