## Background  
I'm 20 and I go to school at Providence College, majoring in political science. To pay the tuition and keep the lights on in my tiny off‑campus apartment, I work evenings as a barista at a coffee shop near the campus quad. My parents live a couple of towns away in Pawtucket, so I drive back on Sundays to help with groceries and to spend a little time with my younger brother, who is still in high school. Most weekdays I wake up at 7 am, grab a coffee I made the night before, and sprint to my first class at 8:30. Between 10 and noon I squeeze in a study session at the library, then head to the shop for a shift that runs from 4 pm to 9 pm. On Saturdays I either meet up with a group of friends at the local park or volunteer at a community garden that supplies fresh produce to a nearby food pantry. My routine is a mix of lecture halls, espresso machines, and the occasional late‑night Netflix binge when I finally have a break. I’m fluent in Spanish at home and English at school, which sometimes lands me translating for classmates or helping my parents navigate paperwork.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was the only one pushing for the things that mattered to me: more affordable student loans, stronger climate action, and expanded health care coverage. I remember standing in line at the campus voting booth, listening to a friend talk about how the new tuition‑freeze proposal could actually make a dent in my debt. That felt real and immediate, so I handed over my ballot. Since then I’ve kept tabs on what the administration is doing about climate legislation and the rollout of the student‑debt relief program, and I’m still pretty convinced the Democrats are moving in the right direction, even if they sometimes stumble over internal fights.  

For the 2026 midterms I’m definitely voting. My top everyday worries are rent—my lease is up next spring and prices in Providence keep climbing—and finding a stable job after graduation, because the part‑time barista gig won’t cut it forever. I’m also watching the local race for a seat on the state senate; the candidate who’s promising to push for more affordable housing in the district caught my eye.  

When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as the ones who at least talk about the issues that affect my life, even if they’re not perfect. The Republicans, on the other hand, feel like they’re stuck in a different era, focused on tax cuts for the wealthy and rolling back environmental protections that matter to my community. I’m not a political scholar, but I feel more comfortable with the side that’s trying to address climate change and student debt, even if I’m frustrated by how slow progress can be.
