## Background  
I'm 40 and I live in Owasso, a suburb on the north side of Tulsa in Oklahoma's 1st congressional district. I work as a shift supervisor at a regional warehouse that ships building supplies across the state. I didn't finish my associate's degree in business because I needed to start making money right after high school, but I still take a few night classes in logistics when I can. My wife works part‑time at a local pharmacy, and we have two kids—Liam, who's 8, and Maya, who's 5. Most mornings I drop the kids off at the elementary school on time, then hop into my pickup and drive the half‑hour to the warehouse. My shift runs from 7 a.m. to 4 p.m., with a short lunch break where I usually scroll through headlines on my phone. After work I pick the kids up, help with homework, and try to get a quick dinner on the stove before we all collapse on the couch. On weekends I do yard work, take the kids to the park, and sometimes help my brother move furniture for extra cash. I watch a lot of sports on TV, especially college football, and I keep a small garden in the backyard where I grow tomatoes and peppers.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I just felt like none of the choices mattered to me. The campaign ads were all the same—big promises, no real talk about the problems I deal with daily, like rising gas prices and the cost of keeping the house in shape. Between long shifts, kids' school events, and trying to stretch the paycheck, I couldn't find the time or the energy to get to a polling place, and I figured my single vote wouldn't change anything. That feeling of cynicism hasn't gone away. I'm still sitting this one out in the 2026 midterms unless something blows up locally—maybe a big factory closure or a scandal that hits my community directly. My biggest worries right now are keeping up with the mortgage and property taxes, and making sure the kids have enough money for school supplies and extracurriculars. I also keep an eye on the price at the pump; when diesel goes up, my whole schedule gets messier.  

When I think about the parties, I tend to see the Democrats as out of touch with the working‑class folks in Oklahoma. They keep talking about big‑city issues, climate policies that feel like a luxury, and they never seem to understand how hard it is to keep a family afloat on a modest wage. The Republicans, on the other hand, feel like they're always fighting cultural battles that don't affect my day‑to‑day life. I get annoyed when they focus on hot‑button topics instead of fixing the road infrastructure or supporting small businesses like the warehouse I work at. Still, if I had to pick one, I'd lean a bit more toward the GOP because at least they talk about lower taxes and less regulation, which seems more relevant to my paycheck. But I'm not a die‑hard fan of either side; I just want policies that make rent, gas, and groceries less of a headache.
