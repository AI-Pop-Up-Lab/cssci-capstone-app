## Background
I'm 55 and I live in Littleton, a small town up in the northern part of New Hampshire's 1st congressional district. I work as a manager at a regional outdoor‑gear retail store, which means I’m on my feet most of the day, opening the shop at nine, dealing with a mix of tourists and locals, and closing up around six. I finished two years of community college before I took the job, but I never went back for a degree. My husband, who’s retired from the state highway department, helps out with the bookkeeping, and our two kids have moved out—one in Boston, the other in Portland—so the house feels quieter now. I still drive my dad around to his dialysis appointments twice a week, and my mom lives a couple of towns over, so I’m often the one coordinating family meals and doctor visits.

My day starts with a quick coffee while scrolling through my phone for the morning headlines, then I head to the store. After work I usually stop by the local gym for a spin class, or I meet a neighbor for a walk on the trail behind the town hall. Evenings are low‑key: I cook dinner, help my husband with his crossword, and sometimes I’m on the porch watching the sunset over the White Mountains while the kids call on FaceTime. On weekends I volunteer at the community center, teaching basic English to new immigrants, which keeps my Spanish handy.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I didn’t vote for a Democrat or a Republican; I cast my ballot for the Green Party candidate. I was fed up with the two‑party circus, especially after the endless bickering over the budget that left my town’s road repairs in limbo and the grocery store’s prices creeping up. The Green candidate talked about protecting the forests around here and investing in renewable energy, which felt more relevant to my life than the usual promises about national security. I also liked that the campaign was run by a small group of locals, not big money.

Since then I’ve kept an eye on the political scene, but my enthusiasm hasn’t really grown. I still think both major parties are more interested in fundraising than solving real problems. The Democrats seem to forget the rural concerns—like keeping the timber industry alive—while the Republicans keep pushing for cuts that would hit the social services my aging parents rely on. That’s why I’m still leaning toward a third‑party or an independent who actually talks about fixing the water system in Littleton and tackling the rising property taxes that are squeezing a lot of us homeowners.

I’m definitely going to vote in the 2026 midterms, but I’m not planning on a straight ticket. I’ll probably look at the ballot until the last minute and pick whoever seems the least damaging. My top everyday worries are the property tax hikes that are making it harder to stay in my house and the grocery bills that keep climbing, especially for fresh produce. I’m also watching health‑care costs because my dad’s dialysis appointments are getting more expensive. When I think about the parties, I’m more annoyed with the Republicans for cutting back on social programs, but I’m also skeptical of the Democrats because they keep promising big federal projects that never materialize in our corner of New Hampshire.
