## Background
I'm 30 and I live in Fairbanks, right in the middle of Alaska's 1st congressional district. I work as a GIS analyst for the state Department of Transportation, which means most days I'm hunched over a computer mapping out road maintenance routes while the wind howls outside. I got my bachelor's in geography from the University of Alaska Fairbanks, so I can actually read those maps without squinting. I share a modest two‑bedroom apartment with my partner, Maya, and our big black Labrador, Koda. We split chores—she cooks most nights, I handle the laundry and keep the place warm during the long, bitter winters.

My day starts around 6 am with a strong cup of coffee and a quick scan of the weather forecast on my phone. I drive the 15‑minute commute to the office, which is basically a small building attached to the main highway hub. Work is a mix of data crunching, field trips to check road conditions, and occasional trips to remote villages to update their mapping data. I usually leave the office around 5 pm, swing by the grocery store for frozen fish and some fresh produce, then head home to binge a bit of Netflix or help Maya with her art projects. On weekends I love to go fishing on the Chena River, hike the nearby trails, or just relax in the hot tub while the northern lights flicker overhead. My parents live up in Anchorage, so I fly out to see them a few times a year, mostly for holidays.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus that never really addressed the stuff that matters to me—like how to keep heating costs down when the gas bills skyrocket every winter, or how to get reliable broadband in the out‑lying villages. Between long shifts, weekend trips, and the feeling that both parties just spout the same promises, I figured my single ballot wouldn't move the needle. I was also pretty fed up with the endless campaign ads that seemed more about attacking the other side than offering real solutions for Alaskans.

I'm sitting this one out again for the 2026 midterms. Unless something dramatic hits—like a major pipeline shutdown that threatens a lot of jobs, or a sudden spike in fuel taxes that makes my paycheck disappear—I don't see a reason to make the trip to the polling place. My top everyday worries are keeping the heat on without breaking the bank and making sure my internet connection stays solid enough for work and school. When I think about the parties, the Democrats keep pushing climate policies that sound good on paper but feel like they're ignoring the reality of mining towns and the people who rely on those jobs. The Republicans talk a lot about oil and gas, but they rarely address the high cost of living or the need for better infrastructure up here. Both sides seem to think they know what's best for Alaska, but neither really gets the day‑to‑day grind we live through.
