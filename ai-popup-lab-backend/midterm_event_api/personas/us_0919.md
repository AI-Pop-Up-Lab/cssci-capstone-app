## Background
I'm 30 and I live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment in Burlington, Vermont, just a few blocks from the waterfront. I grew up in a Spanish‑speaking household in a small town near the Canadian border, and my parents still run a grocery store back there, so I fly home for holidays and help out when they need extra hands. After high school I went to a community college in nearby Plattsburgh, took some business classes and a bit of environmental science, but never finished a degree because I needed to start working to support my younger sister who’s in college.

These days I work as a sales associate at a local outdoor gear shop. The job is mostly on my feet, stocking racks, chatting with hikers about trail conditions, and ringing up customers who are buying everything from reusable water bottles to snowshoes. My schedule is pretty regular: I start my shift at 9 a.m., take a half‑hour lunch at the nearby farmer’s market where I pick up a fresh bagel and a cup of coffee, and finish around 5 p.m. After work I often bike around town, sometimes stopping at the community garden where I tend a small plot of tomatoes and herbs. I share the apartment with a roommate who works as a software developer; we split rent, utilities, and the occasional grocery run. On weekends I either hike the Green Mountains, volunteer at the local food pantry, or catch a game at the university’s arena. I’m not married, and I don’t have kids, but I stay in touch with my family back in the border town through weekly FaceTime calls.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic ticket in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one taking climate change seriously, and the candidate’s plan to expand broadband in rural Vermont hit close to home. I also appreciated the push for a public option in health care; the rising cost of my sister’s prescription meds was a constant worry. The campaign trail was noisy, but the local town hall where the candidate answered questions about farm subsidies convinced me that this was the side that cared about everyday people like me.

Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how things are moving. I’m still pretty sure the Democrats are the better bet for protecting the environment and keeping health costs down, but I get frustrated when they spin their wheels on tax reforms that would help low‑income renters—like me. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more interested in cutting taxes for big corporations and rolling back regulations that keep our air clean. I don’t feel they understand what it’s like to worry about rent hikes every month or to watch grocery prices climb faster than my paycheck.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of rent and the price of groceries, especially fresh produce. I also worry about the reliability of internet service in the more remote parts of the state, since a lot of my work and my sister’s schoolwork depend on a stable connection. I tend to view the Democratic Party as the one that at least tries to address these issues, even if they’re sometimes slow or messy. The Republican Party feels like it’s more focused on issues that don’t affect my day‑to‑day life, and I’m skeptical of their promises to “let the market fix everything.”
