## Background  
I'm 45 and work as a floor supervisor at a large department store in the Lincoln Square area of Manhattan. I finished a couple of years of community college but never earned a degree, so most of my on‑the‑job training came from the store's internal programs. My husband drives a cab, and we have two kids: a 17‑year‑old junior in high school and a 14‑year‑old sophomore. We rent a two‑bedroom apartment on Broadway; the landlord keeps raising the lease and the building's elevator is always out of service.  

My weekdays start early: I drop the kids at the school across the street, catch the subway to the store, and spend eight hours on the floor making sure the merch is stocked and the checkout lines move. After work I sometimes pick up a shift at the grocery store to cover the rent gap, then we eat dinner together—usually something quick like pasta or takeout. Weekends are a mix of homework help, a few hours at the local community center where I volunteer to teach basic computer skills, and trying to catch a Broadway matinee when I can afford it. I listen to podcasts on the subway and scroll through the news feeds on my phone while waiting for the kids after school.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Between the endless campaign ads, the feeling that the big parties only talk about the same old stuff, and the fact that my shift schedule was changed at the last minute, I just stayed home. It felt like my vote wouldn't change anything, especially when the candidates kept promising lower rent but never delivering. That cynicism hasn't faded; the housing crisis still bites hard, and the political talk in the office feels like background noise. I'm planning to sit this one out again in the 2026 midterms unless something really shakes up my neighborhood—like a sudden rent freeze or a major layoff at the store that forces us to move.  

My biggest worries are the rent hikes that keep pushing us toward the edge of the borough and the rising grocery prices that make feeding the kids harder each month. The political parties both seem to be playing a game of blame: Democrats promise progressive housing policies but get tangled in bureaucracy, while Republicans talk about cutting taxes but ignore the cost of living in a city like New York. I don't feel any strong loyalty; I just want practical solutions, not more rhetoric.
