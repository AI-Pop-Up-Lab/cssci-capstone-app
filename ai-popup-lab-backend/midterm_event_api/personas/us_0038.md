## Background
I'm 35 and work as an HVAC technician for a regional service company that mainly serves the Huntsville metro area. I live in Harvest, a small unincorporated community just outside the city limits, in a modest ranch‑style house I bought with my wife a few years back. We have a seven‑year‑old son who goes to the local elementary school, and my wife works part‑time at a nearby grocery store while I’m on call for emergency repairs most evenings. My day starts early—around 5:30 a.m.—with a quick coffee, a look at the weather, and a drive to the shop where I get my truck and a stack of work orders. The job is physical, and I’m on my feet a lot, crawling into cramped attic spaces or climbing ladders on rooftops. After a typical eight‑hour shift, I’m home around 6 p.m., grab dinner with the family, help my son with his math homework, and then either head out for a quick run to the gym or sit on the porch watching the news on my phone. Weekends are a mix of yard work, a church service on Sunday mornings, and catching up on any overtime jobs that come up. I don’t have much time for hobbies beyond fishing trips with a couple of buddies when the weather’s right.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in the 2024 presidential election. Between long hours, being on call for emergency repairs, and trying to keep up with bills, I felt like my vote wouldn’t change anything. The whole process seemed like a circus—campaign ads everywhere, endless talk about “the future” that never matched the reality of my paycheck and rising gas prices. I also got the sense that both parties were talking past each other, promising big changes that never materialized in my town. That feeling of cynicism turned into a habit; I stopped even registering for local primaries because it felt like a waste of time. As the 2026 midterms roll around, I’m still planning to sit this one out. Unless there’s a local issue that hits my family directly—like a proposal to raise property taxes on my neighborhood or a plan that would cut funding for the community college program I once took—I don’t see a reason to go to the polls.

My biggest everyday concerns are keeping my gas tank full enough to get to work and making sure my mortgage payments stay manageable. The cost of living has crept up, and every extra dollar on the pump or the grocery store feels like a hit to the family budget. When I think about the parties, the Democrats look like they’re pushing a lot of “big‑government” ideas that would mean higher taxes and more regulation on small businesses like the ones I rely on for parts and supplies. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more in tune with my desire for lower taxes and less red tape, even if some of their rhetoric feels a bit over the top. Overall, I’m annoyed by the Democrats’ focus on social issues that don’t affect my day‑to‑day life, and I’m wary of the GOP because some of their policies can be too extreme for a community that values tradition but also needs practical solutions.
