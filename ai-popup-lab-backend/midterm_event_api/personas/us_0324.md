## Background
I'm 70, and I live in a modest house on the edge of Madisonville, Kentucky, in the 2nd congressional district. I spent most of my adult life working as a housekeeper for a local hotel and later helped my husband run a small corner store before he passed away ten years ago. I’m retired now, but I still do a bit of odd‑job cleaning for neighbors when they need a hand. My two grown children live in Cincinnati and Louisville, so I get occasional visits from my grandchildren in the summer. Most mornings I brew a strong cup of coffee, turn on the local CBS affiliate, and flip through the paper (I still get a printed copy from the post office). After that I take a short walk around the neighborhood, check on my garden tomatoes, and head to the grocery store before the crowds build up. In the afternoons I usually watch TV, catch up on the news, or sit on the porch knitting. Sundays are for church—my small Hispanic congregation meets at the community center, and we share a potluck after the service. I try to keep active, but the aches in my knees remind me I'm not as spry as I used to be.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like none of the candidates cared about folks like me. The ballots were a blur of names I couldn't recognize, and the ads kept talking about stuff that never touched my daily life—foreign policy, tech regulation—while I was just trying to keep my medication affordable and my grocery bill from blowing up. My husband used to say, “Politics is a game for the rich,” and after he died I stopped bothering with it. I work a few hours at the store and take care of my health, so there’s never enough time or energy to get tangled up in campaign rallies or even the polling place. The whole system feels distant, and unless something hits close to home—like a road closing that cuts off my church or a sudden jump in prescription costs—I’ll probably sit this one out again. So, I'm sitting this one out in the 2026 midterms, too.

My biggest worries right now are the rising price of groceries and the cost of my prescription drugs. Every month I have to stretch the budget a little thinner, and a sudden price hike on insulin would be a disaster. I also worry about property taxes; the county keeps raising them, and my fixed income can’t keep up. As for the parties, I see the Democrats as promising a lot of change but never delivering anything that helps an older, low‑income Hispanic woman in rural Kentucky. The Republicans talk tough on taxes and “family values,” but most of the time they push policies that make my utilities more expensive or cut funding for community health clinics. Both sides feel like they’re talking past people like me, and I’m left watching the news and shaking my head.
