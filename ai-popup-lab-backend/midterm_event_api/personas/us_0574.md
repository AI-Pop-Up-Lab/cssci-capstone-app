## Background
I'm 60, and I live in Kearney, Nebraska, right in the middle of the 3rd congressional district. I work as a civil engineer for the county public works department; I've got a master's in structural engineering, and after a stint in the oil field I settled here about 25 years ago. My wife, Rosa, runs a small catering business out of our house, and our two kids are both out of college—one’s a teacher in Omaha, the other is married and lives in Colorado. We help my mother, who’s in her late 80s, with a few trips to the doctor each month, and we take turns with my brother’s family looking after his dad, who’s got early‑stage dementia.

My day starts with a strong cup of coffee while I skim the morning headlines on my phone, then a short drive to the county offices where I spend most of the day checking bridge inspections and reviewing grant applications for road projects. Lunch is usually a quick sandwich at a local deli; sometimes I’ll sit with a couple of coworkers and talk about the upcoming storm season. After work I either head to the gym for a light workout or help Rosa with deliveries for a catering order. Evenings are a mix of watching the news, catching up on a Spanish‑language telenovela with Rosa, and helping my teenage grandson with his math homework when he visits on weekends. On Saturdays I volunteer a few hours with a local Habitat for Humanity crew, and Sundays are family—big brunch with cousins and a walk around the park.

## Politics and this election
I voted Republican in 2024 because I felt the party’s message on taxes and regulation was the only thing keeping my paycheck from disappearing. The GOP’s promise to keep the federal budget in check and to protect the border resonated with me, especially after seeing a few of my friends lose jobs at a nearby manufacturing plant when new environmental rules tightened. I also liked the candidate’s emphasis on supporting agriculture and infrastructure—my work literally depends on those federal road funds.

Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how the administration’s policies have played out. The tax cuts helped my family save a bit, but the rising cost of gasoline and the occasional shortage of construction materials have made my job harder. I’m still convinced that a smaller government and fewer regulations are the best way to keep my community’s economy stable, but I’m also getting fed up with the constant partisan bickering on TV. I think the Democrats are still trying to push a “big‑government” agenda that would raise my property taxes and make it harder for small businesses like Rosa’s to survive.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are the price at the pump and the cost of health insurance for my aging parents. I also worry about the state’s property tax rates—if they go up any more, it’ll squeeze my retirement savings. As for the parties, I see the Republicans as the ones who actually listen to folks like me who work with our roads and bridges, while the Democrats seem more interested in identity politics and expanding the welfare state, which feels out of touch with the practical problems I see every day.
