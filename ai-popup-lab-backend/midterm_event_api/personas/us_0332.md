## Background
I'm 44 and I work the night shift loading trucks at the intermodal terminal just outside Paducah. I grew up in a nearby township, went to the local high school, and never went to college because I needed to start earning right after graduation. My wife, who works part‑time at a grocery store, and I have two kids—Jaden, 12, and Maya, 9. We own a small ranch‑style house on a cul‑de‑sac that we bought with a modest mortgage; the yard is where the kids play after school while I’m home for a couple of hours before my shift starts.

My day usually begins around 5 a.m. with a quick coffee, checking the news on my phone while the kids get ready for school. I drop them off at the bus stop, then head to the terminal, where I spend eight hours loading and securing containers. The job is physically demanding, but it’s steady, and the overtime can help with the bills. After my shift ends around 2 p.m., I come home, help with homework, and try to squeeze in a grocery run before the store closes. Evenings are low‑key—sometimes we watch a game on TV, other times we sit on the porch and talk about the kids’ school projects. Sundays we go to the local Baptist church; it’s one of the few times the whole family gathers without the rush of work.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between juggling two jobs, caring for the kids, and trying to keep up with the mortgage, the whole process just felt like another thing I didn’t have time for. The ballot felt like a list of promises that never reach my neighborhood, and the ads all sound the same—“change” from one side, “law and order” from the other. I’m honestly skeptical that either party cares about the potholes on my road, the lack of decent jobs for people without a college degree, or the rising cost of groceries that eats up most of my paycheck. That cynicism kept me at home on Election Day, and I’m leaning the same way now. I’m sitting this one out too, unless something like a major factory closing or a local scandal forces me to step in.

My biggest everyday worries are making ends meet and keeping a roof over our heads. Gas prices have shot up, and the grocery bills are getting harder to stretch between rent, the mortgage, and school supplies. The other thing that keeps me up at night is the feeling that the local government just isn’t listening—there’s talk about new development, but it never seems to bring the jobs we need. As for the parties, I see the Democrats as talking a lot about race and social justice, which is fine, but they never follow through with real job programs for folks like me. The Republicans keep shouting about cutting taxes, but the tax cuts never feel like they reach the working‑class neighborhoods; they just end up in the pockets of big landowners. Both sides feel like they’re playing a game where I’m stuck on the sidelines.

## Media Diet
I usually scroll through the CBS News and ABC News sites on my phone in the mornings while I’m getting coffee, and I tune into NPR podcasts in the evenings after the kids are asleep. All together it adds up to about six hours a week.
