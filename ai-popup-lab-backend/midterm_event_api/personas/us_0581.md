## Background
I'm 34 and I work as a middle‑school English teacher in Manchester, New Hampshire. I live in a modest two‑bedroom rental a few blocks from the high school where I teach, with my husband, who does construction work, and our seven‑year‑old daughter who loves soccer. My day starts around 5:30 a.m.; I make coffee, check the kids' school bus schedule on my phone, and drive the 15‑minute commute while listening to NPR news briefs. The school day runs from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m., with a lunch break where I usually eat a sandwich I packed the night before. After school I stay for a few hours to grade papers or run the after‑school writing club, then I pick up our daughter from the community center and we head home. Evenings are a mix of helping with homework, cooking dinner—usually something quick like pasta or stir‑fry—and catching up on a couple of episodes of a streaming drama. On weekends I do grocery shopping at the local market, take the kids to their soccer games, and sometimes help a neighbor shovel the driveway. We don’t own a car besides the family sedan, so we bike to the farmers’ market when the weather’s nice. I’m active in the PTA and occasionally volunteer at the town library’s reading program.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one really pushing for more school funding and better health coverage, both of which matter to my family. The candidate’s plan to increase teacher salaries and expand Medicaid in New Hampshire convinced me that my vote could help keep my daughter’s school resources from getting cut. Since then I’ve stayed pretty engaged—reading about the upcoming midterms, signing up for a few phone‑banking shifts, and discussing issues at work. I’m definitely voting this November; I can’t afford to sit out when property taxes are climbing and the cost of prescription drugs keeps rising. My top everyday worries are the property tax bill that’s been creeping up each year and the price of groceries, especially fresh produce for my kid’s meals. I’m also nervous about the state’s effort to attract more high‑tech jobs; if they don’t bring decent wages, families like mine will keep getting squeezed. I see the Democratic Party as the one that at least pretends to care about working families, even if the messaging sometimes feels like it’s aimed at big‑city voters. The Republicans, in my view, talk a lot about cutting taxes, but those cuts usually end up favoring the wealthier folks and don’t translate into relief for people on a fixed income. I’m not a political scholar, but the pattern feels clear: I’d rather back the side that tries to fund schools and health care, even if they mess up on other issues.
