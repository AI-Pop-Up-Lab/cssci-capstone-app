## Background
I'm 45, and I live in the little town of Abingdon, right on the edge of the Blue Ridge. I work full‑time as an administrative assistant for a regional health clinic; the job mostly means answering phones, scheduling appointments, and keeping the paperwork straight enough that the doctors don’t have to chase me down. I didn’t finish my associate’s degree in business because I needed to start working to help my parents back in Mexico send money home, and I’ve been juggling that ever since.  

I’m married to Carlos, a construction foreman who’s often on a different site by the end of the day, so when we’re both home we try to squeeze in dinner together—usually tacos or a quick stir‑fry. We have two kids: Maya, 12, who’s into video games, and little Luis, 7, who still asks for bedtime stories about dragons. On weekends I drive the kids to Little League, pick up groceries at the local market, and sometimes help my neighbor mow her lawn for a few extra bucks. My parents live in a small apartment in Richmond; I send them a monthly check and try to visit them at least once a year, though the trips are pricey.  

My day starts early: a coffee, a quick scan of the news on my phone while the kids eat cereal, then the commute to the clinic. After work I’m usually on my phone again, scrolling through social feeds or looking up recipes for the next night’s dinner. I don’t have a lot of free time, but I do enjoy the occasional walk on the town’s historic downtown square when the weather’s nice.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole process was a circus—campaign ads promised “change” but I never saw any real improvement in my own life. Between juggling two jobs, two kids, and trying to keep my parents afloat, voting just felt like another thing I couldn’t fit into an already packed schedule. I also think the candidates both seemed to talk past each other, and I didn’t trust either side to actually address the cost of groceries or the rising property taxes that keep squeezing my family’s budget.  

Now, looking toward the 2026 midterms, I’m still leaning toward sitting this one out. Unless there’s a local issue that hits home—like a school funding cut that would affect Maya’s classes or a sudden spike in utility bills—I don’t see a compelling reason to make the trip to the booth. My top everyday worries are the price of food at the grocery store and the property tax bill that keeps creeping up on our house.  

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats keep talking about “affordable healthcare” but the clinics I work at are still understaffed and overworked. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to want to cut taxes for the wealthy while ignoring the fact that my paycheck barely stretches after rent and utilities. So, for now, I’m “I'm sitting this one out.”
