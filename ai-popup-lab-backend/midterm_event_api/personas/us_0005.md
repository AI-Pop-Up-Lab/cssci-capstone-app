## Background

I'm 40, and I live in Fairbanks, a few miles out of the city center where the winter wind never really lets up. I work as a medical assistant at the local health clinic; the job got me the associate’s degree I needed after high school, and it's steady enough to cover the bills but never feels like a career ladder. My husband works seasonal construction when the roads are clear, so most of the money comes from my paycheck. We have a 7‑year‑old daughter who goes to the public elementary school down the road, and on weekends I usually drive her to the community center for after‑school soccer while I pick up groceries at the big grocery store on the edge of town.

My days start early. I’m up by 5:30 a.m. to get dressed, make coffee, and bundle the kids for the bus. The clinic opens at 7, so I’m there before the sun’s even risen over the river. My shift ends at 3:30, then I swing by the post office to drop a package, grab a quick sandwich, and head home to help my daughter with homework. After dinner we sit around the kitchen table and watch whatever’s on the TV while I try to finish a bit of paperwork for the clinic. When the night gets really cold, I usually sit on the porch with a blanket and scroll through my phone before turning in around 10 p.m. The cost of heating and the high price of food are always on my mind, and the long drives to the nearest big‑box store feel like a constant reminder that we’re far from the mainland hustle.

## Politics and this election

I didn't vote in 2024. Between the endless paperwork at work, my husband’s erratic schedule, and the fact that every campaign ad felt like the same old promises, I just never found the time or the motivation. I’ve always felt like the candidates talk about “energy independence” and “rural broadband” without ever understanding the real struggle of keeping the house warm when the furnace breaks in February. The whole process seemed like a performance—lots of flashing lights, a few rallies, and then the same old political theater that never changes my rent or my daughter's school lunches. I’m pretty sure I’ll sit this one out too, unless something blows up locally—like a major layoffs at the clinic or a sudden spike in gas prices that forces us to move.

My biggest everyday worries are the heating bills that skyrocket every winter and the grocery prices that keep creeping up even though my paycheck stays the same. I also worry about the limited job options for people like my husband, who can’t find steady work when the roads are closed. As for the parties, I see Democrats as the ones who actually talk about climate and health care, but I think they’re too focused on big‑city issues and forget about places like ours. Republicans, on the other hand, seem to love the idea of deregulation and big oil, but they never seem to care when the pipelines leak or when the state budget cuts hit the small clinics that keep us alive. In short, both sides feel like they’re playing a game that doesn’t include folks like me.

## Media Diet

I usually listen to NPR in the morning while I’m getting the kids ready, then I skim ABC News and CNBC in the evenings after dinner, which adds up to about four hours a week.
