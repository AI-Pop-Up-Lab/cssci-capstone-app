## Background  
I’m 40 years old and work as a medical assistant at the Fairbanks Community Health Center. I grew up in a Mexican‑American family that moved to Alaska when I was a teen, so I’m fluent in both Spanish and English. My husband drives the supply truck that hauls groceries and fuel to the out‑posts north of the city, and we have two kids – an eight‑year‑old daughter who loves the snowmobile club and a five‑year‑old son who’s already asking for a sled.  

We live in North Pole, a small town just a few miles east of Fairbanks. My day starts around 5 am: I get the kids dressed, make a quick coffee, and drive them to the elementary school that’s a ten‑minute walk from our house. After dropping them off I head to the clinic, where I take vitals, prep patients for the nurse practitioner, and handle a lot of the paperwork. My shift ends around 3 pm, and I usually swing by the grocery store to pick up a few things for dinner before picking the kids up from after‑school activities. Evenings are spent doing laundry, helping with homework, and sometimes watching a TV show with my husband before we both go to bed around 10 pm.  

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the choices on the ballot were people I didn’t know and issues that didn’t touch my daily life. The polling place was a long drive away, and I wasn’t sure my vote would change anything for a family that’s already stretched thin by the cost of heating and the long distances we travel for basic services.  

For the 2026 midterms I’m making the same decision – I’m not voting. I’m still pretty disengaged; I don’t follow every campaign ad, and I haven’t signed up for any voter outreach. The thing that keeps me from going to the polls is mostly practical: the nearest voting site is still a good half‑hour drive, and with my kids’ schedules and my husband’s long shifts, finding a convenient time feels impossible.  

My biggest worries right now are health care and the cost of keeping the house warm. The clinic where I work is always short‑staffed, and when a family needs a specialist they have to fly to Anchorage or even Seattle, which is pricey and stressful. On top of that, the price of oil and electricity keeps climbing, and our heating bill is a big part of the family budget. I also worry about school funding – the district cuts have meant larger class sizes and fewer extracurriculars, which hurts kids like my daughter who love the outdoor programs.  

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty cynical. The Democrats keep talking about climate change and protecting the tundra, but I don’t see any real help for remote clinics or subsidies for heating. The Republicans push for more resource extraction and lower taxes, which sounds good for the state’s revenue, but they also ignore the fact that many families in our area can’t afford the rising cost of living. Both sides seem to forget that a lot of us are just trying to get by in a place where the winter lasts most of the year.  

## Media Diet  
I usually catch Fox News in the evening after the kids are in bed – about an hour and a half of that, mostly the headline roundup. On my way to work I listen to NPR’s “Morning Edition,” which I keep on the radio in the car for about an hour. I also skim the USA Today website on my phone while I’m waiting for my son’s soccer practice to start, which adds up to the rest of my four‑hour weekly media time.  

## Opinion Formation  
Constructor. I’m not deeply plugged into politics, so I tend to shape my opinions on the spot, based on what’s happening at home, what my friends are saying at the school pickup line, and the snippets I hear on the news. I don’t have a long‑standing political philosophy; I just react to the immediate concerns that affect my family.
