## Background  
I'm 40, and I live in a modest house on the edge of York, Pennsylvania, right in the middle of the 10th district. I work full‑time as a forklift operator at a regional distribution center; the job's steady hours and overtime keep the lights on, but it’s also physically demanding and leaves me exhausted by the end of the day. My wife runs a small nail salon from our front room, and we have two kids—Emily, 10, and Marcus, 7—who both go to the local public elementary school.  

My mornings start around 5:30 a.m. with a quick coffee, a glance at the weather on my phone, and a half‑hour of NPR news while I bundle the kids and head out the door. I catch the 6:45 a.m. bus to the warehouse, where I spend eight hours moving pallets, scanning inventory, and dealing with the occasional angry driver. Lunch is usually a sandwich at the breakroom, and I scroll through a mix of headlines on my phone—sometimes a quick CBS story, sometimes a headline from Breitbart that catches my eye. After my shift I drive home, help with dinner, and try to squeeze in some homework help before we all crash. Weekends are a blur of soccer practices, a grocery run that feels like a mission, and the occasional family gathering at my parents’ place in the nearby suburbs. I don’t have a lot of spare cash, so I’m always watching the price tags and looking for sales.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between working double shifts, taking care of the kids, and feeling that none of the candidates really understood the everyday grind of people like me, the ballot just seemed like another thing I didn’t have the energy for. The whole process felt distant, and the ads were either too polished or too angry—nothing resonated with my life. I told myself I’d be more interested if something hit close to home, like a local factory closure or a school funding crisis, but nothing that year pushed me to the polls.  

For the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out again. I’m still juggling work, family, and bills, and unless a candidate comes out with a concrete plan that actually helps our community—like fixing the potholes on Route 30 or keeping the local grocery store from shutting down—I’m not going to make the extra effort. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of groceries and the property taxes that keep creeping up on my mortgage. The gas price spikes also bite when we have to drive the kids to sports or get supplies.  

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty fed up with both sides. The Republicans keep talking about tax cuts and “small government,” but those cuts never seem to trickle down to us in the suburbs; they feel like a promise that never materializes. The Democrats, on the other hand, push big‑picture social policies that sound good on TV but ignore the fact that my wife can’t afford the higher insurance premiums that come with new regulations. Both seem more interested in scoring points than solving the problems that keep me up at night, so I’m just waiting to see who, if anyone, actually shows up for the people who work the night shift and try to put food on the table.

## Media Diet
