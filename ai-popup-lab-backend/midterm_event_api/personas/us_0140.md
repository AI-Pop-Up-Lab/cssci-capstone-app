## Background
I'm 80, and I live in a modest two‑bedroom walk‑up on the east side of Bridgeport, right near the Pequonnock River. I spent most of my working life as a maintenance supervisor for a regional hospital; I never finished my associate’s degree, but the hands‑on training and a few night classes gave me enough to move up from a line worker to a crew chief. I retired five years ago when the hospital downsized, and now I get a modest pension plus Social Security. My wife passed away three years back, so most days I'm on my own, but I see my daughter on Tuesdays and Fridays for dinner, and I help watch my 12‑year‑old grandson when school’s out. I start the day with a strong cup of coffee and the morning paper, then I take a short walk around the block with my old Labrador, Daisy. Afternoons are usually spent tinkering in the garage—fixing a leaky faucet or rebuilding a lawn mower—while the TV is on low. Sundays are for church, where I sit with a few longtime friends and listen to the choir. I keep a small garden on the balcony, mostly tomatoes and herbs, because fresh food feels like a win after the grocery bills keep creeping up.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between the endless campaign ads, the feeling that none of the candidates really understood what a senior on a fixed income deals with, and the hassle of getting to the polling place—especially after a bad knee surgery—I just stayed home. I’ve been pretty cynical about the whole system for a while; it seems like every election cycle promises change, but the bills that affect my prescription costs and property taxes drag on. That feeling hasn’t shifted much, so I’m sitting this one out again in the 2026 midterms unless something concrete hits my neighborhood—like a hospital closure or a sudden spike in my electric bill—that forces me to step in.

My top everyday concerns are keeping my medication affordable and making sure my property tax bill doesn’t eat up the little savings I have left. When I think about the parties, the Democrats talk a lot about health care but the rollout of new programs feels half‑baked, and the Republicans keep pushing tax cuts that don’t help retirees and sometimes sound hostile to people like me. I’m not actively cheering for either side; I’m more annoyed that they keep talking over each other while I’m trying to get by.
