## Background
I'm 25 and work as a junior software developer for a fintech startup that has an office in the Hudson Valley. I live in a small two‑bedroom apartment on the east side of Newburgh, sharing the place with a roommate who studies nursing at SUNY New Paltz. My mornings start at around 6:30 am; I brew a cheap coffee, scroll the news on my phone while the kettle whistles, then bike the three miles to the train station. The commute takes about 45 minutes, and I usually listen to a NPR podcast about tech trends or a Newsweek newsletter while the train rattles north.

After work, which usually ends around 6 pm, I either head back home to cook something quick—usually pasta or a frozen pizza—or meet my roommate for a quick grocery run at the local market. Weekends are a mix of cleaning the apartment, catching a game at the bar with a few friends from the office, and sometimes helping my parents with yard work back in my hometown of Catskill, which is about an hour’s drive away. I don’t own a car, so most errands are done by bike or the occasional rideshare. I’m not married, don’t have kids, and my student loans are still a monthly reminder on my budget.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party’s stance on climate action and student debt relief aligned with my own worries. I was also encouraged by the local campaign volunteers who knocked on my door and reminded me that the Senate race could affect funding for broadband in our district—something I rely on for work. I didn’t feel the Republican platform addressed the cost of living in the Hudson Valley; their emphasis on tax cuts for big corporations seemed disconnected from my reality of paying rent and juggling loan payments.

Since then, I’ve stayed pretty engaged. I’ve attended a town‑hall meeting about a proposed commuter rail upgrade, and I’m signed up for a few email updates from a progressive advocacy group. I’m definitely going to vote in the 2026 midterms; I think my ballot can still influence who gets the seat in the House and what kind of funding our district will get for infrastructure. My top everyday concerns are keeping up with rent and the rising price of groceries—especially fresh produce, which feels like a luxury sometimes. I also keep an eye on the local job market; the tech scene here is small, and layoffs at my company would hit me hard.

When I think about the parties, I’m generally more comfortable with the Democrats because they at least talk about issues that touch my life—student loans, health care, climate. The Republicans feel like they’re more focused on rural concerns and big‑business tax breaks, which doesn’t help someone like me trying to build a career in a small city. That said, I’m not a die‑hard partisan; I’ll look at the individual candidates and what they say about things that matter day‑to‑day.
