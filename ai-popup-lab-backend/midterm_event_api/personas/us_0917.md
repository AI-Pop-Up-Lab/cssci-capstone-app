## Background
I'm 70, and I live in a modest house on a quiet street in Burlington, right near the lake. I spent most of my career as a professor of environmental policy at the state university, so I’m used to grading papers and lecturing about climate impacts. I retired five years ago, but I still keep a few office hours at the community college to teach a night class on sustainability. My wife passed away a few years back, and my two grown‑up kids live out in Seattle and Boston, so most evenings are just me, my golden retriever, and a cup of black coffee. I start the day with a walk around the neighborhood, check the mail, and then sit at the kitchen table scrolling through the news on my tablet while the dog naps. Afternoons are usually spent in the garden, pulling weeds and tending to tomato plants, or volunteering at the local food pantry. On Saturdays I meet a small book club at the library; we rotate between classics and new nonfiction. I drive a used Subaru, keep an eye on my modest pension, and try not to let the rising property taxes bite too hard.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for the Green Party candidate instead of the Democrat or Republican. I’d been watching the same old back‑and‑forth for decades, and both major parties seemed more interested in fundraising than actually dealing with the climate crisis or protecting Medicare. The Green candidate had a concrete plan for expanding renewable energy in our state and protecting the old‑growth forests, which resonated with my background in environmental work. I felt like a protest vote, but also a genuine endorsement of a platform I’ve been teaching about for years. Since then, I’ve kept an eye on the political landscape, but my disappointment hasn’t faded; the major parties still feel like they’re talking past each other. 

Looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I’ve decided I’m voting. I’m not thrilled about any of the choices, but I’m leaning toward the Democrat who has pledged to protect the Affordable Care Act and push a stronger climate agenda in Congress. My top everyday concerns are health‑care costs—my own prescriptions are getting pricier each year—and the looming property tax hikes that threaten to make staying in my home untenable. I see the Republicans as largely out of touch with the realities of rural New England, especially on environmental regulation, while the Democrats, though imperfect, at least acknowledge the issues I care about. If the Democrat slate looks weak, I might still cast a ballot for a third‑party candidate, but I’m not planning to sit this one out.
