## Background
I'm 80, and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Hilo, right where the rain rolls in off Mauna Kea. I spent most of my working life as a front‑desk clerk at a mid‑size hotel near the harbor; the job never required a degree, but I took a few night classes in business admin, so I count that as “some college.” I retired ten years ago, but I still help out the owner with occasional bookkeeping during the tourist season. My wife passed away five years back, and my two grown‑up children live on the mainland—one in Seattle, the other in Phoenix. They call when they can, and my grandchildren visit every summer, bringing a lot of noise and sushi rolls.

My days start early. I brew a strong cup of Kona coffee, read the print edition of the *Honolulu Star‑Advertiser* while the sun is still low, then head out to tend the small garden behind the house. I grow taro, a few peppers, and a mango tree that finally bears fruit. By mid‑morning I sit on the porch and scan the news on my tablet, usually catching up on the three outlets I keep bookmarked. After lunch—usually a plate of laulau leftovers—I take a short walk down to the community center where I volunteer once a week to help seniors with their phones. Evenings are for TV, a bit of bingo on the local channel, and then a quiet night reading whatever my grandson left me at the last visit.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my vote wouldn't change anything, and the whole campaign felt like a circus. I was also dealing with a bout of pneumonia that winter, and the doctor's office kept calling me about follow‑ups. Between that and the fact that the ballot was mailed to a different address after my wife moved, I just let it slide. The whole process seemed more about fundraising than about solving the real problems people my age face—like rising prescription costs and the fact that the power company keeps hiking rates even though we get more rain than sunshine.

Now, looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I'm sitting this one out too. Unless something dramatic hits the island—like a major utility outage that leaves the whole community in the dark for weeks—I'm not going to make the effort to fill out a ballot. My top everyday worries are the price of electricity and the cost of my Medicare premiums; both keep climbing and my fixed pension just can’t stretch as far as it used to. I also worry about the limited senior services in Hilo; the senior center is understaffed and the bus routes are getting cut.

When I think about the parties, I see both of them promising the same thing: more money for the government or more tax cuts for the rich. The Democrats, in my view, talk a lot about climate and tourism but seem to forget that a lot of us rely on fixed incomes and need stable utility rates. The Republicans keep shouting about cutting taxes, but those cuts never trickle down to retirees; they mostly benefit big landowners and corporations. So I’m pretty annoyed with the whole system, but I also understand that if I stay home, I’m giving up any chance to push them to actually listen.

## Media Diet
I split my seven weekly news hours between NPR’s morning podcasts, ABC News videos during lunch, and catching up on the latest CBS News livestream in the evenings. I usually do it while I’m cooking or after I’ve finished my garden work.
