## Background
I'm 55, and I live in Beckley, West Virginia, right near the foothills of the Appalachians. I work as the director of community health programs for the regional hospital, a job I got after finishing my master's in public health and a doctorate in health administration. My days start early—around 5:30 a.m. I make a pot of coffee, scroll through NPR headlines on my phone while the kids are still asleep, then drive the 20 minutes to the hospital. I’m married to a retired coal miner who now does odd‑job work for extra cash, and we have two grown‑up children who live out of state but come home for holidays. My older mom, who’s in her late seventies, still lives with us; I help her with meds and doctor appointments. After work I usually hit the gym for an hour, then pick up groceries at the local market. On Sundays I go to a small Black Baptist congregation downtown, where I’m part of the choir. Most evenings are spent catching up on paperwork, watching a bit of TV, or video‑chatting with my sister in Ohio.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus—candidates kept talking about “change” but it never translated to anything concrete for people like me in southern West Virginia. The ballot felt like a choice between two sides that both ignored the real struggles: rising drug prices, limited broadband, and the fact that my mom’s Medicare paperwork gets lost in a maze of bureaucracy. On top of that, my schedule was a mess—long hours at the hospital, caring for my mom, and trying to keep the house running. I figured my single vote wouldn’t move the needle, so I stayed home.

I’m sitting this one out too. Unless something dramatic happens locally—like a major hospital closure or a big scandal that hits my community directly—I don’t see a reason to go to the polls. My top everyday worries are the cost of prescription drugs and the lack of decent jobs for younger folks who want to stay in the area. I’m also worried about property taxes creeping up while the county’s services feel thin. As for the parties, I’m pretty fed up with the Democrats; they promise health care fixes but never fund the programs that actually reach rural clinics. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep pushing tax cuts that never seem to help the working‑class families here and they’re hostile to the kind of social programs my mom relies on. Both sides feel out of touch, so I’m just waiting for a candidate who actually talks about fixing the hospital system and expanding broadband.
