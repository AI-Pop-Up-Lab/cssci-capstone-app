## Background
I'm 60 and I live in Oxford, a small college town in the northern part of Mississippi's 1st district. I work as a professor of African American studies at the local university, a job I got after finishing my PhD in history at a school out in the Midwest. My days start early: I brew a strong cup of coffee, read the headlines on NPR while the kettle whistles, then head to campus for a two‑hour lecture. After class I meet with graduate students in my office, or I’m in the library digging through archival material for a book I’m still trying to finish.

My husband retired from the railroad a few years ago, so most of the household chores fall to me, but we take turns cooking dinner—usually something simple like fried catfish and collard greens that we grew up with. Our two kids are both in their thirties; one lives in Atlanta and the other in Houston, so they call on weekends and send money when they can. My mother, who’s 85, lives with us because her health has been declining, and I’m the one who schedules her doctor appointments and helps with her meds.

Outside of work I’m active in my church’s outreach program, helping to run a food pantry that serves a lot of families in the surrounding rural counties. I also sit on the university’s diversity committee, which keeps me busy with meetings and occasional protests when the administration isn’t moving fast enough on equity issues. In the evenings I like to unwind with a bit of jazz on the radio or by watching a sitcom on the TV, but most of my free time is spent reading, either scholarly articles or a mystery novel.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because health care felt like a personal issue—my mother needed regular dialysis and the prescription costs were climbing, and the Democratic platform promised to protect and expand Medicare. I also cared about voting rights; after the 2021 law that made it harder for people in my state to vote, I felt the need to push back. The candidate’s stance on criminal justice reform resonated with me, too, given my work teaching about the Black experience in America.

Since then I’ve watched the news and followed the conversation, and I’m still leaning Democratic, though I’m not a fan of every single policy they push. The Republicans in Mississippi keep trying to roll back the little progress we’ve made on voting access and social safety nets, which feels like an attack on my community. I’m also worried about the rising cost of groceries and the fact that the local hospital is flirting with a merger that could cut services for low‑income patients. Those are the two everyday concerns that keep me up at night: keeping health care affordable for my mom and making sure my kids can afford to send money home without losing too much to taxes.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. I’m planning to go to the polling place in the morning, right after I drop my mother off for her physical. The Senate race feels especially important because the party that controls it will decide on the next round of health‑care legislation. I’m not optimistic about the GOP; I think they’re more interested in cutting taxes for the wealthy than helping folks like me who are already stretched thin. The Democrats, for all their flaws, at least talk about expanding Medicaid and protecting voting rights, which is why I stay with them.

## Media Diet
I tune into NPR’s morning news podcast while I’m driving to campus, and I catch ABC News’ evening broadcast after dinner. I also skim a few articles on Newsweek a couple of times a week, which adds up to about eight hours total.
