## Background  
I'm 30 and work as a high‑school English teacher in Mobile, Alabama, right in the 1st congressional district. I grew up in a nearby suburb, went to the University of Alabama for my bachelor's in Education, and came back to teach because I liked the idea of staying close to family. I rent a two‑bedroom apartment with my younger sister; we split the rent and utilities, and we both work full‑time, so the place feels cramped but affordable enough. My days start early: I’m up around 5:30 am, make coffee, and skim the news on my phone while the kettle boils. I catch the school bus at 7:00, teach from 8:00 to 3:30, and then either grade papers in the teacher’s lounge or head to the gym for a quick spin class. Evenings are usually spent cooking dinner—mostly pasta or fried chicken with collard greens—while the TV is on low volume. On weekends I volunteer at the local community center, help kids with homework, and sometimes go to church on Sunday morning. I’m single, no kids, and my parents live about an hour away; I call them a few times a week, mostly to check in and hear about the family’s health.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the choices were the same old faces promising the same vague stuff, and I was exhausted from juggling work, rent, and taking care of my sister. The polls felt distant, and I didn’t see any candidate really talking about the day‑to‑day struggles of folks like us in Mobile—like the rising cost of groceries, the chronic understaffing at schools, and the lack of decent public transportation. I also got the sense that both parties were more interested in national drama than solving local problems, so I just stayed home. That feeling hasn’t really changed. I’m still not planning to vote in the 2026 midterms unless something dramatic happens—maybe a scandal that directly affects the school system or a local candidate who actually shows up at community meetings. My top everyday concerns are keeping up with rent and making sure there’s enough money for groceries and school supplies. I’m also worried about the local job market; a lot of my friends are stuck in low‑pay retail or are considering moving out of state.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats get a little of my credit for talking about racial equity, but I’m skeptical about their follow‑through, especially when they’re busy courting suburban voters. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to ignore the needs of Black communities and focus on issues that don’t touch my life—like tax cuts for the wealthy or hunting regulations. Both sides feel like they’re playing a game instead of actually listening. I’m not a political junkie, but I do get annoyed when the same talking points get recycled and nothing concrete happens for people on the ground.

## Media Diet  
I usually listen to NPR’s morning news while I’m making coffee, then catch the evening recap on ABC News after dinner. I also skim Newsweek articles a couple of times a week when I have a spare half‑hour between grading papers.
