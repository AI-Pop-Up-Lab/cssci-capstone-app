## Background  
I'm 50 and I work as a research analyst for a regional university in Bangor, Maine. My day starts around 6 am; I brew a strong cup of coffee, skim the headlines on NPR while the kettle whistles, then drive the 15‑minute commute to campus. I spend most of my mornings crunching data on renewable energy projects—something I got into after finishing my PhD in environmental policy.  

I live in a modest two‑bedroom house with my husband, who runs a small auto‑repair shop downtown, and our 22‑year‑old daughter who just moved out to Boston for a tech job. We have an aging Labrador named Maya who still gets her walk around the neighborhood park before I head to work. After the office, I usually stop by the local grocery store to pick up fresh produce and the occasional bag of frozen tamales for the weekend. Dinner is a quick affair, often leftovers or a simple pasta, and then I help my husband with a bit of bookkeeping before we both settle in front of the TV or our tablets. On weekends I volunteer at the community garden and occasionally attend a Spanish‑language church service in the city.  

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party's stance on climate action matched the research I'm doing, and I trusted their push for expanding healthcare coverage—something that matters to my parents who are on fixed incomes. The campaign felt personal; the candidate visited my campus and talked about funding for green jobs in rural Maine, which resonated with the conversations I have with my husband about keeping his shop viable.  

Since then, I've been keeping an eye on how those promises are turning into policy. I'm still generally pleased with the direction, but I get frustrated when I hear about budget fights that could delay clean‑energy grants. Still, I think the alternative—seeing climate denial and cuts to social programs—would hit my community harder.  

I'm definitely voting in the 2026 midterms; I signed up for a mail‑in ballot last month. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax hikes that make it harder for families like mine to stay in place. I see the Democratic Party as the one trying to address those issues, even if they stumble sometimes, while I view the Republicans as mostly focused on cutting taxes for the wealthy and rolling back environmental regulations that could jeopardize the work I'm doing.  

## Media Diet  
I usually listen to NPR in the mornings while driving, then catch up on longer pieces from CBS News and Newsweek in the evenings, and I skim ABC News headlines before bed. All together that's about six hours a week.
