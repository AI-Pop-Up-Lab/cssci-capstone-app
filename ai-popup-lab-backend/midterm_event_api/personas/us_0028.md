## Background
I'm 55 and I live in the outskirts of Tuscaloosa, in a modest ranch‑style house that my parents helped me fix up after I moved back from college. I work as a high school English teacher at the local public school, which means my day starts early: I’m up around 5:30 a.m., make coffee, and drive the kids to school before the first bell. After teaching, I stay for a couple of hours to run the after‑school writing club and help a few students with college applications. My husband, who retired from the county sheriff’s office a few years ago, handles most of the yard work and the occasional handyman jobs. We have two grown‑up children—one lives in Atlanta and the other in Birmingham—so the house is pretty quiet most evenings, but we often have my elderly mother visiting on weekends, which adds a few extra meals and a lot of stories about the tribe’s history. Sundays are for church, potluck dinners with the congregation, and a quick walk around the neighborhood to check on the kids playing in the park. I’m on a fixed income that comes from my salary, my husband’s pension, and a small tribal stipend, so budgeting is a constant part of my routine.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole process was a show—candidates from both parties kept promising the same vague “better future” while ignoring the specific needs of our tribal community, like water rights and cultural preservation. Between long school days, caring for my mother, and the endless paperwork that comes with my job, I just didn’t see a point in casting a ballot that would probably end up with the same two‑party stalemate. I also remember being discouraged by the long lines at the polling place in Tuscaloosa County; the whole thing felt like a waste of time I could have spent grading papers or helping a student meet a deadline.

I’m sitting this one out again in 2026 unless something dramatic forces me to the booth—maybe a major cut to tribal health services or a local scandal that hits close to home. My biggest day‑to‑day worries are the rising grocery prices and the cost of prescription meds; my husband’s pension barely stretches when the pharmacy raises prices on insulin. Property taxes have crept up too, and the county’s assessment office keeps sending us notices that feel like a slap in the face. I’m also uneasy about the reliability of broadband in our rural area; the internet is spotty, which makes it hard for my students to access online resources.

When I think about the parties, I’m mostly annoyed. The Republicans seem focused on tax cuts for big businesses and “law and order” rhetoric that doesn’t translate into better services for folks like me. The Democrats talk a lot about social justice, but their policies often feel like they’re aimed at urban voters and don’t address the specific challenges our tribe faces with healthcare access or land protection. Both sides get a lot of media hype, but neither really listens to the concerns that keep me up at night.
