## Background  
I'm 40 and I live in Scottsbluff, right on the edge of Nebraska's 1st district. I work as an automotive service technician at a family‑run garage downtown; I got my associate’s degree in automotive technology from Central Community College, so I’m comfortable with my hands but not exactly a “college‑educated” guy. My wife, Maria, works part‑time at the local grocery store, and we have two kids—12‑year‑old Maya and 8‑year‑old Jonah. We rent a two‑bedroom house a few blocks from the high school, which means a lot of early mornings getting the kids to school, me doing a 7 a.m. shift, and Maria dropping off groceries before her shift starts. After work I usually swing by the kids’ basketball practice, then we all eat dinner together while the TV is on low. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs, a quick trip to the nearby reservoir for fishing, and helping Maya with her science project. I’m a member of the local Oglala Lakota community center, which organizes cultural events and sometimes helps with tribal health outreach. I don’t travel far; most of my world is the garage, the school, the grocery store, and the occasional visit to the reservation office a half‑hour away.

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic ticket in 2024 because I felt the party was finally taking tribal water rights and healthcare funding seriously. When the Biden administration pushed for more funding to tribal clinics, I thought that could mean a new clinic closer to my kids’ school, and that mattered more than any abstract debate about the economy. I also liked the way the Democratic candidate talked about expanding broadband in rural areas; we still get spotty internet, and that makes my kids’ homework a nightmare.  

Since then I’ve stayed pretty tuned into how the issues are playing out. The Republicans in the state have been pushing for stricter land‑use regulations that could hurt our reservation’s farming plots, and that’s a red flag for me. The Democrats, on the other hand, keep promising more tribal representation but sometimes miss the mark on actual funding. Still, I think the balance of power matters: I’m voting in the 2026 midterms because I don’t want to hand the Senate over to a party that might roll back the modest gains we got.  

My everyday worries are simple: the price of diesel for my truck is climbing, and the cost of prescription meds for my mother’s arthritis keeps creeping up. I’m also watching property taxes rise on our rental unit, which makes it harder for Maria to keep a roof over our heads. When I think about the parties, I’m pretty sure the Democrats are at least trying to listen to tribal concerns, even if they’re not perfect. The Republicans feel like they’re more about cutting taxes for big agribusinesses and less about the small‑scale farmers and tribal members who actually live here. I’m not a political scholar; I just know which side seems to care a little more about the issues that affect my family. So, I’m heading to the polls in November.
