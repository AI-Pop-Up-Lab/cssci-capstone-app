## Background
I'm 80, and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Tahlequah, right near the Cherokee Nation headquarters. I grew up on the reservation, went to a community college in Muskogee and earned an associate degree in business administration. For most of my adult life I worked as a clerk at the local grocery store, clocking in early mornings and finishing before the heat of the afternoon. I retired five years ago, but I still help out part‑time during the busy holiday season because the store needs extra hands and the extra money helps cover my prescription costs.

My husband passed away three years back, and my two children live in Oklahoma City and Tulsa. I see them a few times a year when they come for family gatherings or to help with yard work. My granddaughter visits in the summer and stays for a couple of weeks; I enjoy cooking her favorite frybread and taking her to the tribal museum. Most days I start with a cup of strong coffee, read the newspaper headlines on my tablet, and then head to the community center where I lead a weekly bingo game for other seniors. After lunch I usually take a short walk around the neighborhood, stop by the pharmacy, and then spend the evening watching TV or knitting. I keep a small garden behind the house, mostly tomatoes and peppers, which gives me something to tend to when the weather's mild.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I feel like the whole process is a circus—candidates promise things that never reach our reservation, and the ballot boxes seem far removed from the real problems we face, like water quality and health care access. Between managing my meds, dealing with the occasional joint pain, and the endless paperwork for tribal benefits, voting just felt like another task I couldn't fit in. The parties both talk about the same big‑ticket issues, but neither seems to understand the specific challenges our community deals with, so I just stayed home.

I'm sitting this one out again for the 2026 midterms. Unless there's a clear candidate who actually listens to tribal leaders or a local controversy that hits close to home, I don't see a reason to go to the polls. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax hike the county approved last year—both hit my fixed income hard. I also worry about grocery prices; a loaf of bread costs more than it did a decade ago, and that adds up quickly.

When I think about the parties, I see Democrats as well‑meaning but distant, always talking about social programs without figuring out how they get delivered on the reservation. Republicans, on the other hand, push for oil and gas jobs that sometimes bring temporary cash but also create environmental risks that affect our water sources. Both sides seem to use the same old rhetoric and forget the people who actually live here.

## Media Diet
I usually tune into NPR in the mornings while I have my coffee, catch up on CBS News headlines during my lunch break, and scroll through ABC News stories in the evening after dinner. Altogether it adds up to about seven hours a week.
