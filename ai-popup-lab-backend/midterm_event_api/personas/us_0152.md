## Background
I'm 30, and I live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment in the Riverfront area of Wilmington, Delaware, right in the middle of the DE‑1 district. I work as a software engineer for a fintech startup that moved into the old warehouse district a couple of years ago. I graduated with a master’s in computer science, so most days I’m glued to a dual‑monitor setup, debugging code or pushing updates for a mobile banking app. My days start early: I brew a strong coffee, skim the NPR morning briefing while scrolling through my phone, then hop on the train to the office. The commute is about 20 minutes, and I use the time to listen to a tech podcast or catch up on the news.

After work I usually hit the gym for an hour or meet a roommate for a quick dinner at a nearby food truck—tacos are a weekend staple. On Saturdays I help my parents with yard work in Dover; they’re retired and still own the house they bought in the 80s. I’m single, but I have a close circle of friends from grad school who I see for drinks or board games a few times a month. When I’m not coding or hanging out, I’m trying to pay down the last chunk of student loans and keep an eye on the rent that keeps creeping up each year.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was finally taking climate change seriously and was pushing for more affordable health care, which mattered to me after my sister’s recent hospitalization. I also liked the emphasis on expanding broadband in rural parts of Delaware, something I see as essential for the tech industry’s future. The campaign’s focus on protecting voting rights resonated, too, since I’ve always been wary of any moves that could make it harder for people like my grandparents to cast a ballot.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of rent and the fact that my student loan balance is still ticking down slowly. I’m also watching local job growth; the tech scene here is promising, but I worry about layoffs if the economy stalls. I think the Democratic Party is the only one that’s consistently pushing policies that help middle‑class folks stay afloat—like tuition relief and stronger consumer protections. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more interested in tax cuts for the ultra‑wealthy and rolling back environmental regulations, which feels like a gamble that could hurt both the planet and people trying to make ends meet. I’m not a political scholar, but the way I see it, the parties are pretty polarized, and I’m leaning toward the side that actually talks about fixing the problems that affect my day‑to‑day life.
