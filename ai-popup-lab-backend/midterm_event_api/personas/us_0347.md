## Background
I'm 50, and I live in the Lower Ninth Ward of New Orleans, right in the middle of Louisiana’s 5th congressional district. I work full‑time as a grocery store clerk—stocking shelves, ringing up customers, and handling the occasional break‑room dispute. I never finished high school; I left after ninth grade to help my mother pay the rent when I was 16, and I've been working ever since. I share a two‑bedroom apartment with my 22‑year‑old son, who’s in community college, and my 55‑year‑old sister, who’s on disability. We get by on a modest income, so every dollar counts.

My day starts around 5 a.m. with a quick coffee from a corner stand, then a two‑hour bus ride to the store. I work the 9 a.m.–5 p.m. shift, which gives me a break to grab lunch at the deli across the street. After work I swing by the local library to help my son with his math homework, then we both head back home to cook dinner—usually something cheap and filling like gumbo made from a bag of frozen shrimp. On Fridays we try to catch a free concert at the park, and on Sundays I go to my church’s 10 a.m. service; the pastor’s sermons about community and resilience mean a lot after the hurricanes we keep hearing about.

## Politics and this election
I voted Democratic in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one really caring about the things that hit my life head‑on: Medicaid coverage for my sister, the flood‑control projects that keep our street from turning into a swamp after a storm, and the minimum‑wage push that would make it easier to cover rent and groceries. The campaign ads that talked about fixing the levee system and protecting Social Security resonated with me, and I was tired of hearing the same old “small government” talk that never seemed to address my needs.

Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how the new administration’s policies are playing out. The flood‑mitigation grant we finally got last summer helped patch a few broken culverts, but there’s still a lot of work to do. My son got a scholarship that covered half his tuition, which was a relief, but the cost of textbooks still hurts. I’ve also seen a few Republican candidates in local races try to downplay climate‑change funding, and that makes me nervous because the next big storm could hit us hard again.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are keeping the utility bills from blowing up—electricity and water are already pricey—and making sure there are stable, decent‑paying jobs in the area so people like me don’t have to juggle three part‑time gigs. I’m not a political scholar; I just know the Democrats have pushed for more health‑care support and infrastructure fixes, even if they sometimes sound like they’re talking down to folks who didn’t finish school. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep talking about “personal responsibility” and cutting taxes, but those ideas feel out of touch when you’re trying to keep a roof over your head and a kid in school. I get annoyed when they dismiss the need for federal flood relief, and I’m skeptical when they say they’ll “fix the economy” without offering concrete help for working families.
