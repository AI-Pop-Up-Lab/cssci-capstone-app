## Background  
I'm 60, and I live in Wallingford, a suburb of New Haven in Connecticut's 3rd district. I work full‑time as a maintenance supervisor at a mid‑size manufacturing plant that makes automotive parts. I didn’t finish my associate’s degree in mechanical engineering because the plant hired me after I took a few technical courses and I’ve been on the ladder ever since. My wife, who retired from the local library a few years back, and I own a modest two‑bedroom house with a small backyard where we grow tomatoes in the summer. We have two grown‑up kids—one lives in Boston, the other is back here working as a nurse—so the house is mostly just the two of us and an occasional visit from my mother, who lives on the reservation in New York and comes down for holidays.  

My day starts at 5:30 a.m. with a quick coffee and the news on my phone while I drive the 12‑minute commute to the plant. I’m on shift from 7 a.m. to 3 p.m., then I usually stop at the grocery store on the way home, pick up the kids’ laundry, and later in the evening I’m either fixing something around the house or watching a game on TV. On weekends I help my wife with the garden, meet up with a few old high‑school buddies at the local bar, and sometimes volunteer at the community center teaching basic computer skills to seniors. I keep a modest budget; the mortgage and property taxes take up most of the paycheck, and I’m always watching the utility bills because the heating costs in winter can spike.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the choices were the same old arguments I’d heard for years, and none of them addressed the things that matter to me—like the rising property taxes, the cost of my mother’s health care, and the fact that my plant keeps cutting overtime. Between long shifts, caring for my aging mom, and trying to stretch the budget, I just didn’t see the point in spending a Saturday at a polling place. I also get a lot of cynical talk from my friends about how politicians treat Native American issues as a footnote, which makes me feel invisible. That feeling hasn’t changed much, so I’m sitting this 2026 midterm out as well, unless something blows up locally—like a factory closure or a big scandal that directly hits my community.  

My top everyday concerns are the property tax hikes that keep my mortgage from getting any easier and the price of prescription drugs for my mother. I’m also watching the local job market; the plant has been talking about automation, and I worry about what that means for the crew. As for the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats keep promising more social programs but then push tax increases that hit homeowners like me. The Republicans talk about cutting taxes and deregulation, but they also push cuts to things like Medicaid that would make my mom’s situation worse. Neither side seems to have a concrete plan for small‑town manufacturing or for addressing the specific needs of Native communities, so I just roll my eyes and stay home.

## Media Diet  
I usually skim NPR headlines with my coffee in the morning, then catch a half‑hour of CBS News during lunch and an evening roundup on ABC News after dinner. Altogether it adds up to about six hours a week.
