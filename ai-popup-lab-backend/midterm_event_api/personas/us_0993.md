## Background
I’m 45 and I work as a construction laborer for a contractor that builds road sections around the foothills outside Cheyenne. My wife runs a small boutique that sells handmade jewelry, most of it made from locally sourced stones. We have two kids—Mia, 14, who’s obsessed with skateboarding, and Eli, 10, who spends his weekends hunting with his grandparents. Most mornings I’m up before sunrise, grab a coffee from the diner on the highway, and drive the 30‑minute commute to the job site. The work is physically demanding, but it pays the bills and lets us keep the house we bought a decade ago. After work I usually swing by the kids’ school pickup line, help with homework, and then we eat dinner together. Weekends are a mix of family hikes, helping my sister with paperwork for the reservation, and occasional gigs at the local bar where I play a bit of guitar. When I’m not working, I spend a few hours fixing up the old pickup truck in the garage or watching the news on my tablet before bed.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus—campaign ads screaming over the same old promises, and none of the candidates seemed to understand the daily grind of folks like me. Between long shifts, family responsibilities, and the cost of getting to a polling place, it just felt like a waste of time. I also get a lot of cynicism from friends who say their votes never change anything, especially when federal policies keep ignoring tribal sovereignty and the real cost of living on the reservation. That feeling hasn’t gone away, so I’m sitting this one out too. Unless there’s a sudden scandal that hits the local oil lease deals or a health‑care rollout that directly impacts my kids, I’m not planning to cast a ballot in the 2026 midterms.

My biggest worries right now are the rising price of gasoline and the fact that my health‑insurance premiums keep creeping up, making it harder to afford regular check‑ups for my family. The second thing on my mind is property taxes—our land assessment went up last year and the county office says it’s only going to get higher. When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel out of touch, talking about climate policies that would shut down the very jobs that keep my crew employed. The Republicans, for all their talk about “energy independence,” sometimes push legislation that threatens tribal water rights, which is a huge red flag for me. So I’m stuck feeling like both sides are missing the mark, but I’m more annoyed with the Democrats because I see them as the ones pushing the biggest changes that could hurt my community’s way of life.
