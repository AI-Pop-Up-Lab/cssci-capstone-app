## Background
I'm 25 and work as a customer‑service associate at a big‑box retailer in Worcester. I grew up in a nearby suburb and moved back after a year of community college, where I took a few business classes before deciding school wasn't for me right now. I share a two‑bedroom apartment with a roommate who works as a nurse; we split rent and utilities, and we both keep a tight budget because the rent in town has been creeping up. My mornings start around 6 am; I catch a bus to the store, clock in for an eight‑hour shift, and usually grab a coffee at the break room. After work I swing by the library to use the free Wi‑Fi and sometimes finish a class assignment or read up on a hobby—mostly indie video games and a bit of DIY woodworking. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs, visiting my parents in the nearby town of Leicester, and catching a movie at the local theater when I can afford it. I don't own a car, so everything revolves around the bus schedule and the occasional bike ride to the farmer’s market. My family is fairly close; we text a lot, and I help my younger sister with her school projects when I have spare time.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. By the time the primaries rolled around I was pulling double shifts, my roommate was on a grueling night schedule, and I felt like none of the candidates really spoke to the mess I'm trying to sort out—rising rent, grocery prices that keep stinging my paycheck, and a feeling that my vote wouldn't change anything. The whole process seemed like a distant theater, and I just didn't have the energy to navigate the ballot or even figure out where my polling place was. That cynicism hasn't faded much. I keep hearing the same promises from both parties, but when I look at my rent check or the price tag on a bag of milk, I don't see any relief. So for the 2026 midterms I'm sitting this one out, too, unless something local—like a proposed rent‑control ordinance or a big employer pulling out of Worcester—gets me to the polls. My top everyday concerns are keeping a roof over my head and making sure my grocery bill doesn't eat the rest of my wages. I think the Democrats are stuck in a comfort zone, talking about climate and social issues while ignoring the cost‑of‑living squeeze for folks like me. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to think the solution is just to cut taxes for the rich and let the market sort everything out, which feels like a joke when I'm trying to stretch a $15 hourly wage.
