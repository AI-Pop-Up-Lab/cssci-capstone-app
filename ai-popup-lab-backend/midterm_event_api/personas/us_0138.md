## Background  
I'm 75, and I live in a modest house in West Hartford, right on the edge of the 5th district. I spent most of my adult life as a professor of Latin American history at a state university, so I’m retired now but still keep a stack of academic journals on my kitchen table. My wife passed three years ago, and I have two grown children—one lives in Boston, the other in Washington—so most evenings the house is quiet except for the hum of the TV and the occasional call. I help my 78‑year‑old mother with groceries once a week, and I volunteer twice a week at the local senior center, teaching Spanish conversation to other retirees. My day usually starts with a strong cup of coffee and the morning edition of the local paper (which I read online). After a short walk with my old Labrador, I spend a couple of hours on the porch reading whatever article catches my eye—often something about immigration policy or a new study on climate impacts in the Caribbean. Lunch is usually a sandwich and leftovers, then a nap or a crossword puzzle. In the late afternoon I might stop by the community garden to check on the tomatoes, or I’ll head to the library to return books I’ve been borrowing. Dinner is simple, and I end the day watching the nightly news on a small TV in the living room, sometimes followed by a documentary on PBS that I record on my DVR. I’m not heavy on technology, but I do have a tablet that I use to read articles and video chat with my kids.

## Politics and this election  
I voted Democratic in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one really looking out for seniors like me—protecting Social Security, keeping Medicare from being gutted, and pushing for more affordable prescription drugs. I also liked the candidate’s stance on immigration reform, which matters to me as a Hispanic family with roots in Central America. I wasn’t thrilled about the whole campaign circus, but the prospect of losing health coverage made me go to the polls.  

Now, for the 2026 midterms, I’m voting. My top concerns are keeping Social Security intact and making sure my prescription drug costs don’t keep climbing. I also worry about property taxes here in West Hartford; the town keeps raising them to fund schools, and I’m on a fixed income, so every extra dollar feels like a squeeze. When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem more attuned to the safety net we rely on, even if they argue about the details. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep pushing for tax cuts that mostly benefit the wealthy, and they talk a lot about “fiscal responsibility” while ignoring the fact that my Medicare check is already thin. I’m not convinced they understand what it’s like to have a limited budget and need reliable health care. I do get annoyed when both sides start shouting over each other on TV, but I’m more inclined to give the Democrats the benefit of the doubt because they’ve at least tried to address the issues that hit my generation directly.

## Media Diet  
I start my day with NPR’s morning news while I sip coffee, then I skim Newsweek articles in the afternoon when I’m on the porch, and I finish the evening with the CBS Nightly News broadcast. Altogether that adds up to about nine hours a week.
