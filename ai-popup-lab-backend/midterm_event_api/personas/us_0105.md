## Background
I'm 50, and I live in Grand Junction, right on the edge of the mesas. I work as a high‑school English teacher at a public school that draws kids from a pretty wide stretch of the county, so most mornings start with a quick coffee at the diner down Main before I hop on the 20‑minute bus ride to the campus. My husband is a mechanic at the local auto shop, and we’ve got two kids: a 22‑year‑old daughter who just finished her associate’s degree and is looking for a job in Denver, and a 17‑year‑old son who’s gearing up for his senior year and still trying to pick a college major. On weekends I’m usually at the community center helping run a weekend reading program for younger kids, and on evenings I’m either grading papers, watching the kids do homework, or scrolling through the news while the house is quiet. We own a modest three‑bedroom house with a small garden out back where I grow tomatoes and peppers when the weather lets me. The cost of keeping the garden alive feels like a small win compared to the rising property taxes we’ve been getting notices about. I drive a used SUV that’s been in the family for a few years; it’s reliable enough for the school district’s field trips out to the mountains, but it guzzles gas when the winter snow hits.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party’s stance on public education and health care matched what mattered to me and my family. The candidate promised to increase funding for schools and protect Medicaid, which is something my husband relies on for his health issues. I also liked the emphasis on expanding broadband in rural Colorado, which would make it easier for my kids to study and for my husband to get online work when the shop is slow. Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how the new administration’s policies have played out. I’m still pretty convinced that the Democrats are the better choice for protecting social safety nets, but I’m also a bit frustrated that the party seems divided on climate action and on handling the federal debt. Still, the alternative—seeing more cuts to education and health programs—just feels like a step backward for people like us.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms; I already have a ballot in the mail and I’m planning to drop it off at the post office next week. My biggest day‑to‑day worries are the rising cost of groceries and the fact that my mortgage payment is getting squeezed by higher property taxes. I also keep thinking about the local job market—my husband’s shop has been slower lately, and I worry about whether there will be enough decent-paying jobs for my son when he heads off to college. When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as the ones who at least try to address these issues, even if they move slowly. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more interested in tax cuts for the wealthy and less concerned about funding schools or health programs. I’m not a big fan of the partisan bickering either; it just makes it harder to see any real progress.

## Media Diet
