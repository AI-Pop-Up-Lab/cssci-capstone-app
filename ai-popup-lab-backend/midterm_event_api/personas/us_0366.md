## Background
I'm 45 and work as a data analyst for a biotech firm that has a lab in Worcester. My day starts around 6 am, getting the kids—aged 9 and 12—ready for school, packing lunches, and shuffling out the door. I drive the six miles to the office, where I spend most of my morning cleaning up data sets and running statistical models for clinical trial reports. Lunch is usually a quick sandwich at my desk while I scroll through my phone for headlines. After work I pick up the kids from after‑school sports, swing by the grocery store, and then head home to a modest two‑bedroom apartment in the Oak Hill neighborhood. My partner works night shifts as an EMT, so we split chores and try to keep the place tidy between his long hours and my weekend errands. On Saturdays I volunteer at the local library’s STEM tutoring program, and on Sundays I mostly sleep in and watch a few episodes of a streaming drama before the week starts again. The cost of living in this part of the state is high, so budgeting for rent, utilities, and the kids’ extracurricular fees takes up most of my discretionary cash.

## Politics and this election
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Between the endless campaign ads, feeling like none of the candidates really understood what a middle‑class family like mine goes through, and the fact that my partner was on a two‑week overtime stint the day before the election, I just let the day pass. The whole process feels like a show put on for the media, and I’m not convinced my vote makes a dent. I keep hearing about “the power of one” but it sounds hollow when the issues that matter—affordable housing, rising grocery prices, and decent childcare—never get real attention. So far I’m planning to sit this one out too. Unless there’s a sudden scandal that hits my community directly, or a local ballot measure that could affect my kids’ school funding, I don’t see a reason to bother with the midterms.

My biggest everyday worries are the rent hikes that keep creeping up and the grocery bills that seem to grow every month. When I do think about the parties, I’m annoyed by the Democrats for being complacent about the cost of living crisis, and I’m equally irritated by the Republicans for pushing policies that feel like they’re aimed at rural voters far away from my urban‑suburban reality. Both sides seem to talk past people like me, and I’m left to pick up the pieces on my own.
