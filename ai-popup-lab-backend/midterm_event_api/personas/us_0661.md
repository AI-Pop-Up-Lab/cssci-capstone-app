## Background  
I'm 30 and work as a policy analyst for a community development nonprofit in Jamaica, Queens. I earned a master's in public administration and spend most weekdays drafting grant proposals, meeting with local council staff, and crunching data on affordable‑housing projects. My partner and I share a two‑bedroom walk‑up; we have a three‑year‑old daughter who keeps us on our toes. Mornings start with a quick coffee while scrolling the news on my phone, then a 30‑minute subway ride to our office in downtown Queens. After work I usually swing by the corner bodega for groceries, pick up the kid from preschool, and try to squeeze in a short walk in the park before dinner. Weekends are a mix of laundry, helping my niece with school projects, and occasional visits to my parents in Brooklyn. I’m not a big fan of staying up late, so I usually wind down with a short episode of a streaming series or a podcast about urban policy.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was still the best bet for protecting the social safety net that my family relies on—things like Medicaid, SNAP, and the rent‑stabilization rules that keep our apartment affordable. I was also motivated by the candidate’s promises to invest in public transit upgrades, which would cut my commute time and reduce the crowding on the 7 train. Since then, I’ve watched the administration roll out a few infrastructure projects in Queens, but I’m also frustrated by the slow pace on climate‑resilient housing and the occasional flip‑flop on student loan relief.  

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are keeping my rent from spiraling higher and making sure the city fixes the potholes and broken elevators that make daily life a hassle. I also worry about the rising cost of groceries; a single trip to the supermarket feels like a gamble when prices keep creeping up. When I think about the parties, the Democrats still feel like the lesser of two evils—at least they’re more likely to protect tenant rights and fund public schools. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem out of touch with the realities of a working‑class family in the city; their talk about cutting taxes feels like a luxury I can’t afford, and their stance on climate policy feels like a gamble with my daughter’s future. I’m not a political scholar, but I trust my gut that the party that cares about city services and affordable housing is the one I should back, even if they sometimes miss the mark.
