## Background  
I'm 30, and I live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment in Wilmington, North Carolina, just a few blocks from the river. I work full‑time at a big‑box store as a floor supervisor; the job mostly requires me to keep the aisles stocked, handle customer complaints, and train the part‑time crew. I finished high school in a bilingual program, so I’m comfortable in both English and Spanish, but I never went to college because I needed to start earning right after graduation to help my family. My parents still live in a nearby town; I send them part of my paycheck each month, and I visit them on Sundays for a big family lunch that usually includes arroz con pollo and my mom’s tamales.  

When I'm not at work, I take the bus to the community center where I teach a weekly English‑as‑a‑second‑language class for other Hispanic adults. I also watch a few TV shows in the evenings, and on weekends I try to catch up with friends at the local coffee shop, where we trade gossip about rent hikes and the latest fast‑food deals. My life is a routine of early morning shifts, a quick lunch at the breakroom, and evenings spent scrolling on my phone while the TV blares the news.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in 2024. I felt like my vote wouldn't change anything, especially after hearing the same promises from both parties year after year. Between long shifts, caring for my parents, and the constant scramble to keep up with rent, I just didn't have the energy to fill out a ballot. The whole process felt like a hassle—finding a polling place, dealing with long lines, and then watching the results on TV while I was still at work. That feeling of cynicism has stuck with me, so for the 2026 midterms I'm sitting this one out again unless something dramatic happens in my neighborhood, like a sudden closure of the grocery store that supplies most of our community or a major water‑service issue.  

My biggest everyday worries are keeping up with rent and the rising price of groceries. When I go to the store, a loaf of bread costs more than it did last year, and my landlord just raised my rent by $100. I also worry about the local job market; the store where I work has been cutting hours, and I'm not sure what other options I have.  

When I think about the political parties, I see the Democrats as well‑meaning but often too focused on national issues and not enough on the concrete problems people like me face—affordable housing, reliable public transit, and decent wages. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more interested in cutting taxes for big corporations than helping working‑class families. Both sides feel distant, and I tend to roll my eyes when I hear campaign ads because they all sound the same to me.
