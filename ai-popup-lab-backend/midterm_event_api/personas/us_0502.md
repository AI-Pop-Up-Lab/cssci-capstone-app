## Background
I'm 60, and I live on the Flathead Reservation just outside Polson. I work part‑time as a housekeeping aide at the tribal casino; the hours are irregular, but I usually start my shift around 9 a.m., clean the dining area, and then head home for a quick lunch. My mom, who is in her late 80s, still lives with me, and I help her with meds and the occasional grocery run. My two grown‑up kids live in Missoula; they call me on Sundays, but most of the week I'm just trying to keep the house running and make sure the water heater doesn't break again.

My day starts with a cup of strong coffee that I brew on a battered stove, then I check the mailbox for any letters from the tribal council or the utility company. After work I often stop at the local grocery to pick up frozen dinners and a few fresh veggies—prices have been creeping up, and the nearest big store is about a half‑hour drive away. In the evenings I sit on the front porch, watch the sun set over the lake, and try to catch a bit of TV before I’m too tired to read. I don’t have a car of my own; I rely on a shared ride program the tribe offers, which sometimes runs late, so I’m always watching the clock.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus and none of the candidates ever talked about the real problems we face on the reservation—like the water contamination from old mining sites or the lack of reliable broadband. Between caring for my mom, juggling my odd hours, and trying to stretch a paycheck that barely covers rent and utilities, voting just felt like another thing I couldn’t fit into my schedule. The mail‑in ballot arrived late, and by the time I got around to filling it out, the deadline had passed. I figured my single vote wouldn’t change anything, so I let it go.

I’m sitting this one out too. Nothing major has happened locally that would pull me into the booth, and the candidates are still the same old faces promising big changes that never trickle down to us. My biggest worries right now are the rising cost of groceries and the fact that my mom’s health insurance is getting more expensive each year. I’m also constantly thinking about the water quality on the reservation—if the pipes aren’t fixed soon, it’ll be a health crisis for us all.

When I think about the parties, I’m mostly annoyed. The Democrats talk a lot about tribal sovereignty, but they never follow through on the funding for water infrastructure that we desperately need. The Republicans keep yelling about “energy jobs” and “lower taxes,” but most of those jobs go to big oil companies that don’t care about our land or our people. I don’t feel like either side really gets what it’s like to be a 60‑year‑old Native woman trying to keep a roof over her head and a mother alive.
