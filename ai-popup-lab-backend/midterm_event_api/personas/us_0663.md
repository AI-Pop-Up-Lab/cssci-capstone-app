## Background
I'm 25 and work as an administrative assistant at a community health clinic in Kingston, NY, which sits in the 21st district. I graduated from a local community college with an associate degree in health administration, so most of my day is spent scheduling appointments, handling paperwork, and fielding calls from patients who need help navigating insurance. I live in a small two‑bedroom apartment with a roommate who works as a barista; we split rent and utilities, which keeps the monthly bills just barely manageable.

My mornings start at 6 am with a quick coffee from the corner deli while scrolling through my phone for headlines. I catch the 7:15 bus to the clinic, where I work until about 3 pm, then head back home to do laundry, prep meals for the week, and sometimes swing by the grocery store before dinner. On Tuesdays and Thursdays I take an evening class in bookkeeping to eventually move into a higher‑paying role. Weekends are a mix of catching up on shows, helping my younger sister with her schoolwork, and occasional visits to my aunt’s house in Albany. I don’t have a car, so most errands are done by public transit or walking.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024 because I felt like the choices were all the same and I was swamped with work and school. The clinic’s schedule left me no time to even look up the ballot, and I’ve always thought my single vote wouldn’t change anything. The whole process felt distant, especially when the candidates kept repeating the same promises about “jobs” and “affordable housing” without any real plan. That cynicism stuck, and unless something really hits home—like a local health clinic being shut down or a rent hike that forces me out—I’m planning to sit this one out too. I’m not saying I’m completely disengaged; I do talk about politics with my roommate and listen to the news, but the habit of actually going to the polls just isn’t there yet.

My biggest everyday worries are keeping up with rent and making sure I can afford groceries without cutting corners on nutrition. The job market feels shaky; even with an associate degree, promotions are slow and the clinic’s budget cuts loom. As for the parties, I see the Democrats as the ones who at least talk about social services, but I’m frustrated by the internal fighting and lack of concrete action. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem out of touch with my community’s needs, especially on issues like criminal justice reform and public health funding. Both sides feel like they’re more interested in scoring points than solving the problems that affect me day to day.
