## Background
I'm 40, and I live in a modest house on the edge of West Fargo, just a few miles from the river that cuts through the city. I work full‑time as a warehouse supervisor for a regional distributor that ships furniture and appliances across the Upper Midwest. The job is mostly standing on concrete, directing a crew of about a dozen, making sure pallets are loaded right and the paperwork stays straight. My husband works part‑time at a local hardware store, and we have two kids – a 12‑year‑old daughter and an 8‑year‑old son. Mornings start at 5:30 a.m. with a quick coffee, a glance at the weather on the phone, and a rushed breakfast before I drive the kids to school and head to the warehouse. After a ten‑hour shift, I swing by the grocery store, pick up the kids from soccer practice, and try to fit in a little homework help before we all collapse on the couch. Weekends are a mix of yard work, a church potluck once a month, and the occasional visit to my parents' reservation about an hour north of Bismarck. I don’t have a lot of spare cash, so every dollar counts, and I’m always watching the price tags on groceries and the cost of heating the house when the wind picks up.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, the whole thing felt like a circus and I was too busy juggling two jobs and the kids’ schedules to even think about registering or finding a polling place. The ads were all the same – “vote for change” or “vote for stability” – and I never felt like either side was speaking to my everyday worries about the price of gas, the rent on our house, or whether my kids would have a stable job when they finish school. It just seemed like my vote wouldn’t move the needle, so I stayed home. I’m sitting this one out again in 2026 unless something dramatic hits our community – like a major factory closing or a sudden spike in property taxes that forces us to move. My top concerns right now are the grocery bills that keep creeping up and the property tax assessments that feel like a hidden tax on every paycheck. As for the parties, I see the Democrats as talking a lot about climate and social issues that feel distant from a working‑class family in North Dakota, while the Republicans keep pushing for lower taxes but sometimes ignore the fact that we still need decent roads and reliable broadband in rural areas. Both sides get on my nerves, but I’m more annoyed by the Democrats’ “big‑government” talk that never seems to translate into anything useful for my town.
