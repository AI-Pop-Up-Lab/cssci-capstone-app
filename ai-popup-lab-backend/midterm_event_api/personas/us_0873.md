## Background  
I'm 45 and I live in Vernal, right in the heart of Utah’s 2nd district. I grew up on the Uintah‑Ute Reservation and went to the local community college, where I earned an associate’s degree in automotive technology. After that I got a job as a diesel mechanic at the nearby oil‑sand processing plant; the work is hard, the shifts are long, but it pays the bills and lets me stay close to family. My wife works part‑time at the tribal health clinic, and we have two kids—12‑year‑old Alex and 9‑year‑old Maya—who both go to the public middle school a few miles out of town.

My days start early. I’m usually up by 5 a.m., making coffee and checking the weather before heading to the plant. The job is a mix of routine maintenance and emergency repairs, so I’m often on call. After my shift I pick up the kids from school, help with homework, and try to squeeze in a quick grocery run before dinner. Weekends are a blend of family time—fishing at the Green River, barbeques with the extended clan, and occasional pow‑wow gatherings—and a few hours of community service at the tribal council, where I sit on the transportation advisory board. I drive a used pickup, keep a modest savings account, and try to keep the house in good shape despite the wear and tear of the mountain climate.

## Politics and this election  
I voted Republican in 2024 because I felt the party’s stance on energy development lined up with my own livelihood. The oil‑sand project is a big employer here, and the GOP’s push for fewer federal restrictions meant more jobs and steadier wages for folks like me. I also liked the tax‑cut promises; they meant a bit more breathing room for my family’s budget, especially with school supplies and gas prices creeping up.

Since then, I’ve watched the headlines and heard a lot of talk about the federal government wanting to “re‑wild” more public lands. That scares me because it could mean tighter limits on drilling and mining, which would hit the plant’s output and, ultimately, my paycheck. On the other hand, I’ve heard some of my neighbors say the Democrats are trying to address the water quality issues that have been getting worse after the recent floods. I’m not sold on any of their solutions yet, but I’m paying attention.

I’m definitely going to the polls in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are keeping my job secure as the energy market shifts and making sure the cost of gasoline doesn’t keep climbing—both things that hit the family budget hard. I also worry about property taxes; the county has been talking about raising them to fund new schools, and that would bite right into our paycheck.

When I think about the parties, I see the Republicans as the ones who understand the need for a strong energy sector and who keep government from over‑reaching into our lives. The Democrats, in my view, talk a lot about climate and social programs, but they often forget the practical side of how those policies affect working families in places like mine. I’m not blind to the fact that the GOP can be messy and that not every Republican lawmaker cares about the West, but overall I feel they’re the better bet for my situation right now.
