## Background
I’m 74 and I live in a modest bungalow on a quiet street in Muncie, right in the heart of Indiana’s 3rd congressional district. After a 35‑year career as a professor of English literature at Ball State University, I retired three years ago. My days are slower now, but I still feel the pull of the campus; I drop by the library twice a week to give occasional guest lectures and to keep an eye on the new‑generation scholars. I’m widowed; my husband passed five years back, and we never had kids, but my sister’s two grown‑up children visit often and help with groceries and yard work. I’m also the primary caregiver for my mother, who’s in her late 80s and needs help with medication and transportation to her doctor’s appointments. 

Mornings start with a cup of black coffee on the porch while I scan the local paper and listen to the NPR morning news feed. After that I usually tend to the garden—tomatoes, beans, a few herbs—because fresh produce keeps my grocery bills down. Mid‑day I’m either at the community center, where I lead a book club for seniors, or at the grocery store, picking up eggs, milk, and the occasional treat for my mother. Evenings are simple: a light dinner, a bit of TV, and then I settle at my desk to write a short essay or reply to emails from former students. I drive a reliable old sedan, and I keep a weekly budget notebook because every dollar matters now that my pension is fixed.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate. I did it because I felt the party’s platform on protecting Social Security and Medicare was the only thing that could keep my retirement secure, and I also liked the emphasis on funding public education—something I still care about as a former professor. I was also motivated by the candidate’s promise to invest in rural broadband, which would help my mother’s doctor appointments go virtual when the weather’s bad. Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how the administration has handled inflation and the opioid crisis, issues that hit our community hard. I’m still convinced that the Democrats are more likely to safeguard the safety net I rely on, even though I get annoyed when the party talks down to older folks or seems to ignore small‑town concerns.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. I plan to cast my ballot at the downtown polling place on Election Day; I’ve already requested an absentee ballot as a backup just in case my mother’s health takes a turn. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the reliability of the local bus service, which I need to get my mother to her appointments. I also worry about property taxes—my modest house feels like a bigger financial burden each year. As for the parties, I view the Republicans as generally hostile to the programs that keep retirees like me afloat, especially when they push for cuts to Medicare. The Democrats, in my mind, are the better custodians of those programs, even if they sometimes get tangled up in internal fights that feel far removed from my daily life.

## Media Diet
I tune into NPR’s morning show while I sip coffee, catch up on ABC News headlines in the afternoon, and read a few feature articles on Newsweek in the evening. All together it’s about nine hours a week spread across those three outlets.
