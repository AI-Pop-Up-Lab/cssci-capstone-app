## Background  
I'm 80, and I live in the Oak Lawn neighborhood of Chicago's 10th congressional district. I spent 35 years teaching elementary school Spanish and retired five years ago, so most of my days now revolve around a routine I built over decades. I get up around 6 a.m., make a strong cup of coffee, and read the newspaper—mostly the local edition that still prints a few pages of community news. After that I take a short walk around the block, waving to the kids who ride their bikes past my house.  

My husband passed away three years ago, and my two grown‑up children live in the suburbs, so I get regular video calls from them and their families. My oldest grandson visits on Saturdays; we play cards and I try to teach him a few Spanish words. I volunteer at the nearby senior center twice a week, helping with the bingo tables and leading a small crafts group. On Sundays I attend mass at the local parish and then head to the grocery store, where I usually spend an hour comparing prices on beans, rice, and the medication I need for my arthritis.  

Even though I'm retired, I still keep a part‑time job at the community garden, pulling weeds and teaching kids how to plant tomatoes. It keeps me active and gives me a reason to get out of the house besides my church and the senior center. I drive a modest sedan that I've owned for years, and I’m careful about my budget because my Social Security check and a modest pension are what keep the lights on.

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party cared more about Medicare, prescription drug prices, and protecting Social Security—things that matter a lot to me at my age. The Republican platform seemed focused on tax cuts for big corporations and less on the safety net we seniors rely on. I also liked the candidate’s promise to invest in public transportation, which would make it easier for folks like me to get to medical appointments without driving.

I'm planning to vote again in the 2026 midterms. I think the stakes are still high for seniors; the upcoming Senate race could affect how much funding goes to Medicare and whether there are cuts to the drug‑price negotiation program. My top everyday concerns are keeping my prescription costs low and making sure my property taxes don’t skyrocket, which would force me to dip into my savings.  

When I think about the parties, I see Democrats as the ones who at least talk about protecting the programs that keep me alive and comfortable. The Republicans, in my view, often ignore the needs of older folks and seem more interested in cutting government spending in ways that end up hurting us. I’m not blind to flaws in either side—I know some Democrats have gotten complacent, and I’ve seen a few Republican colleagues who actually care about seniors—but overall I feel more reassured when a Democrat is in office.

## Media Diet  
I start my mornings with NPR’s hourly news updates while I sip coffee, then catch the evening broadcasts from ABC News and CBS News after dinner. I also scroll through NBC News headlines on my tablet before bed, and all together that adds up to about ten hours a week.
