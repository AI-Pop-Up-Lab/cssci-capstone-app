## Background
I'm 60, and I live in Fairbanks, a few miles out from the main street where most of the shops are. I work part‑time as a cashier at the local grocery store, pulling a shift that usually starts at 9 a.m. and ends around 3 p.m., then I head home to take care of a few chores. I don't have a high school diploma; I left school after eighth grade to help my mom with the family farm when I was a teenager. My dad passed away when I was in my early twenties, so I’ve been on my own ever since, except for my adult daughter who lives a couple of hours away in Anchorage and visits a few times a year.

My day kicks off early. I get up around 5:30 a.m., make a strong cup of coffee, and walk my old husky, Skye, around the block before the store opens. The weather in Fairbanks can be brutal, so I keep an eye on the temperature reports and make sure the car battery is good. After my shift, I usually stop by the community center to pick up a free meal or a flyer about a local event, but I rarely stay long. Evenings are mostly spent watching TV, scrolling through my phone, or fixing things around the house—like the leaky faucet in the kitchen that has been dripping for months. I don’t own a car that runs on premium gas; I drive a used sedan that gets decent mileage, which is important because fuel costs are a big part of my budget. I’m not married, and my social circle is pretty small—some coworkers, a neighbor who helps me shovel snow, and the occasional church gathering.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus. The candidates all seemed to be saying the same thing about “fixing Alaska,” but none of them ever mentioned the real problems I deal with—like how the price of heating oil spikes every winter and how the nearest specialist for my arthritis has to be driven to Juneau for an appointment. Being a part‑timer, I was also juggling a shift that conflicted with the early voting hours, and by the time I got home, I was too exhausted to even think about filling out a ballot. I’ve always thought my single vote wouldn’t change anything, especially when I see the same faces on TV promising change but never delivering on the road I drive every day.

I’m sitting this one out too. The midterms feel like the same old routine, and unless something huge—like a massive oil spill that threatens the local water supply or a sudden cut to the state health program—happens, I don’t see a reason to go to the polls. My top everyday concerns are keeping the house warm when the temperature drops below zero and affording my prescription meds. I’m also worried about the broadband service; when the connection drops, I can’t even order groceries online, and that’s a real hassle.

When I think about the parties, I’m mostly annoyed. The Republicans talk a lot about “energy independence,” but they keep pushing for drilling in places that would damage the very wilderness I love, and they never seem to address the high cost of living up here. The Democrats, on the other hand, keep promising more social programs, but they also talk about “green policies” that feel like they’d make fuel even more expensive, and they don’t understand how hard it is to get a decent internet connection in a place like Fairbanks. Both sides feel like they’re talking to people in the lower 48, not folks who have to drive miles for groceries or wait weeks for a doctor’s appointment. So, I’m just going to keep doing what I can and hope someone finally pays attention to the real issues down here.
