## Background
I’m 30, and I live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment in Modesto, right in the heart of California’s 30th congressional district. I work full‑time as a data analyst for a midsize ag‑tech startup that builds sensor platforms for farmers in the Central Valley. My day usually starts around 6 am with a quick coffee from the corner deli, then a 20‑minute drive to the office where I spend most of my morning cleaning up data sets and running predictive models. Lunch is usually a sandwich at my desk while I scroll through a mix of industry newsletters and the occasional news alert. After work I hit the gym three times a week, then come home to cook dinner—mostly pasta or stir‑fry because it’s cheap and quick. I share the place with a roommate who’s in grad school for social work; we split the rent and utilities, which keeps my monthly housing cost around $1,300. On weekends I either help my aunt with errands in the city or catch a late‑night movie with friends from church. I’m not married, don’t have kids, and I’m still paying off my master’s degree loan.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t cast a ballot in 2024. Between long hours at the office, the constant pressure to meet quarterly targets, and the feeling that neither candidate really addressed the everyday grind of people like me, I just shrugged it off. The whole process felt like a scripted performance—campaign ads promised “change” but the changes never seemed to reach the downtown neighborhoods where rent keeps climbing and grocery shelves stay empty. I also felt a bit alienated by the endless partisan bickering; it seemed like politicians were more interested in scoring points than solving the water‑quality issues that affect our farms and, by extension, my job.

Going into the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out again unless something blows up locally—like a major water‑rights dispute that threatens my workplace or a sudden spike in property taxes that would force me out of my apartment. My top everyday concerns are keeping my rent affordable and making sure grocery prices don’t keep rising faster than my paycheck. I’m generally annoyed with both parties: Democrats talk a good game about social safety nets, but I rarely see that money trickle down to the community programs that actually help. Republicans, on the other hand, seem to ignore the cost‑of‑living crisis and keep pushing policies that feel out of touch with a city that’s trying to grow sustainably. So I stay disengaged, hoping that at some point a candidate will actually listen to the grind of a data analyst trying to make ends meet.
