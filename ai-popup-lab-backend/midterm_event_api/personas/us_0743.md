## Background  
I'm 35 and work as a customer‑service supervisor at a regional call center in Lancaster County. My shift starts at 8 a.m., so I’m usually up by 6:30, scrolling through my phone while I make coffee. After a quick breakfast of toast and soy milk, I catch the morning news on my tablet, then hop on the bus to the office. The commute takes about 20 minutes, and I spend most of that time listening to a mix of NPR podcasts and the daily market roundup on CNBC.

I live in a two‑bedroom apartment on the east side of Lancaster with my older brother, who moved back after his job in Philadelphia ended. We split rent and utilities, and we both keep a small garden on the balcony where I try to grow herbs between shifts. Weekends are a blur of grocery runs, grocery‑store sales hunting, and occasional visits to my parents in nearby Reading. My parents still run a small family restaurant, so I help out with deliveries when I can. I’m not married, don’t have kids, and my social life mostly revolves around a tight group of friends I met at a community college cooking class. We meet at a local dim sum place for drinks after work, but most evenings I’m home, watching a show on a streaming service or scrolling through social media before bed.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t cast a ballot in 2024. Between juggling long shifts, caring for my brother’s health issues, and the feeling that the candidates were all the same—promising big changes but never delivering—I just didn’t see the point. The whole process felt like a chore, and the endless ads left me more annoyed than motivated. I also thought my single vote wouldn’t move the needle in a district that seems to swing back and forth every cycle.

I’m sitting this 2026 midterm out too, unless something dramatic happens locally—like a major factory closing or a sudden spike in property taxes that hits my rent directly. My day‑to‑day concerns are paying the rent on time and keeping grocery prices from spiraling higher. I’m also watching the local job market; a few of my coworkers have been laid off, and I worry about the call center cutting hours.

When I think about the parties, I’m mostly fed up with both. The Democrats, in my view, keep talking about “affordable healthcare” but the premiums keep creeping up, and they seem to ignore the small‑business tax burdens that affect places like the restaurant my parents run. The Republicans, on the other hand, push for lower taxes and deregulation, but the rhetoric feels like it’s aimed at a different demographic—often ignoring the concerns of immigrant families like mine who are trying to make ends meet. I don’t feel a strong loyalty to either side; I just want policies that keep my rent affordable and my grocery bill reasonable.
