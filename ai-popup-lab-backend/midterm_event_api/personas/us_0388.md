## Background  
I'm 40, and I live in a modest two‑bedroom condo in the Towson area of Maryland’s 3rd district. I work full‑time as a marketing analyst for a mid‑size health‑tech firm that outsources a lot of its data work to the DC metro region. My day starts around 6 am; I brew a quick cup of tea, scroll through the news on my phone while the kettle warms, then hop on a commuter train to the office in Bethesda. The ride is usually packed, so I listen to a NPR podcast about consumer trends to pass the time.  

After a nine‑hour day, I head home, pick up my six‑year‑old daughter from after‑school soccer, and make dinner—usually something quick like stir‑fry or frozen pizza while I catch up on a few emails. My husband works nights as an EMT, so we coordinate our schedules around his shifts. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs at the local Safeway, helping my daughter with school projects, and occasional visits to my parents who live in the suburbs of Baltimore. I’m not a big fan of big‑ticket vacations; when I do travel, it’s usually a short drive to the Shenandoah Valley for a hike.  

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between my demanding job, my husband’s erratic schedule, and trying to keep up with my daughter’s school activities, I just never found a quiet moment to fill out a ballot. On top of that, I feel like the choices are the same old faces promising the same vague stuff, and I’m skeptical that any of them will actually fix the rising cost of living that hits my family the hardest. I’m also a bit annoyed by how much political ads clutter my social feeds; they just add noise.  

For the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out again. Unless something blows up locally—like a major school funding cut that directly affects my daughter’s program—I don’t see a reason to carve out the time. My biggest everyday worries are the mortgage payments that keep creeping up and the grocery bills that feel like they’re inflating faster than my salary. I also worry about property taxes; the county keeps proposing new levies that would hit us hard.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they’re more focused on identity politics and big‑government solutions that don’t translate to lower bills for ordinary families. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to double down on tax cuts for the wealthy while ignoring the middle‑class squeeze I feel every month. Both sides talk about “working for the people,” but in practice they’re more about scoring points than fixing my mortgage or making groceries affordable.
