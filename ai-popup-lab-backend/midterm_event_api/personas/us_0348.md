## Background
I'm 25 and work as an environmental engineer for a petrochemical company just outside Baton Rouge, in the part of the state that falls in LA‑6. I finished my master's in environmental science two years ago, and the job was the first decent paycheck I could get after a string of internships. I live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment with a roommate who’s in the oil‑field services business. We split rent, utilities, and the occasional take‑out order on weekends.

My day usually starts at 6 am with a quick coffee from the corner diner, then a thirty‑minute drive to the plant where I spend most of my morning checking emissions reports and running compliance software. Lunch is a sandwich at my desk while I scroll through the news on my phone. After work, I either hit the gym or meet my roommate for a beer at a local bar, depending on how tired I am. Sundays are for grocery shopping and catching up on a couple of episodes of a streaming series. I don’t have kids, and my parents live about an hour away in a small town; I visit them a few times a year.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between long hours at the plant, the endless paperwork, and the feeling that my single vote wouldn’t change anything, I just let the day pass. The whole process felt like a performance—candidates shouting about stuff that never touched my daily grind, and the ballot itself seemed like an afterthought. I also felt pretty cynical after hearing about the endless campaign ads and the way the two parties kept trading the same old promises while my paycheck stayed the same.

I’m sitting this one out, too. The 2026 midterms don’t have anything that’s pulling me in, unless there’s a sudden local scandal about the refinery I work at or a drastic change in my paycheck. My biggest worries are the rising cost of gas and the stability of my job. If the price of diesel spikes, my commute gets more expensive, and if the plant cuts staff, I could be out of work. I also keep an eye on property taxes because my rent is already a big chunk of my income, and any increase in local taxes could push me into a tighter spot.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats keep talking about climate action, but the policies they push feel unrealistic for someone working in an industry that’s still the backbone of the regional economy. The Republicans, on the other hand, love to tout oil subsidies and deregulation, which keeps my job safe, but they also bring a lot of cultural rhetoric that feels out of touch with the everyday concerns of people like me. In short, I’m fed up with the noise and just want policies that keep my paycheck steady and my gas tank full.

## Media Diet
