## Background  
I'm 84 and I live in a modest walk‑up apartment on the east side of Worcester, right in the heart of MA‑3. I never finished high school; after dropping out at sixteen I worked as a housekeeper for a local church and later as a laundry attendant at a nursing home. I retired about ten years ago when my husband passed, and now I get a modest Social Security check and a small pension from the church. My two grown‑up children live in Boston and Springfield, so they call most weekends, and my granddaughter visits once a month with her kids.  

My days are pretty routine. I get up around 7 a.m., turn on the radio for the news while I make coffee, then head down to the corner market for a bag of frozen peas and a loaf of bread. I attend the 10 a.m. service at the Baptist church on Tuesdays and Thursdays; the choir is a nice way to keep my mind busy. After church I usually sit on the porch with a crossword puzzle, and in the afternoons I either watch a bit of TV or go to the senior center for bingo and a light lunch. The local senior meals program drops a hot plate at my door on Wednesdays, which is a lifesaver when my knees act up. I try to walk a few blocks each day, mostly to the pharmacy for my blood‑pressure meds, but sometimes the arthritis slows me down and I have to sit on the bench outside the pharmacy and watch the street.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in 2024. Between my health issues, the long lines at the polling place, and the feeling that none of the candidates really cared about people my age, I just stayed home. The whole campaign seemed like a circus, and I couldn't see any reason to waste my energy on a system that keeps raising property taxes while my rent is already stretched thin. I also felt that the ballot didn't reflect anything that mattered to me—no clear plans for prescription‑drug costs or senior nutrition programs.  

Going into the 2026 midterms, I'm sitting this one out again. Unless something dramatic happens—like a proposal to cut funding for the senior meals I rely on, or a local scandal that directly affects my building—I don't see a point in casting a vote. My top everyday concerns are the price of my blood‑pressure medication and the rising property taxes that make it harder to keep my apartment. I also worry about the reliability of the public transportation that gets me to the pharmacy and the senior center.  

When I think about the parties, I feel the Democrats are at least trying to talk about health‑care and senior benefits, but they still seem to forget Black seniors like me, especially in the way they handle policing and community investment. The Republicans, on the other hand, feel like they're talking about “big government” and “tax cuts” that never trickle down to someone on a fixed income. I get annoyed when I hear talk about “personal responsibility” without any safety net. So, if I ever do decide to vote, I'd probably lean toward a Democrat who actually pushes for cheaper meds and stronger senior services, but I'm not holding my breath.

## Media Diet  
I tune into ABC News in the mornings while I make coffee, listen to NPR podcasts during my afternoon walks, and catch the evening roundup on CBS News—about ten hours total each week.
