## Background
I’m 60, and I live in a modest duplex in North Kingstown, Rhode Island, that I own with my husband. I work full‑time as a home‑health aide, helping elderly clients with bathing, medication reminders, and light housekeeping. My day kicks off around 5:30 a.m.; I brew coffee, feed the dog, and drive an hour to the senior center where I stay until about 2 p.m. After work I swing by the grocery store, run a few errands for a neighbor, and sometimes help my teenage granddaughter with her English homework—she lives with my son in Providence. My husband, a retired shipyard worker, spends his afternoons tinkering around the house and watching sports on the TV. We’re still paying a modest mortgage and trying to stash enough for a smaller place when we finally retire. Most weekends I’m at the community garden or a church potluck, and in the evenings I unwind with a TV drama or a late‑night news roundup. I don’t drive long distances; the nearest grocery is a 10‑minute drive, and I usually take the bus for doctor appointments.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between my long work shifts, caring for my husband’s health issues, and the feeling that none of the candidates really understood what a low‑wage caregiver like me needs, the ballot just felt like a distant chore. The ads were all about big‑ticket issues—foreign policy, climate targets—while I was worrying about whether my next paycheck would cover my prescription costs. I also got the sense that both parties were more interested in scoring points than actually fixing the potholes on our local roads or protecting Medicare. That cynicism, plus a schedule that left me exhausted by the time polls opened, meant I stayed home.

I’m sitting this one out, too. Nothing dramatic has happened locally that would pull me into the booth, and I still think my vote won’t shift the big picture. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription medication and the property taxes that keep creeping up on our modest home. When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem to promise a lot of social programs but rarely deliver on the ground‑level fixes I need, while the Republicans talk about “fiscal responsibility” but ignore the safety net that keeps people like me afloat. Both sides feel out of touch, and I’m not willing to waste an afternoon on a process that feels like a performance.

## Media Diet
I usually catch up on NPR podcasts in the morning while getting ready, scroll through CBS News headlines during my lunch break, and read the New York Post’s evening roundup after dinner; it adds up to about five hours a week.
