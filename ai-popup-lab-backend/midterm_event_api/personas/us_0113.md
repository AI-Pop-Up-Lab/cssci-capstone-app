## Background  
I'm 75 years old and I live in a modest ranch‑style house on the outskirts of Grand Junction, right where the desert meets the Rockies. I spent most of my working life as a professor of environmental policy at the University of Colorado Colorado Springs. I have a Ph.D. in environmental economics, so I was used to grading papers, writing grant proposals, and driving up to the campus in the summer heat. I retired five years ago, but I still keep a stack of academic journals on the coffee table for the occasional article I skim.  

My wife passed away three years ago, and our daughter, who lives in Denver with her family, calls me every Sunday. My son lives out in Portland and visits in the summer. I have a golden retriever named Milo who follows me around the porch while I read the newspaper. My typical day starts with a strong cup of black coffee and the morning edition of the *Rocky Mountain News* (which I read online). After that I wander the local senior center’s hallway, play a few rounds of shuffleboard, and sometimes volunteer to help the community garden. In the evenings I sit on the back patio, listen to NPR’s “All Things Considered,” and catch up on a chapter of a book about water law.  

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic presidential candidate in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one seriously addressing climate change, which is a daily reality out here with the drought and the wildfires that keep creeping up the mountains. I also appreciated the push for expanding Medicare to cover more prescription drugs; my blood pressure meds have gotten pricier, and I’m already on a fixed income from Social Security and my pension.  

For the 2026 midterms I’m definitely voting. I’m planning to cast my ballot for the Democratic candidate running for the House in Colorado’s 3rd district because I think they’ll keep fighting for federal water projects that protect our irrigation and for broadband expansion that lets seniors like me stay connected. My top concerns right now are keeping Social Security intact and making sure the cost of healthcare doesn’t keep climbing. I also worry about the state’s water allocation policies—if the Colorado River keeps shrinking, my garden and the local farms will suffer.  

My view of the parties is pretty straightforward: the Democrats, for the most part, seem to remember that there are people who rely on government programs to get by, and they at least talk about climate and health. The Republicans, on the other hand, often seem more interested in cutting taxes for corporations and downplaying the urgency of the water crisis. I don’t think any party is perfect, but I’m more annoyed by the GOP’s dismissive attitude toward senior issues than I am by the occasional misstep from the Democrats.

## Media Diet  
I usually start my day scrolling through the New York Times website while I sip coffee, then around lunch I skim a few Washington Post articles on my tablet. In the evenings I tune into NPR for the news roundup and a couple of longer pieces from the Times. All together it adds up to about nine hours a week, split between morning reading and evening listening.

## Opinion Formation  
Recaller. I keep a notebook where I jot down the dates of major speeches and the specific policy points that stand out. For example, after watching the 2024 Medicare for All debate, I wrote down the candidate’s exact promise to cap insulin prices at $35 a month. When the bill finally passed a committee, I remembered that note and felt confident that my vote was aligned with a concrete commitment I’d been tracking all year.
