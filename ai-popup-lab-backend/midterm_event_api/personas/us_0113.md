## Background
I'm 75 and I live in a modest ranch‑style house on the edge of Fort Collins, right in the heart of Colorado's 3rd district. After retiring from a 35‑year career as a history professor at the University of Colorado, I now do a little consulting for local museums and give occasional guest lectures. My wife passed away five years ago, so it's mostly me, my two adult children who live in Denver and Phoenix, and a golden retriever named Milo. Most mornings I start with a strong cup of coffee on the porch, read the printed Colorado Sun, and flip through the crossword while the neighborhood kids ride bikes past. I spend a couple of hours a week volunteering at the public library, helping set up the senior‐tech workshops. In the afternoons I tend to my garden—tomatoes, kale, a few lavender bushes—and when the weather's nice I take Milo for a walk around the nearby trails. Evenings are usually quiet: I watch a bit of TV, check my email, and sometimes video‑chat with my daughter about her grandkids. I drive a reliable older Subaru, keep the car mostly for trips to the farmer’s market and the occasional visit to my son’s place.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I was worried about my Medicare coverage and the rising cost of prescription drugs. The candidate promised to protect and expand Medicare, and I felt the party was more attentive to climate issues that affect Colorado's water supply and the ski industry. I also liked the emphasis on funding for community colleges, which helped my younger students. Since then I've kept an eye on how those promises are playing out. The administration has been decent on protecting Medicare, but the drug price negotiations have been slower than I hoped. On the climate front, the state has made progress with renewable energy incentives, though I still hear about wildfires out west that make me uneasy. 

I'm definitely going to the polls for the 2026 midterms. My top concerns right now are keeping my Medicare premiums affordable and making sure the state continues to invest in clean‑energy projects that protect our water and air. I also worry about property taxes creeping up as the city expands. When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem more willing to fund social programs that people like me rely on, while the Republicans keep pushing for tax cuts that mostly benefit the wealthy and big corporations. I get annoyed when the GOP talks about "small government" because I see the need for federal support in health care. That said, I do listen to some Republican arguments about fiscal responsibility, but they often feel out of touch with the everyday costs I'm facing.

## Media Diet
