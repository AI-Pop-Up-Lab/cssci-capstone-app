## Background  
I’m 25 and I work as a research coordinator for a community health project at a small nonprofit in Northampton. I graduated two years ago with a bachelor's in public health, so most days I’m shuffling between the office in the downtown arts district and the local clinic where we collect data. I rent a two‑bedroom apartment with a roommate who’s in grad school for environmental engineering; we split the rent and the chores, and the place is a bit cramped but cheap enough for us on our combined incomes.  

My mornings start with a quick bike ride to the office, a latte from the café next door, and a scan of the local bus schedule. I’m on the bus for about 20 minutes, listening to NPR’s “Morning Edition” while catching up on national headlines. At work I spend most of the day pulling together survey data, entering it into spreadsheets, and meeting with community partners about upcoming health fairs. Lunch is usually a sandwich I bring from home, eaten at a park bench when the weather’s nice.  

After work I either head back to the apartment to cook dinner—usually something cheap and quick like pasta—or I meet my roommate for a yoga class at the community center. Evenings are a mix of streaming a sitcom, scrolling through social media, and sometimes reading a long‑form piece on Newsweek when I have the time. Weekends I either help out at the local farmers’ market or take a short drive to the Berkshires to hike. I don’t have kids, and my parents live out of state, so my social life revolves around a tight circle of friends from college and work.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between juggling a full‑time job, a demanding research schedule, and the constant pressure of paying rent, I felt like my ballot wouldn’t change anything. The campaign ads were the same old talking points, and I was sick of the endless “who’s better” rhetoric. I also felt alienated by the parties; the Democrats seemed to take my concerns about affordable housing for granted, and the Republicans were talking about issues that felt far removed from my daily reality in western Massachusetts.  

I’m sitting this one out again in 2026. Nothing major has happened to pull me into the voting booth, and I still think my vote is a drop in an ocean that’s already decided. My biggest worries right now are keeping up with rent and the rising cost of groceries—especially fresh produce, which is ironic given I work on a health project. I’m also watching the local job market; the nonprofit sector is tight, and I’m anxious about whether there will be enough stable positions for people with my background.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they’re comfortable in the power seat and take progressive rhetoric for granted without delivering on the ground level. The Republicans feel like they’re stuck in the past, talking about tax cuts and cultural issues that don’t help a 25‑year‑old trying to make ends meet. I’m frustrated with the whole system, which is why I keep my distance unless something truly local—like a threatened community health clinic—forces me to act.

## Media Diet  
I get my news in four weekly hours: NPR in the mornings on my commute, ABC News updates in the evenings after work, and I skim a couple of Newsweek articles whenever I have a spare half‑hour on the weekend.
