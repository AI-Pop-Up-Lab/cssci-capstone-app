## Background  
I'm 25 and I work as a junior accountant at a regional insurance firm in Gulfport. After finishing my degree in finance at a state university, I moved back home to help my parents with some repairs on their house while I saved up for my own place. I rent a two‑bedroom apartment a few blocks from the beach, sharing it with a fellow recent grad who works in hospitality. My days start early: I catch the 6 a.m. bus, grab a coffee, and skim the NPR headlines on my phone while the bus rolls past the pier. The office is a quiet open‑plan space; I spend most of my time reconciling expense reports and running quarterly audits. Lunch is usually a sandwich from a nearby deli, and I try to squeeze in a quick game of basketball at the community center twice a week. Evenings are a mix of streaming shows, scrolling through social media, and sometimes helping my mom order groceries online. On weekends I either go fishing with my dad or volunteer at the local community college tutoring center, helping other Asian‑American students navigate the transition to college life. I don’t own a car, so everything is tied to the bus schedule, which makes me pretty aware of any changes to public‑transport funding.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was the only one seriously talking about expanding health coverage and making college more affordable. My own student loans were still hanging over my head, and the promise of a tuition‑free community college program felt like a lifeline. I also cared about climate policy; living on the Gulf Coast, I’ve seen a few bigger storms hit, and I wanted a government that would invest in resilient infrastructure. The campaign rallies in Gulfport were pretty lively, and a few of my coworkers gave me rides to the polling place, which made the whole thing feel less like a chore and more like a community effort.

Since then my view of the parties has sharpened a bit. I still think Democrats are more attuned to issues that affect younger folks like me—student debt, rent spikes, and climate preparedness—but I’m also frustrated by what I see as endless infighting and a lack of clear messaging on the economy. The Republicans in the district keep pushing for lower taxes, which sounds good on paper, but I hear from my parents that those cuts often translate into fewer services for things like road maintenance and public schools, which directly affect our daily lives. I’m not a fan of the more hard‑line rhetoric on immigration and social issues that some GOP candidates use; it feels out of touch with the diverse community we have here.

For the 2026 midterms, I’m definitely going to the polls. My top concerns right now are the rising cost of rent—my lease is up next year and prices have jumped at least 8 % in the last six months—and the uncertainty around my student loans as the federal repayment policies keep shifting. I also keep an eye on local job growth; the insurance firm I work for is doing okay, but I hear friends in the hospitality sector worried about seasonal layoffs. Overall, I see the Democratic Party as the better bet for protecting the social safety net, while I remain skeptical of Republican promises that seem to ignore the realities of a coastal, multicultural district like ours.
