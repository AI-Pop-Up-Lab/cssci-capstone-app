## Background
I'm 25 and work the night shift as a stock clerk at the big‑box store down on Route 8. I grew up in a working‑class family in Naugatuck, and after dropping out of high school I bounced between a few odd jobs before landing this gig. My mom still lives in the house we grew up in, and I rent a small upstairs room above a laundromat on Main Street to be closer to work. My roommate is a fellow 20‑something who does freelance graphic work from a laptop—she's the only one in the house with a college degree.

My days start early: I’m up at 5 a.m., make a quick coffee, and catch the bus to the warehouse. The shift is mostly standing, scanning barcodes, and restocking shelves while the store fills with morning shoppers. I come home around 2 a.m., eat whatever’s in the fridge, and try to get a few hours of sleep before the cycle repeats. On my days off I usually hit the gym for a quick workout, scroll through my phone, and sometimes go to the local fast‑food joint to hang out with a few coworkers. I don’t have a car, so everything is by bus or on foot. Money is tight; most of my paycheck goes to rent, utilities, and the occasional grocery run.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between juggling a night shift, a part‑time gig to make ends meet, and dealing with a leaky roof in my room, politics felt like a luxury I couldn’t afford. The candidates all seemed the same to me—big promises, empty speeches, and none of them seemed to understand what it’s like to scramble for a paycheck. I also got fed up with the endless ads and the way the news keeps yelling at you to “vote or lose everything.” It just added noise to a life that’s already noisy.

I’m sitting this 2026 midterm out, too. Unless something hits close to home—like a local factory closing or a sudden rent hike—I don’t see a point in making the trip to the polling place. My biggest worries are keeping my room roof from collapsing and making sure there’s enough money for groceries next month. I’ve tried to stay informed, but the headlines feel like they’re made for people who have time to read a full article, not for someone pulling a double‑shift.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats talk about “big government” while the Republicans keep shouting about “tax cuts” that never reach people like me. Both sides seem more interested in scoring points than fixing the everyday stuff—like affordable housing, reliable public transit, and decent wages. I guess I’m more cynical than hopeful, but I still keep an eye out for any candidate who actually mentions the cost of rent or the price of a gallon of milk.
