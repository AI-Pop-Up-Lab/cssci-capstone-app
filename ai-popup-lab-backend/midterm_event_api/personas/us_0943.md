## Background
I'm 20, a sophomore at the University of Wisconsin‑Madison studying sociology. I live in a small three‑person apartment on the near‑west side, just a few blocks from campus. My roommates are both out‑of‑state students, so we split rent, utilities, and the occasional pizza order. Most mornings I grab a coffee at the campus café, then head to my first class—usually a lecture on race and public policy. After classes I work a 15‑hour a week shift as a barista at a local coffee shop to help cover my tuition and rent. Evenings are a mix of homework, streaming a show on Netflix, and scrolling TikTok while trying to keep up with friends back home in Milwaukee. My parents visit during spring break; they’re both retired teachers who still talk about the “good old days” of civil rights activism. I’m involved in a student organization that focuses on criminal justice reform, but most of my time feels pulled between school, work, and trying to stay afloat financially.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, it felt like a waste of time. Between juggling two jobs, a heavy course load, and constantly worrying about whether my next rent check will clear, I couldn’t justify spending a Saturday at a polling place. I also felt like the choices on the ballot didn’t speak to my reality—none of the candidates seemed to care about the student debt crisis or the rising cost of groceries in Madison. The whole system just feels like a loop of promises that never change anything for people like me. I’m not thrilled about the idea of voting again in 2026, so I’m sitting this one out unless something huge happens locally—like a major protest that gets national attention or a policy change that directly affects my tuition or housing.

My top everyday concerns are keeping up with tuition payments and finding an affordable place to live. Rent keeps going up, and the stipend from my barista job barely covers it after tuition and books. The other thing that keeps me up at night is the price of groceries; a simple basket of food feels like a luxury sometimes. When I think about the parties, I’m pretty skeptical of both. The Democrats talk a lot about social justice, but I rarely see that translate into real funding for community programs or student loan relief. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to ignore issues that matter to me—like reproductive rights, climate action, and police reform—while pushing policies that feel like they benefit the wealthy. I don’t feel represented by either side, and that’s why I’m not motivated to cast a ballot this cycle.
