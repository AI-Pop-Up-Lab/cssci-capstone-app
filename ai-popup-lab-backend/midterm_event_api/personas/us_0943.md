## Background  
I'm 20 years old and a sophomore at the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, majoring in communications. I grew up in the North End of Milwaukee, but for school I live in a small two‑bedroom apartment in Greenfield with my roommate, Maya, who’s also a junior. My parents still live in the city; I visit them on weekends for Sunday dinner, which usually means fried catfish and collard greens.  

To pay for tuition and rent, I work evenings at a local coffee shop near the campus. The shift starts at 5 p.m. and ends around 10 p.m., so most of my day is a mix of lectures, library study sessions, and the occasional campus club meeting—I'm on the Black Student Union board, which keeps me busy with events and fundraising. When I'm not in class or at work, I’m scrolling through TikTok, texting friends, or trying to find a cheap streaming plan for the shows I binge on weekends.  

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. At the time I was still figuring out how to register and felt like the choices on the ballot didn’t speak to my life as a student dealing with rent hikes and mounting student loans. I also thought, “Why waste my time on a race I’m not sure about?” That kind of indecision stuck with me for a while.  

Now, looking at the 2026 midterms, I’m actually planning to cast a ballot. I signed up for early voting at the Greenfield clerk’s office last month because I realized that the state legislature is debating a bill that could raise the property tax rate on renters—something that would directly affect my monthly budget. My top concerns are affordable housing and the cost of my student loans. I’ve seen my roommate get a notice that her rent could go up by $150 next year, and the idea of having to juggle that with a growing debt load makes me uneasy.  

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty skeptical of both. The Democrats talk a lot about student debt relief, but the programs they propose feel half‑baked and don’t reach people like me who are just starting out. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep pushing for cuts to social programs and seem to ignore the fact that many of us are working multiple jobs just to stay afloat. I’m not a fan of the rhetoric from either side; I’m looking for a candidate who actually has a plan to keep rent from spiraling and maybe offers some concrete student‑loan forgiveness that isn’t just a political talking point.  

## Media Diet  
I squeeze in about an hour of news each week. I usually scroll through the NYTimes app on my phone while I’m waiting for my coffee to brew in the morning, and I catch a quick CNN segment on the news channel while I’m getting ready for my shift. Once a week I listen to an NPR podcast on the bus home, just to hear a longer take on the issues that matter to me.  

## Opinion Formation  
My opinion formation style is Constructor. I don’t sit down and read policy papers before deciding; I go with what feels right in the moment. For example, last week a friend told me about a local candidate who promised to work with landlords to cap rent increases. I didn’t dig into their voting record; I just liked the idea that someone was “actually listening” to renters, so I decided to support them at the upcoming town hall. That gut feeling, plus a quick chat with Maya, shaped my stance more than any deep research.
