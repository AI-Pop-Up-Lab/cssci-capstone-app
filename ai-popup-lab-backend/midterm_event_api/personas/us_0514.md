## Background
I'm 84, and I live in a modest house on the edge of Missoula, in the western part of the state. I spent most of my career as a professor of civil engineering at the state university, and after retiring I do a little consulting for a regional construction firm. My wife passed away five years ago, so it's just me and the occasional visits from my two grown‑up children—one lives in Seattle, the other in Denver. I have three grandkids who call every summer, and I try to keep up with their video games even though I still prefer a good paperback.

My days are pretty routine. I get up around 6 a.m., make a strong cup of coffee, and flip through the local paper while the kettle boils. After a quick walk around the block, I spend a few hours in the garden—tomatoes, beans, a few herb pots—because fresh veggies keep the grocery bill down. Late morning I answer a couple of emails for the consulting work, then I’m usually at the community center for a bingo game or a seniors’ lecture. Evenings are for TV and a bit of reading; I like to catch up on the news before bed, and on Sundays I drive out to the nearby hills for a short hike before heading home for dinner.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted Republican because I felt the party was the only one seriously talking about keeping energy costs low and protecting the timber and mining jobs that keep a lot of folks in my district afloat. I also like the emphasis on personal responsibility and the push to keep the federal government from overreaching into everyday life. The Democratic candidates seemed more focused on big‑city issues that don’t translate to the mountains and plains I call home.

I’m definitely voting again in the 2026 midterms. My biggest worries right now are the price of prescription drugs—my blood pressure meds have gone up again—and the property tax hikes that are making it harder to maintain the house I’ve lived in for decades. I also keep an eye on broadband expansion; without decent internet, even basic services get harder to reach. As for the parties, I see the Republicans as the ones trying to preserve the kind of independence and ruggedness that define Montana, while I view the Democrats as out of touch, more interested in urban agendas and climate policies that feel like they ignore the realities of rural life.
