## Background  
I'm 65, and I live in Groton, a small town on the Thames River that’s part of Connecticut’s 5th district. I work full‑time as a maintenance supervisor at the local naval shipyard; the job’s mostly fixing HVAC units, doing routine inspections, and making sure the break rooms stay stocked. I didn’t go to college, so the high‑school diploma I got in 1964 is the highest credential I have.  

I’m married to Ellen, who retired from the public school system a few years ago. We have two grown‑up kids—one lives in Boston, the other in Hartford—and they visit us every summer. Most mornings I’m up by 5 a.m. to grab a coffee, read the paper, and head to the shipyard for a 7 a.m. shift. After work I stop by the local grocery store, pick up a few things for dinner, and then we either watch a game on TV or tend to the garden in the backyard. On Saturdays I help out at the community center, fixing the gym equipment and occasionally mowing the lawn for a neighbor who can’t get out. Sundays are slower; we attend a non‑denominational service and then spend the afternoon on the porch, watching the river and chatting with the occasional passerby.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in the 2024 presidential election. Between long hours at the shipyard, caring for Ellen’s arthritis meds, and the feeling that none of the candidates really understood the day‑to‑day grind of people like us, I just didn’t see a point. The whole process felt like a circus, and I was convinced my single vote wouldn’t change anything. That same sense of cynicism is still with me, so I’m sitting this one out too. I’ve got a lot on my plate already—rising property taxes that are squeezing our fixed retirement income, and the cost of prescription drugs that keeps creeping up. Those are the two things that keep me up at night more than any campaign ads.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem to promise a lot of social programs but then raise taxes to pay for them, which hurts retirees like us. The Republicans talk about cutting taxes, but most of the proposals I hear about either skip over the middle class or swing far to the right on social issues I’m not comfortable with. Honestly, both sides feel disconnected from the concerns of a small‑town worker who just wants to keep his house, his health, and his peace of mind.

## Media Diet  
I catch up on ABC News and CBS News in the evenings after dinner, and I listen to NPR’s hourly updates during my morning commute. I also skim NBC News headlines on my phone while waiting for Ellen’s appointments, adding up to about seven hours a week.
