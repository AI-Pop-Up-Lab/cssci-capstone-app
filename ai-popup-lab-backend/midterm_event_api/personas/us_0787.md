## Background
I'm 45, and I live in a modest house on the edge of Spartanburg, South Carolina, in the 1st congressional district. I work full‑time as a cashier at the local grocery store, pulling the afternoon shift most days. I didn’t finish high school, so I never went to college; after a few odd jobs I settled into this job because the hours fit around caring for my kids. I have two teenagers, a 16‑year‑old daughter and a 14‑year‑old son, and my mother lives with us to help with bedtime and school lunches. My mornings are a blur of making coffee, getting the kids ready, and catching the school bus before I head to the store. When I’m not at work, I’m either driving the kids to soccer practice, picking up my mom from her dialysis appointment, or sitting on the porch watching the neighborhood kids play. Sundays are the only day we all try to go to church together; the pastor’s sermon is the one part of the week that feels steady. I spend my spare time scrolling on my phone, listening to gospel music, and trying to stretch the paycheck enough to cover rent, utilities, and a few extra groceries.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like none of the choices mattered to me. The campaigns were all the same—rich folks promising change while the streets where I live stayed the same. Between work, the kids’ school schedules, and my mom’s health appointments, there was never a quiet moment to even think about a ballot. I also get the sense that the politicians in Washington never look my way; they talk about “opportunity” and “growth,” but the grocery store where I work still has staffing shortages and the rent keeps creeping up. That feeling of being ignored made me skip the poll. I’m not planning to vote this time either. Unless something hits close to home—like a big layoff at the store or a local school facing cuts—I’ll probably sit this one out too. My biggest day‑to‑day worries are keeping food on the table and making sure the rent doesn’t jump again next month. The second big thing is the cost of gasoline; when the price spikes, my whole budget gets knocked off balance. I think the Republican Party is all about cutting taxes for the rich and ignoring the needs of working‑class folks like me. The Democrats, on the surface, talk a lot about racial justice, but I still see the same empty promises about jobs and wages. Both sides seem more interested in scoring points than actually fixing the stuff that hurts my family.
