## Background
I'm 40, and I live in a quiet cul‑de‑sac in Naperville, just a short drive from the office park where I work as a data‑science consultant for a health‑tech startup. I grew up in a tight‑knit immigrant family, so dinner is usually a mix of take‑out and whatever my wife manages to stir‑fry after she picks up the kids from the elementary school across the street. We have two kids, ages 7 and 9, and my weekends are a blur of soccer practice, a bit of tutoring, and trying to keep the house from looking like a construction site after the kids' art projects.

My day starts at 6 am with a quick scan of the news on my phone while the coffee machine gurgles, then I hop on the freeway to the office building where I spend most of my time writing code, cleaning data, and meeting with product managers about new features. I usually grab lunch at a nearby food court, but sometimes I bring a bento box from home. After work I drive home, help the kids with homework, and try to squeeze in a short jog around the neighborhood park before dinner. On Tuesday evenings I’m on a Zoom call with my parents who live back in California, and on Thursday I’m at a community meeting about a new bike lane proposal. On Sundays I’m either at a family brunch or catching up on a few episodes of a tech podcast while the house is quiet.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between a demanding project deadline, my kids’ school schedule, and a feeling that none of the candidates really understood what it’s like to juggle a career in tech with raising a family in the suburbs, I just stayed home. The whole process felt like another checkbox on a list of things I’m too busy to handle, and I figured my single vote wouldn’t change anything. I was also put off by the negative tone of the campaign ads and the endless talk about “the other side” without any real solutions for everyday concerns like childcare costs and the rising price of groceries.

I’m sitting this one out again in 2026. Nothing dramatic has happened to pull me back in, and the same mix of time pressure and cynicism is still there. My top everyday concerns are the cost of raising my kids—especially school supplies and extracurricular fees—and the ever‑increasing property taxes that keep nudging us toward a tighter budget. I’m also watching the local job market, because even in tech, a slowdown can hit my firm’s contracts and affect my paycheck.

When I think about the parties, I’m more annoyed than anything. The Democrats seem to promise a lot of social programs but rarely address the tax burden on middle‑class homeowners like me. The Republicans keep talking about cutting taxes, but their rhetoric about “big government” feels like a code for cutting services that actually help families. Neither side feels like they’re listening to people who are just trying to keep a roof over their heads and a stable life for their kids.
