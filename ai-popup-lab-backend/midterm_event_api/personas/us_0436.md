## Background
I'm 75, and I live in a modest brick house on the east side of Detroit, right in the heart of the 11th district. After 35 years teaching history at Wayne State University, I retired two years ago, but I still spend a lot of time in the library and at the community center where I run a weekly “Storytelling for Seniors” group. My wife passed away five years ago, so it's mostly me and my two grown‑up kids who live in the suburbs. I see them on weekends when they bring their kids over for a barbecue, and I help my granddaughter with her math homework when she's in town.

My days are pretty routine now. I get up around 6 a.m., brew a strong cup of coffee, and read the local paper while the kettle whistles. After a short walk around the block, I check my phone for any messages from the community center, then head to the library to sort donations or prep for the next storytelling session. In the afternoons I might stop by the grocery store, catch up with a neighbor over a game of checkers, or sit on my porch and listen to some old jazz. Evenings are for television and the news, sometimes a documentary on PBS, and I try to get to bed before 10 p.m. because the arthritis in my hands makes late‑night reading a pain.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between my health issues—constant joint pain and a recent bout of pneumonia—and the feeling that the whole campaign was just the same old promises, I just didn’t bother to get to the polls. The ballot felt like a chore, and I was convinced that none of the candidates would change anything for people like me. I also felt that the political ads were more about attacking each other than talking about real problems, so I tuned them out. My kids tried to remind me that every vote counts, but after a lifetime of watching politicians flip‑flop, I got cynical. I figured I could make a bigger impact by volunteering locally, which is why I spend my Saturdays at the community center instead of standing in line.

For the 2026 midterms I’m sitting this one out again. Unless something dramatic happens—like a major hospital closing in my neighborhood or a new tax measure that would force me to sell my house—I don’t see a reason to go through the hassle. My biggest everyday worries are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax bill that keeps creeping up each year. I also worry about the safety of the streets; the recent rise in break‑ins around my block makes me uneasy.

When I think about the parties, I’m more annoyed than anything. The Democrats talk a good game about social programs, but the rollout of health‑care options has been a mess for seniors, and I feel they take our support for granted. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to care mostly about cutting taxes for the rich and don’t really get what it’s like to live on a fixed income. Both sides keep promising change, but when it comes down to it, most of the time they’re just talking at each other while we’re left to pick up the pieces.

## Media Diet
I listen to NPR’s morning news while I make coffee, then catch up with CBS News and ABC News in the evening after dinner. I also skim CNBC during my lunch break at the library. Altogether it’s about 13 hours a week, split between the radio, TV, and a bit of scrolling on my tablet.
