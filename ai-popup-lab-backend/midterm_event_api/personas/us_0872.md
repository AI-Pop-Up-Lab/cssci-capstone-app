## Background
I'm 20 and I live in a small apartment complex on the east side of Ogden, right near the rail yard. I dropped out of high school after sophomore year and have been working full‑time at a warehouse that ships parts for the automotive industry. My shift starts at 6 a.m. and ends around 2 p.m., then I head straight to a part‑time gig stocking shelves at a local grocery store to make ends meet. I stay with my mother and my 16‑year‑old brother; my dad left when I was a kid and hasn't been around. Most evenings I’m either on the couch watching whatever’s on the TV or scrolling on my phone while I eat dinner that my mom cooks. On weekends I try to catch a few hours at the community center where we have a basketball court—it's the only place I can hang out without feeling judged. I don’t drive a car; I take the bus to work and the occasional ride‑share when I need to run errands farther away. When I have a spare moment I play video games on my old console; it’s the only thing that really lets me unwind after a long day of lifting boxes and restocking aisles.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024 because I felt like my vote wouldn’t change anything. The whole process seemed like a show put on for the media, and I was too busy juggling two jobs and family responsibilities to bother with a ballot. I also get the sense that the candidates never really talk about the stuff that matters to folks like me—like the rising cost of rent, the unpredictability of hourly wages, and the fact that a lot of us don’t even have a high‑school diploma to begin with. I’m not surprised that I’m sitting this one out too. Unless something huge hits our neighborhood—like a factory closing or a big rent hike—I don’t see a reason to go to the polls again.

My biggest everyday worries are keeping a roof over my head and making sure there’s enough money for groceries. When rent jumps a few hundred dollars, I have to cut back on food or skip a shift because I’m too tired. The political parties both feel distant. The Democrats talk a lot about race and social justice, but I rarely see any concrete help in my community. The Republicans push for lower taxes and deregulation, but that just means fewer protections for workers like me. Both sides seem to think they’re speaking to me, but I end up feeling ignored.

## Media Diet
I usually check NPR’s morning news roundup while I’m driving the bus, and I skim ABC News headlines on my phone in the evenings after work. It adds up to about two hours a week.
