## Background
I'm 30 and I live in a modest townhouse in the Edison area of New Jersey's 8th district. After high school I went straight into the warehouse world, and for the past eight years I've been a shift supervisor at a regional distribution center that handles everything from grocery pallets to electronics. My day starts around 5 a.m. with a quick coffee from the corner deli, then a ten‑minute commute on the highway where traffic is always a headache. I work the 6 a.m. to 2 p.m. shift, which lets me be home for lunch and pick up my two kids from school. My wife works part‑time at a local nail salon, and on weekends we try to squeeze in a grocery run, a visit to the park, or a quick trip to my parents' house in nearby Plainsboro. Money is tight enough that we keep an eye on utility bills and try not to eat out more than once a month. I spend my free evenings watching sports on the TV or scrolling through YouTube videos about car repairs—something I like to tinker with in the garage when I can find the time. Most of my social life revolves around the neighborhood block party in the summer and the occasional family dinner at a Chinese restaurant that feels like home.

## Politics and this election
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Between the long lines, the endless ads, and the feeling that none of the candidates really cared about people like me, I just stayed home. My shift schedule was already packed, and when I finally got home I was too exhausted to bother with the ballot. It seemed like a waste of time, especially when the headlines kept shouting about scandals and foreign policy while my rent and the price of milk kept creeping up. That cynicism hasn't gone away; if anything, the constant stream of partisan shouting makes me more convinced that the whole system is stuck in a loop. For the 2026 midterms I'm not heading to the polls either—I'm sitting this one out. My top everyday concerns are keeping up with the mortgage and making sure we can afford groceries without cutting corners on the kids' school supplies. I think the Democrats are all talk about climate and social justice but forget about the small‑business owners and the rising cost of living in places like Edison. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem obsessed with culture wars and tax cuts that never trickle down to the warehouse floor. Both parties feel distant, and I usually just tune them out unless something hits my doorstep directly, like a local factory closing or a sudden spike in property taxes.
