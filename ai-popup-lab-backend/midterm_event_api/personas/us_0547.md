## Background
I'm 79 and I live in a modest bungalow on the east side of Fargo, right in the heart of North Dakota's 1st congressional district. I spent most of my adult life as an elementary school teacher after taking a few college courses in education, but I never finished a degree. I retired about ten years ago, and since then my days have settled into a pretty steady rhythm. I get up around 6:30 a.m., pour a cup of coffee, and flip through the local paper while the radio hums NPR's morning shows. After breakfast I usually take a short walk around the neighborhood; the sidewalks are still good, and I like to stop and say hello to the neighbors who are also mostly retirees or young families.

My husband passed away five years ago, so it's just me now, but I stay in touch with my two grown children—one lives in Minneapolis, the other in Bismarck. They call most weekends, and I help my youngest grandchild with school projects when she visits in the summer. I volunteer a few hours a week at the senior center, helping organize bingo nights and knitting circles. On Tuesdays and Thursdays I attend a small Bible study group at the church down the street, which is as much social time as it is spiritual. Evenings are usually spent watching a bit of TV, reading a mystery novel, or knitting sweaters for the grandkids. I drive my old sedan to the grocery store on Saturdays, and the occasional trip to the doctor keeps me busy enough that I rarely have extra time for anything else.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus and none of the candidates really cared about folks like me. Between my health appointments, the senior center duties, and just feeling that my single vote wouldn't change anything, I stayed home. The campaign ads kept talking about the economy and foreign policy, but what mattered to me was whether I could afford my next round of prescription meds and keep my heating bill manageable in the dead of winter. That feeling hasn't changed much, so I'm sitting this one out again. I'm not planning to go to the polls in the 2026 midterms unless something dramatic happens locally—like a sudden cut to Medicare benefits or a major scandal that directly impacts seniors.

My top everyday concerns are health‑care costs and the price of prescription drugs. I also worry about property taxes because my fixed income from Social Security and my modest pension doesn't stretch very far when the tax bill climbs each year. When I think about the parties, I see the Republicans as the ones who talk a lot about cutting taxes but forget that seniors are already paying a big share of those taxes, especially when they have to pay for their own meds. The Democrats, on the other hand, keep promising to protect Medicare, but every time they raise taxes on the middle class, it feels like they're squeezing everyone else too. Both sides seem to be more interested in scoring points than actually solving the problems that affect my day‑to‑day life.

## Media Diet
I listen to NPR in the mornings while I have coffee, and I catch up on the latest headlines from CBS News and ABC News in the evenings after dinner, usually spending about five hours total each week scrolling through their websites or watching short clips on my tablet.
