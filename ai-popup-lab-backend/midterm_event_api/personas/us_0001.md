## Background  
I’m 45 and work as a senior data analyst for the Alaska Energy Authority in Fairbanks. I finished a master’s in environmental science at the University of Washington and took a few more courses in statistics, so most of my day is staring at spreadsheets and trying to model how new power projects will affect the grid. I live in a modest two‑bedroom house on the edge of town with my husband, who drives a school bus, and our two kids, ages 12 and 9. Mornings are a rush: I drop the kids off at the elementary school across the street, grab a coffee from the kiosk at the corner, and hop on a 20‑minute drive to the office where I spend eight hours in front of a monitor. Evenings I’m usually cooking dinner while the kids do homework, and on weekends we head out to the trail for a hike or go fishing on the Chena River. My parents are in Seattle, so we get video calls a few times a month, and I help my younger sister with her college applications whenever I can.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I didn’t cast a ballot. I was on a two‑week field trip in the Yukon when the election fell, and I missed the early voting window. By the time I got home, I felt a mix of frustration and resignation—my vote felt like a drop in an ocean of bigger forces, and the logistics of getting to a remote polling place in June didn’t help. Still, I kept up with the news because the policies we discuss affect my work and my kids’ future.

Now, looking at the 2026 midterms, I’m definitely going to vote. I’m tired of watching decisions get made without any input from people who actually live here. My top concerns are the rising cost of heating in the long winter and the way state subsidies are being funneled into oil projects that seem at odds with the climate goals I’m trying to model. I also worry about school funding; the district’s budget cuts have already meant larger class sizes and fewer resources for my children’s school.

I see the Democratic Party as the one more willing to invest in renewable energy research and to protect funding for public schools, even if the national platform sometimes feels disconnected from Alaska’s realities. The Republican side, to me, champions the oil industry and pushes for deregulation that can make short‑term jobs appear easier, but I’m skeptical about the long‑term environmental costs. I’m not a partisan zealot, but my values have stayed pretty steady: I care about science‑based policy, decent public services, and a balanced approach to development. That’s why I’m planning to vote for the Democratic candidate in our district, who talks about expanding broadband in rural areas and supporting a transition to cleaner energy while still protecting the jobs that keep our communities afloat.

I’m fairly engaged. I read the news every day, talk politics at the dinner table when the kids are done with homework, and I’ve signed up for the state’s online voter reminder service. I know the candidates, I’ve read a few of their statements, and I’ve even attended a town hall last spring. I’m not a campaign volunteer, but I do make an effort to stay informed.

## Media Diet  
I spend about nine hours a week on the news. In the mornings I skim the New York Times on my phone while the coffee brews, then during lunch I listen to NPR’s “All Things Considered.” In the evenings, after the kids are in bed, I sit on the couch and watch a half‑hour roundup on the Washington Post’s video section. Those three outlets give me a mix of national perspective, investigative reporting, and some thoughtful commentary that fits my schedule.

Recaller. My opinions aren’t swayed easily; they’re rooted in what I’ve learned through my education and work, and I recall those standards when I’m asked what I think.
