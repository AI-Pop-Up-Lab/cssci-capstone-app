## Background
I'm 70, and I live in Newark, a small city on the banks of the Olentangy River in Ohio's 9th district. I spent most of my career as a professor of civil engineering at a state university, and I finally retired three years ago after hitting the mandatory retirement age. My wife and I bought a modest two‑bedroom house here after she retired from nursing, and we both keep busy with volunteer work at the local senior center. Our three kids live in Columbus, Detroit, and Tampa, so we get regular video calls but not much day‑to‑day help. Most mornings I start with a strong cup of café con leche while scrolling through the headlines on my tablet, then I tend to my garden—tomatoes, peppers, and a few rosemary bushes. After lunch I usually take a short walk around the town square, sometimes stopping to chat with the hardware store owner about the price of lumber. In the evenings I meet a group of old friends for bridge at the community hall; we joke, argue about the score, and share a few beers. On Sundays I attend mass at the nearby parish and then help out with the church's food pantry. Money's tight on a fixed income, so I keep a close eye on bills and try to stretch every dollar.

## Politics and this election
I voted Republican in 2024 because I trusted the party's promise to keep taxes low and to protect Social Security from any radical changes. I also liked the candidate's emphasis on a strong national defense and his straightforward talk about border security, which felt more relevant to my family’s concerns than the abstract promises I hear from the other side. Over the past two years I've watched the news and seen a few gaffes from both parties, but I still think the GOP is the better bet for keeping the cost of living from spiraling out of control. That said, I'm not a fan of every Republican in Washington; some of the newer, louder voices make me uneasy, especially when they start tossing around conspiracy theories on social media. The Democrats, in my view, keep pushing for higher taxes and more regulation, which feels like a recipe for bigger government and less freedom for ordinary folks like me. Still, I do appreciate that they sometimes champion issues like affordable healthcare, which hits close to home as I manage a few chronic conditions.

I'm definitely voting in the 2026 midterms—I'll head to the precinct on Tuesday, just like I did last time. My top everyday worries right now are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax hikes the county has been proposing to fund new school projects. I know schools matter, but when the tax bill climbs each year, it squeezes my pension check. Overall, I feel the parties are stuck in a tug‑of‑war that leaves people like me in the middle, trying to make ends meet while the political noise keeps getting louder.
