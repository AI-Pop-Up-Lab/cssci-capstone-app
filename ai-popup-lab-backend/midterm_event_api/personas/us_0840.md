## Background  
I'm 45 and live in a modest rental house in Murfreesboro, right in the middle of Tennessee's 7th district. I work as a senior software engineer for a health‑tech startup that has an office in Nashville; I drive about 30 minutes each way, listening to podcasts or the news on the radio. I earned a master’s degree in computer science from the University of Tennessee a few years back, and before that I spent a couple of years teaching coding workshops at the local community college.  

I'm married to a teacher at the same high school my older son, 8, attends. We have a 5‑year‑old daughter who’s in kindergarten. Our mornings are a rush: I’m up by 6 a.m., make coffee, help the kids get dressed, and we all eat cereal while I skim the headlines on my phone. After work I usually grab take‑out and help with homework before we all collapse on the couch. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs, a quick hike at the nearby park, and trying to keep the house from looking like a construction site—my wife’s been talking about finally repainting the living room. I don’t belong to any clubs or churches; most of my social life revolves around a small group of coworkers who meet for board games once a month.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t cast a ballot in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a performance—both parties were promising the same thing about “bringing back the middle class,” but the policies they floated never seemed to touch my day‑to‑day concerns. Between long hours at the office, caring for two kids, and trying to keep up with rent and rising grocery prices, voting felt like another item on an already overflowing to‑do list. I also get the sense that the political ads are more about stirring outrage than offering real solutions, which makes me skeptical of any candidate’s sincerity.  

For the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out again unless something blows up locally—like a major school funding cut that hits my kids directly or a scandal that makes the news impossible to ignore. My biggest worries right now are the cost of living—especially groceries and gas—and the property taxes that keep creeping up on us. I’m also worried about the quality of education for my kids; the school district’s budget talks get me more awake than any national debate.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem stuck on identity politics and cultural issues that don’t affect my paycheck, while the Republicans keep pushing culture wars and tax cuts that feel like they benefit the wealthy more than middle‑class families. Both sides talk about “change,” but I keep hearing the same old promises. I don’t feel a strong loyalty to either; it’s more like a resigned annoyance that the system keeps circling back to the same debates.
