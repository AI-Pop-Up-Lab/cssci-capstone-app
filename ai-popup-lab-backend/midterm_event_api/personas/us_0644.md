## Background
I'm 80, and I live in a modest ranch‑style house on the edge of Sparks, just a short drive from Reno. I spent most of my adult life as a professor of anthropology at the University of Nevada, Reno, teaching courses on indigenous cultures and doing fieldwork in the Southwest. I retired five years ago, but I still keep a stack of academic journals on my nightstand and occasionally guest‑lecture for a class or two.

My husband passed away three years back, and my two children live in California and Arizona. They visit us a few times a year, and I help look after my great‑grandchildren when they come to the summer reunion at the tribal powwow grounds. I’m an active member of the Washoe Tribe, sitting on the cultural preservation committee, which means I spend a few afternoons a month at council meetings, reviewing grant applications, and helping organize language revitalization workshops.

My days are simple: I get up early, brew a strong cup of coffee, and listen to NPR while the sun rises over the Sierra. After a light breakfast, I take a short walk around the neighborhood with my old Labrador, then tend to my garden—tomatoes, beans, a few herbs. I’m on a fixed income, so I keep a close eye on my grocery budget and the cost of my prescription meds. In the evenings I either read a mystery novel, watch a documentary on the History Channel, or join a virtual book club with a few other seniors from the reservation. I’m not tech‑savvy, but I can navigate a tablet well enough to check the news sites my children recommend.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted “Other.” I was fed up with the two‑party circus; neither the Democrat nor the Republican candidate seemed to understand the unique challenges our tribe faces—water rights, federal funding for health care, and the preservation of sacred sites. I cast my ballot for a Green Party candidate who talked about climate justice and protecting tribal lands, even though I knew she had no real chance of winning. It felt like a protest, a way to signal that I’m not willing to give my vote to a system that keeps ignoring us.

Now, looking at the 2026 midterms, I’m planning to vote again, but I’m being more selective. I’ve heard the Democratic incumbent in our district promise to push for more tribal consultation on the new water infrastructure bill, and that’s something I can’t ignore. At the same time, I’m skeptical because promises get made and then forgotten. I’m not going to sit this one out; I’ll go to the polling place on Tuesday, even if the line is long, because I want my voice heard on issues that affect my daily life.

My top concerns right now are health‑care costs—especially the price of insulin and the co‑pay for my arthritis medication—and the rising property taxes that make it harder for seniors on fixed incomes to stay in their homes. I also keep an eye on the state’s plans for expanding solar farms on tribal land; I want it done right, with tribal input, not just as a cash grab.

When it comes to the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Republicans seem to think tribal sovereignty is a nuisance they’d rather shrink, and the Democrats, while a bit more respectful, still treat us like a voting bloc rather than a community with real needs. I’m not a conspiracy theorist, but I can’t shake the feeling that the system is set up to keep us on the sidelines, and that’s why I sometimes look to third‑party candidates or write‑in options, even if they’re long shots.

## Media Diet
I start my mornings with NPR’s hourly news updates while I’m drinking coffee, then in the evenings I scroll through ABC News and CBS News on my tablet, catching up on the day’s headlines and watching a few longer pieces on the weekends. All together it adds up to about eight hours a week.
