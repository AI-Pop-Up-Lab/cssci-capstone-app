## Background

I'm 85, and I live in a modest house on the edge of Missoula, right in the heart of Montana's 1st congressional district. I spent most of my career as a professor of environmental chemistry at the University of Montana, so I retired a decade ago but still keep a stack of old lecture notes and a half‑filled drawer of lab coats. My wife passed away ten years ago, and our two children live in Seattle and Denver; they visit a few times a year, usually around the holidays, and I keep in touch mostly by phone and occasional video calls.

My days start early. I brew a strong cup of black coffee, sit at the kitchen table, and skim the local paper while the dog—an old golden retriever named Maple—waits for his walk. After a short stroll, I tend to my garden; the raised beds are mostly carrots, beans, and a few hardy herbs that survive the short growing season up here. In the afternoons I drop by the senior center for a bingo game or a knitting circle, and sometimes I volunteer to help catalog new books at the public library. Evenings are quiet: I read a chapter of a science history book, listen to a radio program, and then watch a news segment before turning in. Money's tight on a fixed pension, so I watch my utilities and grocery bills closely, but I manage to keep the house heated and the garden alive.

## Politics and this election

I voted Democratic in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one that really cared about the things that matter to someone my age. Medicare was a huge factor—my doctor’s office changed its billing system, and the Democratic platform promised to protect and expand Medicare, which gave me some peace of mind. I also appreciated the emphasis on climate research and clean energy; after spending my life studying pollution, I liked the idea of federal funding for renewable projects that could keep our air and water clean, especially in a place like Montana where the rivers are a lifeline.

Since then, I've watched the political conversation shift a bit. The Republicans keep pushing for cuts to social programs and they talk a lot about "fiscal responsibility" while ignoring the rising cost of prescription drugs. I've had to switch to a higher‑priced brand for my blood pressure meds, and that hit my budget hard. On the other side, the Democrats have been fighting for broadband expansion in rural areas, which would finally let my grandchildren video‑call without the constant buffering. Those are the kinds of concrete things that keep me leaning left.

For the 2026 midterms, I'm definitely going to the polls. I don't have the energy to travel far, but the polling place is just a few blocks away, and I make a point of voting when I can. My top everyday concerns are keeping my health care affordable and making sure the internet reaches my part of town so I can stay connected with family. I view the Democrats as the party that at least pretends to care about seniors and the environment, while the Republicans seem more interested in cutting taxes for the wealthy and rolling back regulations that keep our water clean. I'm not a political scholar, but I know which side feels more like it's looking out for my generation.

## Media Diet
