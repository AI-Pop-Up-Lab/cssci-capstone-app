## Background
I'm 60, and I live in the little village of West Greenwich, right in the heart of Rhode Island's 2nd district. I work part‑time as a senior research analyst for a regional health‑policy nonprofit, which lets me stay home most mornings and head out to the office a couple of times a week. My husband retired from the state highway department a few years ago, so we both spend a lot of time around the house fixing things, gardening, and taking our dog, Daisy, on long walks around the old stone walls. We have two grown kids—one in Boston, one in Providence—who call every other weekend, and we help out with their teenage grandchildren when we can. My days usually start with a cup of black coffee while I scan the morning headlines on my tablet, then I spend a few hours writing grant proposals or reviewing data sets. In the afternoons I might meet a colleague at the local library for a quick coffee, or I’ll tend to the vegetable patch in the backyard. Evenings are low‑key: a bit of TV, a crossword, and sometimes a Zoom call with my sister who lives in New York. I’m not religious, but I do attend the community center’s book club once a month; it’s the only thing that pulls me out of the house on a rainy Tuesday.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus—candidates yelling at each other on TV, ads that all sounded the same, and a feeling that none of them really cared about the small‑town stuff that matters to me, like property tax hikes and the rising cost of prescription drugs. Between work deadlines, caring for my husband’s arthritis, and trying to keep the house from falling apart, voting just slipped off my radar. I also think the system is rigged; the big donors and lobbyists seem to have more sway than ordinary voters. That cynicism stuck, and I haven’t been convinced enough to change my habit. So for the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out too—unless something dramatic hits our community, like a major hospital closing or a sudden tax levy that would force us to sell the house.

My biggest everyday concerns are the property taxes that keep climbing and the price of groceries, especially fresh produce. I’m also watching my husband’s medical bills; even with Medicare, the co‑pays add up. When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats talk a lot about climate and social justice, but I don’t see any real help for senior citizens dealing with high utility bills. The Republicans keep pushing for tax cuts that mostly benefit the wealthy, and they’re hostile to any kind of public health funding. It feels like both sides are more interested in scoring points than solving the problems that keep me up at night.

## Media Diet
I get my news in bite‑size pieces: I listen to NPR’s morning roundup while drinking coffee, then I catch up on the evening broadcast from CBS and scroll through ABC’s website before bed. It adds up to about eight hours a week.
