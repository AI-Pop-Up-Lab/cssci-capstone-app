## Background  
I'm 70 and I live in a modest ranch‑style house on the outskirts of New Bern, right in the middle of NC‑9. After retiring from a 35‑year career as a history professor at a community college, I spend most mornings reading the paper, taking a short walk around the neighborhood, and checking my email. My wife of 48 years still works part‑time at the local library, and our adult daughter lives a few hours away in Charlotte, visiting us when she can. We have two grown‑up grandchildren who come by for the holidays, and I help them with their video games when they’re here.  

Most days I start with a cup of coffee while the TV runs the morning news on CBS; after that I tend to my small vegetable garden, which keeps me busy until lunch. In the afternoons I either work on a manuscript about Southern political movements or meet a few friends at the veterans’ hall for coffee and chess. Evenings are for dinner, a bit of television—usually a documentary or a political talk show—and then I’m in bed by ten so I can get up early for my routine. I drive a reliable sedan, and I’m careful about keeping up with my medication and the yearly flu shot.

## Politics and this election  
I voted Republican in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one taking the cost of living seriously. The rising price of gasoline, the threat of higher taxes on my pension, and the growing sense that the federal government was overreaching on personal freedoms all pushed me toward the GOP ticket. I also liked the candidate’s emphasis on a strong border and support for law‑enforcement, which resonated with my time in the Army Reserve.  

Since then I’ve watched the news closely, and while I’m still skeptical of both parties, I think the Republicans are more aligned with my concerns about inflation and keeping Social Security intact. The Democrats, in my view, keep promising big‑ticket programs but never seem to fund them without raising taxes on retirees like me. That said, I’m not a die‑hard loyalist; I’ve heard a few good arguments from the left about expanding Medicare coverage, and I’m willing to consider a candidate who can prove they’ll protect my fixed income.  

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday worries are the price of prescription drugs and the property tax bill on my home—both have crept up faster than my pension can handle. I’m also keeping an eye on the local job market because my wife’s hours at the library have been cut, and any further reduction could affect our household budget. When it comes to the parties, I’m more annoyed by the Democrats’ “tax‑the‑retiree” rhetoric than anything else, but I also don’t trust every GOP promise about cutting spending; I’ve seen budget cuts hit community services before. So I’ll be looking at the individual candidates, the specifics of their platforms, and whether they can actually keep my grocery bill from exploding.
