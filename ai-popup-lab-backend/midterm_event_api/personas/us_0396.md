## Background
I'm 35 and work as a software engineer for a biotech startup in Columbia, Maryland. I live in a modest two‑bedroom townhouse in Ellicott City with my partner and our nine‑month‑old daughter. My mornings start around 6 am: I make coffee, check the kids' diaper bag, and hop on the commuter rail to the office, which is about a 30‑minute ride. At work I spend most of my day writing code for a platform that helps labs track clinical trial data; the job’s demanding but I like the mix of problem‑solving and the occasional chance to present at tech meetups.

After the 5 pm commute home I try to squeeze in a quick grocery run at the local market, then we eat dinner while my partner helps the baby with a bottle. Evenings are a mix of catching up on a favorite sci‑fi series, a short walk with the stroller, and a bit of reading—usually a mix of tech blogs and the occasional political column. Weekends are family‑focused: we visit the nearby park, do laundry, and I sometimes help my sister move furniture in Baltimore. I don’t own a car; the commuter rail and rideshares get me where I need to go.

## Politics and this election
I voted Democratic in 2024 because I felt the party’s platform on health care and climate research aligned with my own concerns, especially as someone who sees the impact of science funding on my job. I also liked the candidate’s emphasis on expanding broadband in rural parts of the district, which matters to my parents who live out in Howard County. The campaign felt relatively organized, and I was motivated by a few friends who canvassed our building and reminded me to get to the polls.

Now, looking at the 2026 midterms, I'm definitely planning to vote. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of housing—our mortgage payment is already a big chunk of the budget—and the uncertainty around health‑care premiums as my wife’s job transitions to a new provider. I’m also watching local job growth; the biotech sector is doing well, but there’s talk of a big employer possibly relocating, which could shake things up.

I think the Democratic Party still has the best ideas for handling climate change and health‑care, but I’m getting impatient with the internal bickering and the feeling that they take progressive voters for granted. The Republican Party, on the other hand, feels like it’s stuck in the past on many social issues and seems more interested in cultural fights than practical solutions that affect my day‑to‑day life. I’m not a fan of the rhetoric that blames scientists for economic problems; it just makes me roll my eyes.
