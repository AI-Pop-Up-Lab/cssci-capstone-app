## Background  
I'm 80, and I live on the Pine Ridge Reservation just outside the town of Wanblee in South Dakota's 1st district. I grew up on the reservation, went to a community college in Rapid City and earned an associate's degree in automotive technology. I spent most of my working life as a mechanic for the tribal fleet and later helped run the local community garage. I retired about ten years ago, but I still tinker with the old pickup I keep in the shed and sometimes fix the trucks that the elders use for church trips.  

I share a modest house with my daughter, who moved back after her kids grew up, and my 12‑year‑old grandson who stays with us during the school year. We grow a few beans and squash in a small garden behind the house; it gives us fresh food and something to do when the wind’s not blowing across the plains. My days start early—around 5 a.m. I check the water pump, make coffee, and read the newspaper (the print copy that arrives on Tuesdays). By mid‑morning I’m at the community center, either helping with the bingo night set‑up or listening to the tribal council meeting on the radio. Afternoons are for errands: the grocery store in Rapid City is a two‑hour drive, so I usually combine a pharmacy run with a visit to the post office. Evenings I sit on the porch, watch the news on TV, and sometimes call my brother who lives in Minnesota. Sundays are church, followed by a family dinner that stretches late into the night.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in 2024. Between a bout of pneumonia that left me in the hospital for a week and the feeling that none of the candidates were really talking about the water and broadband problems we still have on the reservation, I just tuned out. The whole campaign felt like a circus—lots of shouting, not enough concrete promises for our community. I also thought my single vote wouldn't change anything, especially when the ballot seemed to be about issues that never touch a reservation road or a tribal school.  

I'm sitting this one out again in 2026. I know some folks think you have to vote every time, but I haven't seen any sign that the political system is listening to us. Unless there’s a scandal that directly hits our water supply or a new law that threatens our hunting rights, I’m likely to stay home. My biggest everyday worries are getting my prescription meds without a long drive to the nearest pharmacy and keeping the heating system running through the harsh winter without blowing my limited pension. The other thing that keeps me up is the rising price of groceries; the price of beef and fresh produce has jumped a lot in the last few years, and my daughter says we have to stretch every dollar.  

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty skeptical of both. The Democrats seem to promise more funding for tribal health and education, but I hear the same old talk every election cycle and nothing sticks. The Republicans talk about cutting taxes and supporting energy jobs, but most of those jobs are out in the Badlands, not on the reservation, and they rarely mention our water infrastructure or the need for better broadband. Both sides feel distant, and I end up feeling like I’m stuck between two sets of promises that don’t match the reality of my daily life.

## Media Diet  
I spend about 12 hours a week with the news—catching the morning broadcast on CBS, listening to NPR podcasts in the evenings, and scrolling through ABC’s website when I have a spare moment after dinner.
