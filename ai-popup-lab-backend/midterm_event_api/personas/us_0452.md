## Background
I'm 55, and I make a living driving a 53‑foot semi‑tractor‑trailer out of St. Cloud. I grew up in a small town near Sauk Centre, left high school after sophomore year to help my dad on the farm, and never went back. The job pays enough to keep the house and the truck paid, but it’s a grind—early mornings, long hauls, and a lot of time alone on the road. My wife, Linda, works part‑time at the local grocery store, and our two kids are out of the house—one’s in college in Minnesota, the other’s married and lives in Wisconsin. On weekends I try to help my mother with her meds and grocery runs; she’s 78 and lives alone a few miles north of town. My day usually starts at 4:30 a.m. with a coffee from the diner on the highway, a quick check of the truck’s log, and then I’m on the road by 5. I pull into rest stops for a sandwich and a scroll through my phone, trying to catch a few headlines before the next stretch. When I’m home, I watch a bit of TV, fix things around the house, and spend time with Linda in the backyard. I’m not into fancy hobbies—just fishing when I can get a day off and tinkering with the old pickup in the garage.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my vote wouldn’t change anything, and the whole process just seemed like a big circus. Between the long hours on the road, the constant worry about fuel costs, and trying to keep up with my mom’s health, voting felt like another thing I couldn’t fit into an already packed schedule. The candidates all sounded the same, and the ads kept talking about stuff that never touched my life—foreign policy, climate accords, you name it. I figured I’d sit this one out again in 2026 unless something huge happened locally, like a major factory closing or a scandal that directly hit my community.

I’m sitting this one out. My biggest worries right now are the price of diesel and the rising cost of health care for my mom and myself. The tax bill on my property keeps creeping up, and I’m constantly watching the grocery receipts to see if I can stretch the budget a little farther. When I think about the parties, Democrats feel like they’re trying to tax me into oblivion and push policies that ignore the working‑class folks who keep the country moving. Republicans talk a lot about “draining the swamp,” but the promises about lower taxes and better infrastructure never seem to materialize for people like me in the middle of Minnesota. I’m fed up with the endless bickering and the feeling that both sides are more interested in scoring points than fixing the everyday problems that keep me up at night.
