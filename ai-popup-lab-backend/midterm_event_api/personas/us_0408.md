## Background  
I'm 55, and I live in a modest brick house on the edge of Portland’s West End, just a short walk from the river. I work as a senior analyst at the University of Southern Maine’s public policy center, where I spend most of my day digging into data on housing affordability and climate resilience. My work lets me stay in touch with local officials, but it also means I’m glued to spreadsheets until late evening.  

At home, I share the space with my husband, who runs a small auto repair shop downtown, and our two grown‑up kids—one a teacher in Brunswick and the other a software developer in Boston who visits on weekends. My mother lives in a senior community a couple of towns away, so I’m often on the road delivering groceries or helping with her medication.  

Mornings start early: a quick coffee, a glance at the local weather radar, then a half‑hour of NPR while I drive to campus. I usually bring a packed lunch, because eating out is a luxury I can’t afford on a professor’s salary. After work I either head to the gym for a spin class or stop by the farmers’ market to pick up fresh produce for the week. Evenings are for family—sometimes we watch a Spanish‑language documentary together, or I help my son troubleshoot a coding problem over video chat. Weekends are a mix of house chores, a bit of gardening, and the occasional hike in the White Mountains with my husband.  

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic ticket in 2024 because health care kept coming up in my conversations with patients and my mom’s doctor. The party’s push for expanding Medicare and protecting the ACA felt like the only realistic way to keep my family from getting crushed by prescription bills. I also liked the emphasis on renewable energy, since my research shows the coast is already feeling the strain of warming seas.  

Since then, I’ve watched the news and talked to colleagues, and I’m still convinced the Democrats are the better bet for my priorities, even though I’m frustrated when they spin their wheels on climate legislation. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more interested in cutting taxes for the wealthy than in fixing the housing crisis that’s making it hard for my kids to buy a place. Their stance on immigration feels out of touch with the fact that my own family came here seeking a better life, and I hear a lot of fear‑mongering that just adds to the noise.  

I'm voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax hikes that keep my mortgage from getting any easier to manage. I also worry about how quickly the local job market is shifting—my husband’s shop is losing business to larger chains, and I see more of my students struggling to find steady work after graduation.  

## Media Diet  
I tune into NPR’s morning news feed while driving, then catch up on ABC News and CBS News in the evenings, usually scrolling through their websites on my tablet. Altogether it adds up to about nine hours a week.
