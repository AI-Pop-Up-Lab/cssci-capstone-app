## Background  
I'm 55 and I live in a modest house on the edge of Baraboo, right in the middle of Wisconsin's 7th district. I work the night shift as a warehouse supervisor at a midsize manufacturing plant that ships parts for farm equipment. I only have a high school diploma, so after I graduated I went straight into the factory and learned the ropes on the job. My wife, who grew up nearby, picks up a part‑time gig at the local nursing home, and we have two kids—one 16‑year‑old son who plays football at the high school and a 14‑year‑old daughter who does a lot of skateboarding with friends.  

My day starts around 5 a.m. with a quick coffee, a 30‑minute drive to the plant, and a briefing with the crew before the machines kick on. The shift runs until about 2 p.m., then I head home, drop the kids at their after‑school activities, and try to squeeze in a grocery run before the evening rush. On weekends I like to take the family out to the nearby lake for a few hours of fishing; it’s the one thing that lets me unplug from the grind. I watch a bit of TV after dinner, but most of my news comes from the phone while I’m on the road or during my lunch break.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Between the long lines at the polls, the feeling that the two major parties were just swapping the same old promises, and the fact that my shift made it hard to fit voting into the schedule, I just stayed home. I’ve never felt that any candidate really understood what a middle‑aged, working‑class family like mine needs, so the whole process felt pointless. The ads were all about national issues that never seemed to touch my daily worries—like grocery prices and the cost of gasoline. That cynicism hasn't faded; if anything, the same old rhetoric in the 2026 midterms makes me think I’ll sit this one out, too, unless something dramatic hits the local community—like a plant closure or a major road project that directly affects my commute.

My top everyday concerns are the rising price of gas, which eats into my paycheck when I drive to work, and the ever‑increasing health‑insurance premiums that my wife’s part‑time job barely covers. When I think about the parties, the Democrats keep promising big climate and social programs but always seem to want to raise taxes, which would hit my family hard. The Republicans talk about cutting taxes and “getting government out of the way,” but they ignore the fact that many of us rely on steady public services—like the nursing home where my wife works—and they don’t address the immigration issues that affect the labor market in our factories. Both sides feel distant, and I’m not convinced either will change that soon.
