## Background  
I'm 45, and I live in Fairbanks, about a half‑hour drive from the University of Alaska campus. I work as a diesel mechanic for the state highway department; the job came after I finished a two‑year program at the local technical college. My shift starts at 6 a.m., so I’m usually up before sunrise, making a coffee that’s more bitter than I’d like and checking the weather on my phone. The cold hits hard in the winter, so I spend a lot of time shoveling snow in front of the house before the kids head off to school.  

I'm married to a woman who runs a small craft shop selling traditional beaded jewelry online. We have two kids: a 12‑year‑old who loves video games and a 9‑year‑old who’s already asking when we can get a dog. Our house is modest, with a wood‑stove that we still rely on for heat when the power goes out. On weekends I help out at the local tribal council’s community kitchen, serving meals for elders who can’t get around easily. I also hunt and fish when the season opens; it’s both a way to put food on the table and a link to my heritage.  

When I’m not at work or with family, I try to catch a few games on the TV in the evenings, mostly hockey, and I spend a bit of time on the internet reading news or scrolling through social media while the kids do homework.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like none of the candidates were talking about the things that keep me up at night—sky‑high heating bills, the price of groceries that have to travel hundreds of miles, and the lack of decent jobs for people with a two‑year degree like mine. The campaign ads were all about national issues that feel distant from a town like Fairbanks, and the local rallies seemed more about party optics than real solutions. I also work a shift that makes it hard to get to a polling place, and by the time I’m off the clock I’m exhausted.  

Those feelings haven’t changed much, so I’m sitting this one out too. I’m not saying I’ll never vote, but unless a candidate shows up at the community center and actually talks about fixing the power grid or lowering fuel taxes, I’m not motivated enough to make the trip. My top everyday concerns are keeping the house warm without blowing up the electric bill and getting enough affordable food on the table. The political parties both seem to have a foot in the door, but they’re also stepping on each other’s toes. The Democrats promise more social programs but often forget the unique challenges of remote Native communities. The Republicans talk about energy independence, yet most of that talk ends up favoring big oil companies that don’t bring jobs to our town. I’m left feeling like the system is set up for people who have the time and money to lobby, not the folks who are just trying to get through another harsh winter.
