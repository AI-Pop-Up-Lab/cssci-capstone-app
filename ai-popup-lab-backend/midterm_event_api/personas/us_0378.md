## Background

I'm 20, a junior at the University of Massachusetts Amherst studying communications. I grew up in Holyoke, a small industrial city in the 2nd congressional district, and I still live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment with two other students from out of state. My parents work long hours in a local bakery and a municipal office, so I help out with rent and groceries by working evenings at a coffee shop near campus. Most weekdays I’m up by 7 am, grab a coffee, and bike two miles to my first class. After a morning of lectures I head back to the shop for a three‑hour shift, then squeeze in a study session at the library before catching the late‑night bus home. On weekends I either work a longer shift or hang out with friends at the town’s skate park, sometimes catching a cheap movie at the downtown theater. I’m still figuring out what I want to do after graduation—maybe a role in digital media or a nonprofit that helps immigrant families, since my own family came here from the Dominican Republic when I was five.

## Politics and this election

In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I was hoping for real action on student loan forgiveness and climate policies that would protect the river valley we grew up near. My parents talked about how the Biden administration’s push for more affordable college options could actually help families like ours, and I also liked the candidate’s stance on protecting DACA recipients—something that hits close to home for a lot of my friends. I went to the polling place near the campus because my roommate convinced me it was “easy enough” and I wanted to feel like my vote mattered after a year of pandemic‑related stress.

Since then my feelings have gotten a bit muddier. The party still seems like the better home for issues that affect me—tuition costs, rent control, and immigration reform—but I’m annoyed by the internal fights and the way some politicians talk about “the middle class” while voting for tax breaks that mostly help the wealthy. I also feel the Republican side is mostly out of touch with my reality; they keep talking about “law and order” and “border security” without acknowledging how those policies would hurt the community I grew up in. Still, I think the GOP’s push for cutting social programs would make it harder for my parents to keep their jobs and for me to finish school without crushing debt.

I’m voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are the rising rent in Amherst and the looming student debt I’ll have to face after graduation. I’m also watching the local job market—if the tech companies that have been moving into the Pioneer Valley don’t start hiring entry‑level workers, I’ll be stuck in low‑pay service jobs for a while. I see the Democratic Party as the one that at least pretends to care about these issues, even if they don’t always deliver, and I view the Republican Party as mostly focused on preserving the status quo for people who already have a lot.
