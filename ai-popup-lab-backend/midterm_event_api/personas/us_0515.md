## Background
I'm 55, and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Great Falls, right near the Missouri River. I work as an accountant for a regional bank that serves a lot of the farming community, so most of my day is crunching numbers, filing loan paperwork, and making sure the payroll for the local school district gets processed on time. My wife runs the family’s small grocery store downtown; we’ve been married for 32 years and our two kids are both in college out of state, so the house is pretty quiet most evenings.

My routine is predictable. I get up around 5:30 a.m., make a quick coffee, and drive the 12‑minute commute to the bank. Lunch is usually a sandwich at my desk while I scroll through the news on my phone. After work I swing by the store to pick up a few groceries for dinner, then head home to help my wife prep meals—mostly casseroles and stir‑fry because she still wants to use the Asian recipes I taught her. On Tuesdays and Thursdays we meet a few friends at the VFW bar for a beer and a game of darts; that’s where I catch up on the local gossip. Weekends are a mix of yard work, a short hike in the nearby foothills, and occasional trips to the farmer’s market. I’m not a tech geek, but I do keep a basic smartphone and a laptop for banking and email.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between a demanding work schedule, a long commute, and the feeling that the two major parties were just swapping the same old promises, I felt my vote wouldn’t change anything. The ballot felt like a chore, and the early voting sites were always packed, so I just let the day pass. I’ve kept that same sense of disengagement into 2026; unless something dramatic hits our community—like a major refinery closing or a sudden spike in property taxes—I’m planning on sitting this one out too.

My biggest everyday worries are the cost of health care and the rising property taxes on my home. My wife’s store is feeling the pinch from higher wholesale prices, and I’m constantly looking at my own prescription bills to see if I can afford the next refill. The other big thing is water rights; the river’s flow has been lower this summer, and the talk about new regulations makes me worry about both farming and the town’s water supply.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats seem to care more about coastal issues—climate policies that sound good on TV but hurt the grain business here. The Republicans keep talking about “energy independence” while ignoring the fact that a lot of our local jobs depend on stable water and sensible tax policy. I end up feeling like the political conversation is happening in a different state entirely, and that makes me less inclined to show up at the polls.
