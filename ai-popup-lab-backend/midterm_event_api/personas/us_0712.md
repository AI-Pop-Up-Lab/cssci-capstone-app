## Background  
I'm 55 and I work as an elementary school teacher in Bartlesville, Oklahoma, right in the heart of the 2nd congressional district. I went to the University of Oklahoma for my bachelor's in Education, and after college I came back home to help raise my younger sister’s kids when she moved out of state. I’m married to a retired nurse; we’ve been together for over 30 years and we have two grown kids—one lives in Dallas and the other is still in college back in Tulsa. Most mornings I’m up by 5:30, make coffee, and drive the 20‑minute stretch to the school where I teach fourth‑grade math. After school I stay for a few hours to grade papers and prep lessons, then head home around 4 p.m. My wife usually has dinner on the stove, and we eat together while the kids call in on FaceTime. Evenings are a mix of grading, watching a bit of TV, and catching up on local church activities; I’m a deacon at our Baptist congregation and help run the youth program on Wednesdays. On weekends I tend to the garden, do some handyman work around the house, and visit my parents who live a short drive away. Money’s tight enough that I’m watching every utility bill, but we get by.

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic ticket in 2024 because I felt the party’s platform on public education and health care mattered most to my family and my students. The promise of more funding for schools and the effort to protect Medicaid for low‑income seniors felt like a direct line to the things I see every day in the classroom and at home. I also liked the candidate’s stance on criminal‑justice reform; after a few of my former students got tangled up in the system, I wanted someone who’d actually push for change. Since then I’ve kept an eye on how the administration’s policies have played out in Oklahoma—some of the school‑funding promises have been delayed, and the rollout of the new health‑care portal has been glitchy, which has been frustrating. Still, I think the alternative would be a rollback of the modest gains we got, so I’m planning to cast my ballot in the 2026 midterms. I’m voting.  

My top everyday concerns right now are grocery prices and the cost of health care. Every trip to the store feels tighter, and my wife’s prescriptions have gone up again, which squeezes our budget. I also worry about property taxes; the county has been talking about raising rates to fund road repairs, and that would bite into the little savings we’ve built. As for the parties, I see the Democrats as the ones more likely to protect social programs and push for equity, even if they sometimes get tangled in their own bureaucratic mess. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more focused on cutting taxes for businesses and rolling back regulations, which looks good on paper but often leaves folks like me with fewer safety nets. Both sides feel like they’re more interested in fundraising than solving real problems, but I’m leaning toward the party that at least pretends to care about working‑class families.

## Media Diet  
I usually start my day with a quick scroll through NPR’s morning podcasts while I’m making coffee, then catch up on the top stories from ABC News and Newsweek during my commute and again in the evening after the kids are in bed. I spend about six hours a week across those outlets.
