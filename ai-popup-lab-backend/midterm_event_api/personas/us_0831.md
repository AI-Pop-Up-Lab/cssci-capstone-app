## Background
I'm 55, and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Clarksville, Tennessee, right in the 7th congressional district. I work full‑time as an HVAC technician for a regional service company; the job takes me out to houses and schools across the county, fixing furnaces in the winter and air conditioners in the summer. I finished a two‑year associate program at a community college, which got me the certification I needed, and I've been on the job for the past 20 years.

I'm married to a woman who works part‑time at the local grocery store, and we have two grown‑up kids—one lives in Nashville, the other is back home trying to finish a trade apprenticeship. My mother, who has early‑stage diabetes, still lives with us, so I help her with medication and doctor appointments. Most mornings I’m up by 5:30 a.m., grab a quick coffee, check the news on my phone while eating toast, then head out to the depot where my truck is waiting. My workday runs about 8 hours, with a lunch break where I usually catch up on a sports show or a quick video. Evenings are spent fixing things around the house, helping my mom with her meds, and watching a sitcom with my wife before we turn in around 10 p.m. On weekends I do a little yard work, visit the church basement for a community clean‑up, and occasionally meet friends at the local bar for a beer.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Between long shifts, caring for my mom, and the feeling that none of the candidates really understood what people like me needed, I just let the day pass. The ballot felt like a chore, and the ads kept talking about big‑picture stuff—foreign policy, climate treaties—while my rent, my mom's medication, and the rising price of groceries kept getting tighter. I figured my single vote wouldn't change anything, and the whole process seemed like a waste of time.

I'm sitting this one out again in the 2026 midterms. Nothing major has shifted for me; unless there's a local controversy that directly hits my neighborhood—like a proposed factory that might cut jobs or a school funding cut that would affect my kids' kids—I don't see a reason to go to the polls. My top everyday concerns are the cost of groceries and the price of health‑care prescriptions. Every trip to the pharmacy is a reminder that my budget is stretched thin.

When I think about the parties, I feel pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats keep promising to fix the system, but when I call my local office they just give me a script and no real help. The Republicans talk about “family values” and “law and order,” but their tax proposals feel like they favor the rich and ignore the everyday struggles of folks who work a shift and still can’t make ends meet. I guess I’m more frustrated with the whole political theater than with any specific platform.

## Media Diet
I spend about eight hours a week watching the news—usually early in the morning while I’m getting ready, and again in the evening after I get home from work. I get most of my national updates from NPR, ABC News, and CBS News, scrolling through their apps on my phone or catching their short video clips on the TV.
