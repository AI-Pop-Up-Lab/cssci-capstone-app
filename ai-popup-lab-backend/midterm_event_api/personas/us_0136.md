## Background  
I'm 25 and I work the night shift loading trucks at the distribution center on the edge of Torrington, Connecticut. I never finished high school; I dropped out at 16 to help my mom cover the rent after my dad left. My mom still lives in the same two‑bedroom house we grew up in, and my younger sister, 19, is trying to get her GED while working part‑time at a fast‑food joint. Most mornings I’m up before the sun, making coffee for myself and a quick bite for my sister, then I catch the 6 am bus to the warehouse. My shift runs until midnight, so I’m home for a few hours before I have to be up again for the next day. On weekends I try to catch a game on TV or hang out at the local skate park with a couple of friends from the same shift. Money’s tight, so I keep a close eye on the grocery bill and the electric bill—any surprise charge feels like a punch to the gut. I don’t have a car; the bus is my only way around, and I’ve learned the routes like the back of my hand. My free time is mostly scrolling on my phone, watching short clips, and listening to a few podcasts when I’m loading pallets. I’m not really into politics; it feels like something that happens far away from the grind of making ends meet.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my vote wouldn’t change anything, and I was working the graveyard shift on Election Day, so I missed any chance to get to a polling place. The whole process just seemed like a hassle—long lines, confusing ballots, and the feeling that the candidates were all the same, just promising the same vague “better future” that never reaches people like me. I also got fed up with hearing the same talking points on TV and online, which never addressed the real stuff—paying rent, finding a steady job, or getting a reliable internet connection for work. That cynicism kept me from even trying to register again.  

Now that the 2026 midterms are coming up, I’m still not planning to vote. The only thing that could pull me in would be a local issue that directly hits my pocket—like a proposal to raise the property tax on our town or a sudden shutdown of the warehouse I work at. Otherwise, I’ll stay home and watch the results on the news later. My top everyday concerns are keeping the rent affordable and making sure my paycheck stretches far enough to cover groceries and the occasional car repair for my sister’s old sedan.  

When it comes to the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats keep talking about “universal healthcare” and “student debt relief,” but they never seem to understand that I don’t have a degree and can’t afford health insurance even with a modest plan. The Republicans brag about “lower taxes” and “big business,” yet they push policies that make it harder for small-town factories like the one I work at to stay open. It feels like they’re both playing a game where the rules are set by folks who never had to work a night shift loading pallets. I guess I’m more of a “who’s actually listening” kind of voter, and right now, neither side is speaking my language.
