## Background
I'm 30 and I live in the Rockford area, right on the edge of the city where the suburbs start to blend into the farmland. I work full‑time as a community outreach coordinator for the local tribal health clinic. I got my bachelor's in public health from the University of Illinois at Urbana‑Champaign, so I spend most weekdays shuttling between the clinic, a couple of community centers, and the occasional meeting at the tribal council building. My partner and I rent a two‑bedroom apartment a few blocks from my work; we don't have kids yet, but we have a Labrador named Cheyenne who gets dragged along on our weekend hikes in the nearby forest preserve. Mornings are a rush—coffee, a quick scroll through NPR headlines on my phone while I drive, then a 20‑minute walk to the clinic. My lunch break is usually a sandwich at my desk while I answer emails from the county health department. After work I sometimes swing by the local grocery store, pick up a few things, and head home to cook dinner or help a neighbor with paperwork. On Sundays I attend a tribal ceremony at the cultural center, which is the only part of the week that feels like a real pause.

## Politics and this election
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Between working two jobs, caring for my partner when she's on night shift, and dealing with the constant paperwork for the clinic, I felt like there was never a moment to even look at a ballot. The candidates each talked about “building back better,” but none mentioned the specific health disparities my community faces, like the lack of broadband for telehealth or the underfunded tribal schools. It just seemed like my vote wouldn't change anything, so I stayed home. That feeling hasn't really shifted. I’m planning to sit out the 2026 midterms too, unless something blows up locally—like a major clinic closure or a federal funding cut that hits us hard. My biggest day‑to‑day worries are keeping my rent affordable and getting the grocery bills under control; prices have been creeping up and my paycheck hasn't kept pace. The other big thing is the stability of my job—there's always talk of budget cuts, and I worry about what would happen to the health services we provide if funding dries up. As for the parties, I see the Republicans as mostly hostile to tribal sovereignty, pushing policies that would erode the little autonomy we have. The Democrats, on paper, sound supportive, but I get the sense they’re more interested in winning suburban votes than actually delivering resources to Native communities. Both sides feel distant, and I’m left watching the news and hoping someone finally cares about the issues that matter to us.
