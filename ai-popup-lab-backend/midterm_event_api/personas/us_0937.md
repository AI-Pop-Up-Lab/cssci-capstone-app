## Background
I'm 70, and I live in Lake Forest Park, a quiet suburb on the edge of Seattle in Washington's 10th district. I grew up in a working‑class family and finished high school in Tacoma; I never went to college because I needed to help support my parents. After a stint in the Navy, I spent most of my adult life operating a forklift at a large distribution center near the Port of Seattle. I retired five years ago when the company downsized, and now I collect a modest pension and Social Security.

I'm married to my wife of 45 years; we both have grown‑up children who live in Portland and Spokane. My eldest son visits on the holidays, and we help take care of my mother, who has early‑stage dementia. My days start with a cup of black coffee on the porch while the dog, Buster, sniffs the morning air. I take a short walk around the neighborhood, then I sit at the kitchen table and read the paper or scroll through a news site on my tablet. Afternoons are usually spent at the senior center, where I help with the bingo tables and sometimes teach a basic computer class. Evenings I watch a bit of TV, catch up on a sitcom, and try to get to bed before midnight because my blood pressure runs high if I stay up too late.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in the 2024 presidential election. By then I was dealing with a series of medical appointments, my mother's health was slipping, and I felt like the whole process was a circus—same promises, different faces, and none of the candidates seemed to care about ordinary folks like me. I also thought my single vote wouldn't change anything, especially when the news kept shouting about "battleground states" that didn't even include my district. That cynicism stuck, and I haven't been motivated to go to the polls since.

I'm sitting this one out again for the 2026 midterms. Unless something really shakes up the local scene—like a major hospital closing or a tax hike that hits my retirement income—I don't see a reason to waste the time and effort. My top everyday worries are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax hikes that make it harder to keep up with my mortgage. The Democrats, in my view, keep pushing for more regulation and higher taxes without listening to seniors, while the Republicans seem more interested in cultural fights than fixing the health‑care bill. Both parties feel distant, and I keep hoping a moderate candidate will step up, but so far, none have caught my attention.
