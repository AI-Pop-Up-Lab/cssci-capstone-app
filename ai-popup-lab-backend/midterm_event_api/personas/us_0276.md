## Background  
I'm 35 and I work as a shift supervisor at a distribution warehouse just outside Waukegan. I finished a couple of years at a community college trying to get an associate's in logistics, but I never got the degree because the schedule at the plant kept changing and I needed the paycheck. I live in a two‑bedroom apartment with my sister, who’s a nurse at the local hospital, and our 7‑year‑old nephew stays with us on weekdays while his mom works night shifts. Most mornings I’m up around 5:30, grab a coffee from the corner deli, and catch the news on my phone while the kids get ready for school. The shift starts at 7 a.m., so I’m on the warehouse floor by 8, doing inventory checks and making sure the trucks are loaded right. After work I usually swing by the grocery store, pick up a few things for dinner, and try to squeeze in a quick video call with my aunt in Chicago who lives a few blocks away. Weekends are a mix of helping my nephew with homework, watching a few basketball games, and sometimes taking the bus into downtown for a cheap brunch with friends. I don’t have a car, so most of my errands are done on foot or by public transit, which means I’m pretty familiar with the bus routes and the occasional pothole on the main road.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus—promises made on TV, ads that never mentioned people like me, and a ballot that seemed like a choice between “more of the same” and “something that might make things worse.” Between long shifts, taking care of my nephew, and trying to stretch every paycheck, I just didn’t see a point in carving out the time to go to the polls. I’ve stayed pretty skeptical since then; the headlines keep shouting about “record turnout” and “historic wins,” but my rent keeps going up and the grocery aisles are still full of price tags I can’t ignore. I’m sitting this 2026 midterm out as well, unless something hits close to home—like a major plant closure in the district or a sudden change to my housing assistance—that forces me to pay attention.  

My biggest everyday worries are keeping up with rent and making sure there’s enough money for groceries and school supplies. I also worry about the local job market—if the warehouse cuts hours or automation replaces some of the crew, that would hit me hard. When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats talk a lot about “investing in communities” but the funding never seems to trickle down to the neighborhoods I see every day. The Republicans keep yelling about “law and order” and “lower taxes,” but their tax plans feel like a joke when I’m already paying city taxes that barely cover trash pickup. Neither side feels like they’re listening to people who work the night shift and still have to worry about a kid’s school lunch money.
