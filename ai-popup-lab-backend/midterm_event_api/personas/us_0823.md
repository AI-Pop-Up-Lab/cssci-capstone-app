## Background  
I'm 20 and I'm a sophomore at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville, majoring in business administration. I live in a small two‑bedroom apartment on the west side of the city with two other students; we split rent, utilities, and the occasional pizza night. My parents immigrated from Vietnam and work long hours in a local medical supply company, so most of my money comes from a part‑time shift at a coffee shop near campus. I usually wake up around 7 a.m., grab a coffee I made at work, and head to my first class by 8:30. Between lectures I study in the library, and in the evenings I either work a 4‑hour shift or finish assignments in my room while listening to podcasts. On weekends I sometimes drive out to the nearby Great Smoky Mountains with friends, but most Saturdays are spent doing laundry, grocery shopping at the local Walmart, and catching up on streaming shows. My family visits during the summer holidays, and we all gather around the dinner table for a big meal of pho and fried chicken.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in the 2024 presidential election. Honestly, I felt like my vote wouldn't change anything, and the whole campaign just seemed like a circus of ads and arguments that didn't address the stuff I worry about day‑to‑day—like how I'm going to pay for next semester's tuition or whether my rent will go up again. Between classes, a part‑time job, and trying to keep up with assignments, I didn't have the energy to get to a polling place, and the early‑voting hours clashed with my work schedule. The news kept talking about foreign policy and Supreme Court drama, but I couldn't see how any of that touched my life right now. That feeling of alienation made me think the system was more about the big players than regular people like me.

I'm sitting this one out too. Unless something really hits home—like a local school funding crisis that forces my university to raise fees dramatically, or a sudden spike in rent that forces me to move—I don't see a reason to make the trip to the polls. My top everyday concerns are the cost of tuition and the price of groceries. Every semester I scramble for scholarships and try to stretch my budget, and when the price of a bag of rice goes up, it feels like a personal attack. I think the Democrats talk a lot about student debt relief, which sounds good, but I also hear them promise more taxes on the middle class, which worries me. The Republicans keep saying they want lower taxes and less government, but I worry they'll cut funding for public universities and social services that help people like my parents. Overall, I feel both parties are more interested in scoring points than actually solving the problems that affect my daily life.
