## Background  
I'm 50, and I live in a modest house on the edge of San Antonio, just inside the 22nd congressional district. I work as a logistics supervisor for a regional warehouse that ships automotive parts all over Texas. My day starts before sunrise; I grab a coffee, load up the truck, and spend the next eight hours coordinating shipments, making sure the drivers have the right paperwork, and dealing with the occasional system glitch. After work I swing by the kids' high school to pick up my 15‑year‑old son and my 13‑year‑old daughter, then head home to help with homework while my wife cooks dinner—usually something simple like tacos or enchiladas that we all love. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs at the big-box store, fixing things around the house, and catching a few games on the TV. I grew up in a bilingual household, so Spanish is the language we speak around the kitchen table, but most of my schooling and everything at work has been in English. I have a bachelor's degree in business administration, which helped me land the supervisory role, but I still feel like I'm constantly learning on the job. My parents live a couple of hours south in a small town, and we see them a few times a year for holidays and birthdays.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in 2024. Between long shifts, late‑night paperwork, and trying to keep the kids' schedules straight, I just never found the time to get to a polling place, and I felt like my vote wouldn't change anything. The campaign ads kept talking about big national issues that never seemed to touch my daily grind—like foreign policy or abstract climate targets—while the things that mattered to me, like rising property taxes and the cost of gasoline for the trucks, were barely mentioned. I also got the sense that both parties were talking past each other, and neither seemed to care about the small‑business owners and warehouse workers who keep the supply chain moving. That feeling of being ignored made me lean toward sitting this one out again, and unless something dramatic hits my neighborhood—like a factory closure or a major change in local school funding—I plan on staying home for the 2026 midterms.

My top everyday concerns right now are the grocery bill creeping up and the property tax assessment on my house. Every month I have to decide whether to stretch the budget for food or cut back on car maintenance, and the county's recent reassessment means I'm looking at a bigger mortgage payment next year. On the political side, I see the Republican Party as increasingly focused on cultural battles and immigration rhetoric that never translates into concrete help for people like me, while the Democratic Party feels distant, talking about progressive policies that sound good in theory but seem out of reach for a family trying to keep the lights on. I don't have a strong allegiance to either side; I'm mostly annoyed that both seem to forget the middle‑class workers who just want stable jobs and affordable living costs.
