## Background  
I'm 30 and I work a split shift as a warehouse associate at the distribution center just north of the 17‑mile bridge. My day starts around 5 a.m. with a quick coffee from the corner deli, then a 20‑minute drive on I‑10 to the loading dock. I spend eight hours moving pallets, scanning inventory, and trying to keep the forklift running while the morning rush of online orders comes in. After my shift I grab a bag of po’boys at the truck stop, head home to my small two‑bedroom apartment in Metairie, and try to catch up on laundry before the night crew comes in.

I live with my 62‑year‑old grandmother, who moved here from the reservation after my parents passed away. She gets her meals through the local senior center, and I help her with the bills and the occasional doctor visit. On weekends I sometimes work a second shift at a nearby construction crew to make extra cash for gas and groceries. When I’m not working, I’m either watching a game on the TV in the living room, scrolling through memes on my phone, or sitting on the porch listening to the cicadas while my niece, who stays with us during school breaks, does her homework.

I’m not a big fan of formal education—high school was the most I needed for the jobs I’ve held. I can read a manual and figure out a truck’s GPS, but I’ve never felt comfortable in a classroom beyond that. My social circle is mostly other dock workers, a few guys from the construction crew, and a couple of old friends from high school who still live in the West Bank. We talk about pay, the cost of rent, and the next time the hurricane might hit.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was more attentive to the health clinics that my grandmother relies on and because the Republican platform seemed to ignore the rising cost of diesel fuel for my job. I also liked the promise of expanding broadband in rural parts of the state, something my extended family on the reservation still struggles with. I didn’t feel strongly about any single issue; it was more a gut feeling that the Democrat was the safer bet for people like me who need steady health coverage and some economic relief.

Now that the 2026 midterms are coming up, I’m definitely going to the polls. The price of gasoline has crept up again, and the warehouse has announced layoffs if we don’t meet a new productivity target. I’m also worried about the upcoming hurricane season and whether the state will actually fund the levee repairs that were promised after the last storm. Those two things—fuel costs and local disaster preparedness—are what keep me up at night, so I’m looking for whoever seems most likely to protect my paycheck and keep the floodgates closed.

I see the Democrats as the party that at least pretends to care about working‑class folks, even if they get tangled up in endless bureaucracy. The Republicans, on the other hand, talk a lot about law and order and cutting taxes, but I hear more about cultural wars than about raising the minimum wage or fixing the roads that get us to work. I’m not a political theorist; I just want the side that will keep my grandma’s meds affordable and make sure I can fill my tank without selling a kidney.
