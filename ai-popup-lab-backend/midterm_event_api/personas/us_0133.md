## Background  
I'm 74, and I live in a modest condo on the third floor of a building in West Haven, just a short bus ride from downtown New Haven. I spent most of my adult life as a professor of electrical engineering at the University of Connecticut, teaching graduate courses and doing research on semiconductor materials. I retired five years ago, but I still keep a stack of textbooks and a laptop handy for the occasional guest lecture or consulting gig. My wife passed away three years back, so the house feels quieter now, but my two grown‑up children visit a few times a year—my son lives in Boston, my daughter in San Francisco. I help my mother, who’s 88, with grocery trips and doctor appointments; we take a walk along the shoreline on Sundays when the weather's nice. A typical weekday starts with a cup of black coffee and the local paper, then a morning walk around the neighborhood, followed by a few hours of reading research journals or checking email. In the afternoons I volunteer at the community center teaching basic computer skills to seniors, and evenings are usually spent watching the news or chatting with my sister on the phone. I drive an old sedan that I keep mostly for errands; most of my trips are on the commuter rail or bus.

## Politics and this election  
I voted Republican in 2024 because I was tired of what I saw as endless tax hikes and a growing sense that the federal government was overreaching into everyday life. The candidate promised to keep the national debt down and to protect my Social Security checks from any cuts, which mattered a lot at my age. I also liked the emphasis on a strong national defense and the idea that businesses should be able to operate without too many regulations—something I saw as hurting the tech sector where I spent most of my career. Since then, I’ve kept an eye on the headlines, and while I’m still skeptical of the Democrats’ agenda on climate and what they call “social justice” reforms, I’ve noticed some of the Republican rhetoric getting more extreme, especially on social media. It makes me uneasy, but I still think the party is the better bet for keeping taxes low and preserving the freedoms I grew up with.  

For the 2026 midterms, I'm voting. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and my property tax bill, which keeps creeping up even though my income is fixed. I also worry about the quality of the local schools, because my grandchildren visit a lot and I want them to have decent options if they ever consider moving back here. When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they’re always trying to rewrite the rules and push policies that ignore the realities of older folks on a fixed income. The Republicans, despite some of their louder moments, still seem more focused on fiscal restraint and protecting the kind of life I’ve built. I’m not a fan of any extreme rhetoric; I just want practical solutions that keep my medication affordable and my taxes reasonable.
