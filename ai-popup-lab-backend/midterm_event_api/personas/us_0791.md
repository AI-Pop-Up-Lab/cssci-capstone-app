## Background
I'm 35 and I live in a small unincorporated community just outside Spartanburg, in South Carolina's 2nd congressional district. I never finished high school; I left after ninth grade to start working full‑time to help my mom pay the bills. These days I'm a laborer for a local construction crew, mostly doing demolition and concrete pouring. The job is hard, the hours are early, and the pay is just enough to keep a roof over our heads.

I'm married to a woman who works part‑time at a grocery store, and we have two kids—a ten‑year‑old daughter and an eight‑year‑old son. We own a modest three‑bedroom ranch house with a leaky roof that we keep patching up ourselves. Most mornings I wake up at 5:30 a.m., grab a coffee from the kitchen kettle, and drive the 20‑minute commute to the job site. After work, I usually stop at the convenience store for a pack of cigarettes and a soda, then head home to help the kids with their homework before my wife cooks dinner. On weekends I do odd jobs for neighbors—mowing lawns, fixing fences—to bring in a little extra cash. I watch a lot of TV in the evenings, mostly news clips on my phone while I eat dinner, and I spend my free time on the porch trying to fix the old pickup truck that keeps breaking down.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in the 2024 election. Honestly, I felt like none of the candidates cared about people like me. The whole process seemed like a circus, and I was working two shifts that week, so I just gave up. The ballot felt like a choice between two sides that both promised things I couldn't see happening in my town—higher wages, better roads—but never delivered. That feeling of being ignored made me sit out, and unless something really shakes up my neighborhood—like a factory closing or a sudden tax hike—I plan on sitting this one out too. So I'm **sitting this one out** for the 2026 midterms.

My biggest everyday worries are keeping up with grocery prices and making sure the house doesn't fall apart. Gas and food keep getting more expensive, and my paycheck never stretches as far as it used to. The other thing that keeps me up at night is the property tax bill; every year it creeps higher and my wife and I have to scramble to find money for repairs.

When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as a bunch of city folk who talk about climate change and student loans while ignoring the real problems of folks who work with their hands. The Republicans, at least on the surface, seem to care more about taxes and jobs, but they also get caught up in fights that feel disconnected from my daily grind. Neither side feels like they really get what it's like to be on a construction site, so I'm mostly just annoyed with both.
