## Background  
I'm 21 and I’m finishing a master’s in public health at the University of Mississippi in Oxford. I work part‑time as a research assistant in the university’s health disparities lab, pulling data for a study on rural health outcomes. I live in a two‑bedroom apartment with a roommate who’s also a grad student; the rent is $650 a month, which eats a good chunk of my stipend. Most mornings I grab a coffee at the campus café, walk to the lab for a few hours of data cleaning, then head to a class on epidemiology. Afternoons are a mix of lab meetings, library time for my thesis, and sometimes a shift at the campus health center where I help with vaccination clinics. Evenings are usually me cooking something cheap—often ramen or a frozen pizza—while scrolling through Instagram or catching up on a Netflix series. On weekends I either drive a couple of hours to Tupelo to visit my parents or stay home and do laundry, grocery shopping, and the occasional hike on the Natchez Trace. I’m not into big‑ticket hobbies; my free time is mostly spent on school assignments, a few Zoom calls with my thesis advisor, and trying to keep my student loan balance from spiraling.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was the only one actually talking about student debt relief and expanding Medicaid in rural areas like my hometown. The promise of a tuition‑free community college program also hit home—my parents still own a small farm and can’t afford to help me pay off loans. I was pretty hopeful that the administration would push for more funding for public health research, which matters for my work. Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how well those promises are being kept. I’m still leaning Democratic, but I’m also a bit skeptical because I haven’t seen much progress on loan forgiveness and the recent budget cuts to the university’s research grant program hurt my lab. I’m definitely going to vote in the 2026 midterms; I think my ballot can still send a signal, especially in this swing district. My biggest everyday worries are paying rent on time and keeping my grocery budget under control—prices keep creeping up, and my stipend barely stretches. I also worry about the lack of affordable health care options for people in the surrounding counties. As for the parties, I see the Democrats as more attuned to the issues that affect students and low‑income folks, even if they’re sometimes slow to act. The Republicans feel like they’re more focused on big‑business tax cuts and cultural issues that don’t really touch my daily life, and I’m tired of hearing the same old “government overreach” rhetoric that seems disconnected from what’s happening in a small town or a campus lab.
