## Background
I'm 20, and I live in a modest apartment on the north side of Erie, Pennsylvania, just a few blocks from the lake. My parents moved here from Puerto Rico when I was a kid, and we all speak Spanish at home, though I’m comfortable in English too. After graduating from the local high school two years ago, I didn’t go straight to college; instead, I took a job at a grocery store as a checkout clerk. The schedule is a mess—early mornings on weekdays, a few evenings, and sometimes a weekend shift when the store is short‑staffed. I’m also taking a couple of community‑college courses at night, hoping to get an associate’s degree in business eventually, but the workload keeps me pretty busy.

I share the apartment with my mom and my 16‑year‑old brother, who’s still in high school. Mom works part‑time as a nurse’s aide, and we all chip in on rent and utilities. After work I usually swing by the corner bodega to grab a cheap dinner, then help my brother with his homework before I crash on the couch and scroll through my phone. On Sundays I try to visit my aunt who lives a short drive away; she cooks the best arroz con pollo and we catch up on family news. My free time is mostly spent listening to music, watching a few shows on streaming services, and texting friends about school, work, and whatever’s happening in the neighborhood.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like none of the candidates really cared about people like me—just a lot of promises that never seemed to touch my daily grind. Between juggling work, school, and family responsibilities, getting to a polling place felt like another chore I couldn’t fit in. I also get the vibe that my vote won’t change anything; the whole system feels stacked, and I’ve heard a lot of talk about “the rich getting richer” and “politics as a game.” That cynicism made the whole process feel pointless, so I stayed home.

Going into the 2026 midterms, I’m still leaning toward sitting this one out. Unless something hits close to home—like a major layoff at the store or a local policy that directly affects my rent—I don’t see a compelling reason to make the effort. My biggest everyday worries are keeping a steady paycheck and making sure I can afford groceries and rent without falling behind. The price of food has been creeping up, and my mom’s hours are getting cut, so the financial pressure is real.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty frustrated with both. The Democrats talk a lot about social programs, but I don’t see any concrete help reaching my community, and they seem to forget about working‑class families in places like Erie. The Republicans push for lower taxes, but they also push for cuts to social safety nets that my family relies on, and their rhetoric sometimes feels hostile to immigrants and people of color. It’s like they’re both shouting from opposite ends of a hallway I can’t get into. I wish there were candidates who actually listened to folks juggling multiple jobs and worrying about the next paycheck.
