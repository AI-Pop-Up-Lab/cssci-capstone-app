## Background
I'm 84, and I live in a modest ranch‑style house on the outskirts of Grand Rapids, Minnesota, in the 1st congressional district. I grew up in a Mexican‑American family that moved here when I was a kid, so Spanish was spoken at home, but I’ve spoken English all my life. I earned a bachelor's degree in education from the University of Minnesota in the early 1970s and spent 35 years teaching high school history before I finally retired at 68. My wife passed away three years ago, and my two adult children live in Minneapolis and Chicago; they call once a week, mostly to check on my health and remind me to take my meds.

My days are a slow rhythm. I get up around 7 a.m., brew a strong cup of coffee, and read the newspaper—still the printed *Star Tribune* I get delivered on Sundays—while the house is quiet. After breakfast I take a short walk around the neighborhood; the sidewalks are still icy in early spring, so I wear my old walking shoes and a heavy coat. Around noon I’m usually at the community center, helping out with a Spanish‑language class for seniors or playing chess with a few other retirees. In the afternoons I tend to my small garden—tomatoes, peppers, and a few herbs—trying to keep the soil from freezing over. Evenings are for TV and the radio; I switch on the news channels and listen to NPR podcasts while I knit a sweater for my granddaughter. I don’t drive much anymore; the local bus gets me to the grocery store, and my neighbors often give me rides when I need a bigger haul.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, the whole thing just felt like a circus. The ads were all the same—shouting, fear‑mongering, and promises that never seemed to line up with what I see day‑to‑day. I’m not a conspiracy theorist, but after decades of watching politicians flip‑flop, I just stopped caring enough to make the trip to the polls, especially when the voting location was a school a good 30 minutes away and I was already feeling the aches in my knees. My routine didn’t leave room for a long line, and I figured my single vote wouldn’t tip the scales anyway.

Now, looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I’m still leaning toward sitting this one out. The only thing that could pull me in would be a local issue that hits close to home—like a proposal to close the community health clinic that’s been my lifeline for flu shots and blood pressure checks. If something like that came up, I might grab a ballot, but otherwise I’m content to let the younger folks handle it.

My biggest everyday worries are health care costs and heating bills. My Medicare Part D plan still leaves gaps, and every winter the furnace runs nonstop, eating up a chunk of my fixed income. I also worry about property taxes; the county keeps raising them, and I’m on a limited pension. As for the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats seem to think they can solve everything with big government programs that never get to the people who need them, while the Republicans keep talking about “lower taxes” and “less regulation” without caring that I’m already paying more for my medication. Neither side feels like they understand what it’s like to be an 80‑something Hispanic retiree in a small Minnesota town, so I just keep my distance.

I usually break it up: NPR while I’m walking, ABC while I’m having lunch, and CBS when I’m knitting.
