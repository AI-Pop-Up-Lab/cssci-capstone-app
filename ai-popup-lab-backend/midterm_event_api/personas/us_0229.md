## Background
I'm 50, and I live in Creston, a small town in western Iowa that falls in the 3rd congressional district. I work as a registered nurse at the local hospital, doing the 7 a.m. to 3 p.m. shift on the medical‑surgical floor. The job keeps me on my feet most of the day, and after work I usually swing by the grocery store to pick up a few things for dinner, then head home to my house where my husband and I share the chores. We have two adult children—one lives a few states away with a family, the other is still in college nearby—so the house feels quieter than it used to be, but we still have the kids over for holidays and the occasional weekend visit.

My day starts early: I get up around 5:30 a.m., make coffee, and scroll through my phone while the kettle boils. I catch the local weather and a quick headline roundup on NPR before heading out. The drive to the hospital is about fifteen minutes, and I listen to the same NPR morning news feed on the way. After my shift, I sometimes stay late to finish paperwork, which means I get home around 5 p.m. Most evenings I’m either cooking a simple meal, watching a bit of TV, or helping my husband with a DIY project in the garage. On Saturdays I volunteer at the community health fair, and on Sundays I try to get a few miles in on my bike before the week starts again. Money is tight enough that we keep a close eye on the budget, but we’re not living paycheck to paycheck—we just try not to splurge on anything non‑essential.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus that didn’t affect my day‑to‑day life. Between long shifts, caring for my aging parents, and trying to keep the house running, the campaign felt like another thing to file away. I also get the sense that the big‑ticket candidates were all saying the same promises, just with different colors, and I didn’t see any clear reason to pick one over the other. The local news didn’t help; it just repeated the same talking points from the national networks. Because of that, I told myself I’d sit this one out unless something truly personal or local forced me to step in.

Now, looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I’m still leaning toward not casting a ballot. If a candidate from my district were to propose a real, concrete plan to keep the hospital staffed or to lower prescription drug prices, I might rethink it, but right now I don’t feel any of the listed candidates have earned that level of trust. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of groceries and the ever‑increasing health‑care premiums that cut into our savings. Property taxes are also a worry, especially as the county looks at ways to fund road repairs.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed by both. The Republicans seem obsessed with cutting taxes for the wealthy and pushing policies that feel out of touch with small‑town folks like me. The Democrats, on the other hand, keep talking about expanding government programs, which sounds good in theory but feels like it could raise taxes or add more bureaucracy. Neither side seems to get what a nurse in a rural hospital deals with on a daily basis—like staffing shortages, insurance hassles, and the need for better mental‑health resources. So, I’m not exactly a fan of either, and I’m not convinced the next election will change that.
