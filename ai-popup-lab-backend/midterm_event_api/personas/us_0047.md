## Background  
I'm 55 and work as a research professor in the biology department at the University of Arkansas at Pine Bluff. I grew up in a small town in California, moved to Arkansas after finishing my PhD because a faculty opening opened up and my husband got a job at the local hospital. We live in a modest ranch‑style house on the edge of Pine Bluff, close enough to the campus that I can bike a few miles to work most days. My husband, a radiology tech, and I have two kids; the oldest is in his senior year of college in Dallas, the younger just started a trade program back home.  

My days start early: I get up around 5:30 am, make coffee, and skim the NPR morning news while the kettle boils. I bike to campus, teach two undergraduate labs, then spend the afternoon in the lab sorting samples and writing grant proposals. On Thursdays I meet with a community outreach group that runs a free health‑screening clinic in the town center; I help coordinate volunteers and explain basic nutrition to folks who come in. Evenings are usually spent cooking dinner, checking in with my kids over FaceTime, and catching up on a few episodes of a streaming drama. Weekends are a mix of grocery trips, mowing the lawn, and the occasional visit to my parents in Little Rock. I don’t have a lot of spare cash, so I keep an eye on utility bills and try to stretch my grocery budget with sales and coupons.

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one seriously pushing for increased funding for public universities and community health programs—both of which affect my work and my family directly. The candidate’s stance on protecting Medicare and expanding Medicaid also mattered; my dad, who lives in a senior living facility, relies on those services. I was also motivated by the candidate’s promise to invest in rural broadband, something that would make my research collaborations much smoother.  

Since then, I’ve watched the administration’s attempts to pass more science‑based climate policies, and I’m cautiously optimistic, though I know there’s a lot of pushback from the state legislature. I’m definitely going to the polls for the 2026 midterms; I think my vote still matters for keeping education funding steady and preventing cuts to health programs. My biggest day‑to‑day worries are the rising cost of prescription drugs for my dad and the property tax hike that’s been proposed to fund a new school wing in our district.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like the more reasonable side on issues that touch my life—education, health care, and a bit of environmental regulation. I do wish they’d pay more attention to the specific challenges of small towns in Arkansas, but at least they’re trying. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more focused on cutting taxes for big businesses and rolling back regulations that protect public health. I’m not a fan of the rhetoric around immigration; it feels disconnected from the reality of my community, where we have a few families who came here decades ago and are part of the local fabric. In short, I’m leaning heavily toward the Democrats and plan to cast my ballot.

## Media Diet  
I spend about six hours a week on the news: NPR in the mornings while I bike, ABC News in the evenings after dinner, and I read a couple of Newsweek articles on the weekend when I have a quieter moment.
