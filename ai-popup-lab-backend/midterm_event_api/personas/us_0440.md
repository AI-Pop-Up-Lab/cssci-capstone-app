## Background  
I'm 60, and I live in a modest house on the east side of Saginaw, right near the river that used to be the lifeblood of the town. I work as a diesel‑mechanic at the local freight yard; the job's mostly hands‑on, fixing trucks that haul grain and lumber across the Midwest. I got my associate's degree in automotive technology at a community college after leaving the Navy, so I can read a wiring diagram without sweating.  

My wife, Linda, retired from the school district after 35 years as a cafeteria manager, and we have two grown kids—one lives in Detroit with a family, the other is back home fixing up a house in Flint. On weekends I help my mother, who’s 84, with grocery runs and doctor appointments; she lives a few miles away in a senior complex.  

My day starts early. I’m up by 5:30 a.m., make coffee, and read the headlines on my phone while waiting for the yard to open. After a 10‑hour shift, I usually stop by the local barbershop to catch up with the guys, then head home for dinner. Sunday mornings we’re at the Baptist church downtown, where I sing in the choir and volunteer at the food pantry. When I’m not at work or church, I’m either watching a game on the TV in the living room or tinkering in the garage on a project car.  

## Politics and this election  
I voted Democratic in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one really looking out for folks like me—people who rely on steady wages, Social Security, and affordable health care. The Democratic candidate promised to protect Medicare, keep the union contracts at the yard, and invest in community colleges, which mattered to me after all those years of trying to get my kids a decent education without breaking the bank. I also liked the tone; the candidate talked about criminal‑justice reform and voting rights, which hit home after I saw a few of my old Navy buddies get tangled up in the system.  

Since then, I’ve been watching the news and talking to people at the yard, and I still think the Democrats are the better bet for my day‑to‑day concerns. That said, I’m not blind to the messes they’ve made—some of the promised infrastructure projects stalled, and the inflation spike last year ate into my grocery budget. Still, the alternative feels like a gamble that could swing the yard’s union contract into a more hostile direction, and I’m not willing to risk that on a party that seems to want to cut taxes for the super‑rich and roll back environmental regulations that hurt the Great Lakes.  

I’m voting in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday worries are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax hike the county just approved—both are squeezing my fixed income. I also keep an eye on the local job market; the freight yard is stable now, but there’s talk of automation that could cut shifts. As far as the parties go, I view Democrats as the ones who at least talk about protecting the safety net, even if they sometimes stumble on delivery. Republicans, in my view, keep pushing for tax cuts that end up benefiting corporations more than working‑class families, and they seem less interested in funding public schools or health programs that keep my community afloat.
