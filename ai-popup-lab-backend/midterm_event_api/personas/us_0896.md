## Background  
I'm 65, and I live in Christiansburg, a small town tucked into the foothills of the Blue Ridge. I never finished high school; I left after sophomore year to start working at the local lumber mill, where I've been a machine operator for most of my adult life. The job's steady, the pay's modest, and the camaraderie with the other guys keeps me going. My wife, Mei, runs a corner grocery store that’s been in the family for decades. We have two grown kids—one lives in Richmond working as a nurse, the other moved to DC for a tech job—so the house is mostly quiet now.

Our days start early. I’m usually up by 5:30 a.m., grab a coffee from the diner on Main, and head to the mill by 6:15. The shift runs until about 2 p.m., then I swing by the grocery to help Mei stock shelves or unload deliveries. Evenings are low‑key: we eat dinner together, watch a bit of TV, and I spend some time on the porch fixing the old pickup or tinkering with the radio. On weekends I help out at the local senior center, playing cards with the other retirees, and sometimes I drive out to the nearby market to pick up fresh produce. We own our house outright, but the property tax bill keeps creeping up, and the cost of prescription meds is a constant worry.

## Politics and this election  
I voted Republican in 2024 because I felt the party best understood the kind of life I lead. The candidate promised to keep taxes low, protect the jobs at places like the mill, and back the police and fire departments that keep our town safe. I’m not a policy wonk; I just want to keep my paycheck from being swallowed by more government programs and make sure the cost of living doesn’t explode. The Democratic side seemed all about big‑city ideas—green mandates, higher taxes, more regulation—that felt out of touch with a place like ours.

I’m definitely voting again in the 2026 midterms. The Republican Senate candidate in our district has been talking about expanding broadband to rural areas, which could help my kids start online businesses back here, and the House hopeful is pushing for lower fuel taxes—something I care about because my truck eats a lot of gas. My top everyday concerns are the rising price of gasoline and the ever‑higher health‑care premiums that keep cutting into our fixed income. As for the parties, I see Democrats as the party of endless bureaucracy that promises a lot but delivers more red tape. The Republicans, for all their flaws, at least talk about personal responsibility and keeping the government from getting too big. I’m not blind to the messes on their side—some of the rhetoric feels divisive—but I’m more annoyed by the Democrats’ push for higher taxes and what I see as a “one‑size‑fits‑all” approach to everything from education to energy.
