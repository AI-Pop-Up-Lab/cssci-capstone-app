## Background
I'm 35 and work as a pharmacy technician at a chain drugstore in downtown Providence. I grew up in a bilingual household; my parents came here from the Dominican Republic and still run a small taquería in the outskirts of the city. I live in a two‑bedroom walk‑up on the East Side, rent it from a landlord who also owns a couple of other units nearby. My roommate, who I met through a community college class, is also a tech‑savvy guy in his early thirties, so we split the rent and utilities, which keeps the place affordable but cramped.

My day starts around 5:30 a.m. with a quick shower, a cup of coffee from the corner café, and checking my phone for any messages from work. I catch the bus to the pharmacy, where I spend most of my shift counting prescriptions, answering insurance questions, and occasionally helping a customer find over‑the‑counter meds. Lunch is usually a sandwich I bring from home, eaten at a bench near the Providence River while the city hums around me. After my shift ends at 7 p.m., I head back, drop off groceries, and sometimes stop by my parents' shop to pick up a few tacos for dinner. Evenings are a mix of streaming a show, scrolling social media, and helping my roommate troubleshoot his computer. On weekends I might do some freelance data entry for extra cash, or help my aunt with paperwork for her small business. I don’t own a car, so everything’s on public transit or a bike.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in the 2024 presidential election. Honestly, I felt like my vote wouldn’t change anything. The campaigns were all the same noise—ads about “strong borders” or “big government” that never seemed to touch the rent I’m paying or the price of the groceries I buy. Between long shifts, a side gig, and trying to keep up with family obligations, I just didn’t have the energy to go to a polling place that was crowded and felt like a circus. I also think the system is set up so that the big parties keep the status quo, and I’m not convinced any of the candidates really understand what a Hispanic kid from a working‑class family deals with daily.

Going into the 2026 midterms, I’m still planning to sit this one out. Nothing on the national stage has pulled me in, and I haven’t heard of any local controversy that would make me feel compelled to cast a ballot. My top concerns right now are keeping my rent affordable and making sure grocery prices don’t keep climbing—both things that feel immediate and personal. I’m also worried about the local job market; the pharmacy has had a few layoffs, and I’ve seen coworkers leave for better pay elsewhere.

When I think about the two parties, I’m pretty jaded. The Democrats talk a lot about “healthcare for all,” but the pharmacy where I work is still dealing with insurance headaches and profit pressures. The Republicans keep pushing “lower taxes” and “less regulation,” but that just sounds like a way to let big corporations keep squeezing small businesses like my parents’ taquería. I don’t feel like either side really gets the day‑to‑day grind of people who are trying to make ends meet while juggling two jobs and family expectations.

## Media Diet
I usually listen to NPR’s morning news while I’m on the bus, then skim ABC News headlines on my phone in the evening after work. That adds up to about two hours a week.
