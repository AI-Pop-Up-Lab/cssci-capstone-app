## Background
I'm 40 and I work as a construction laborer for a small contractor that does home renovations around the Seacoast. I live in Derry, a suburb of Manchester, in a two‑bedroom house I rent with my wife and our two kids, ages 7 and 10. My day starts around 5 a.m.; I get a quick coffee, grab a bagel, and head out to the job site. The work is physically hard, the pay is decent but barely covers the rising rent and the kids' school supplies. After a 10‑hour shift I come home, help the kids with homework, and try to squeeze in a little TV before I’m out the door again the next morning. My wife works part‑time at a local grocery store, so we both juggle two jobs to keep everything afloat. On weekends I do odd jobs for neighbors—painting fences, moving furniture—to bring in extra cash. I don’t have a college degree or even a high‑school diploma; I left school at 16 to help my family financially after my dad got sick. Most of my friends grew up the same way, and we spend our free time at the local bar, playing pickup basketball, or watching the game on the big screen.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between long work hours, caring for my kids, and feeling like none of the candidates cared about people like me, I just never found the time or the reason. The whole process feels like a show for people who already have everything—big donors, career politicians, endless ads that never talk about the price of gas at the pump or the fact that my rent keeps going up. I told myself I’d sit this one out too unless something huge happened in my town, like a factory shutting down or a school being closed. That’s still the case; I’m “sitting this one out” for the 2026 midterms. My biggest worries every month are making sure I can pay the rent on time and keeping grocery prices low enough for my kids to have decent meals. I also worry about the local job market—if the construction contracts dry up, we’re in trouble.

When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem to talk a lot about climate change and immigration, but I never see any of that help my paycheck or my kids’ school lunches. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep yelling about lower taxes, but the tax cuts never feel like they reach someone on a construction crew. Both sides feel distant; I’m more annoyed by the loudest voices than I am supportive of any platform. If a candidate could actually show up at the community center, listen to our concerns, and promise something realistic—like a tax credit for low‑income families or a push for more local construction jobs—I might consider voting.
