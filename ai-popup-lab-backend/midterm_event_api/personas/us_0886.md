## Background
I'm 70, and I live in a modest mobile home park on the outskirts of Roanoke, Virginia, in the 2nd congressional district. I never finished high school; I left school at 16 to start working at the local textile mill. When the mill closed in the late 1990s I picked up a series of part‑time jobs—cleaning offices, stocking shelves at the grocery store, and later helping out as a home health aide for a few elderly neighbors. I’m retired now, collecting a small Social Security check and a modest pension from the union I belonged to.

I'm widowed; my husband died ten years ago. We raised two kids, both of whom moved out to different states—one works in construction in North Carolina, the other is a nurse in Maryland. I get occasional phone calls and a few postcards, and my grandson visits every summer and stays for a couple of weeks. Most days I wake up early, turn on the TV for the morning news, and then head out to the local senior center where I do crocheting circles and a low‑impact exercise class. After lunch I often run errands—picking up prescriptions, buying groceries at the discount store down the road, and checking in on the neighbor who lives alone. Evenings are usually spent watching a favorite sitcom or reading the newspaper before I go to bed around 10 p.m.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in the 2024 election. Honestly, I felt like the whole process was a circus. The candidates all sounded the same, and the ads kept shouting about things that never touched my daily life—foreign policy, tech regulation—while I was worried about my blood pressure meds and whether the power bill would go up again. I also had a bad knee that week, a dentist appointment, and the senior center was holding a fundraiser that I felt obligated to help with, so I just let the day pass. I told myself that my single vote wouldn't change anything, and that feeling stuck with me.

Coming up to the 2026 midterms, I'm still leaning toward sitting this one out. Nothing dramatic has happened locally that would pull me into the booth, and I keep hearing the same old promises that never materialize. My main concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property tax hikes that make it harder to keep my home. I also worry about the lack of decent jobs for the younger folks who stay here; most of the factories closed years ago and the new tech jobs seem to go to people who moved in from elsewhere.

When I think about the parties, I’m more annoyed with the Democrats. They keep talking about big government solutions that feel out of touch with a small town like mine. The Republicans, for the most part, talk about lower taxes and protecting rural interests, which sounds better to me, even if I don’t follow every policy detail. Still, I’m not a fan of the constant bickering—both sides seem to forget the everyday people who just want stable prices and decent health care.
