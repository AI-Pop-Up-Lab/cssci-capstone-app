## Background  
I'm 60 and live in Scarsdale, a leafy suburb in Westchester County that falls in NY‑15. I work as a senior analyst for a health‑policy research institute that partners with several local hospitals. Most of my day is spent poring over data sets, writing briefing memos, and meeting with a small team of economists. I’m married to a retired civil‑engineer; we’ve been together for 35 years. Our two kids are both in their thirties—one lives in Boston, the other in Seattle—so the house is mostly quiet except for our golden retriever, Max, who needs his morning walk.

My routine is pretty set. I grab a coffee at the corner deli around 7 a.m., skim the headlines on my tablet while Max circles the block, then drive the half‑hour to the institute. Lunch is usually a quick salad in the office kitchen, followed by a conference call with a policy maker in Albany. I get home around 6 p.m., make a simple dinner, and spend the evenings either gardening, watching a documentary, or helping my mother with her medication schedule. On weekends I volunteer at the local library’s adult‑literacy program and meet up with friends for a wine‑and‑cheese night at a nearby bistro.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Republican candidate. I felt the party’s emphasis on lowering taxes and cutting what I saw as wasteful federal spending resonated with my own experience watching budgets swell at the institute. I also appreciated the candidate’s tough stance on immigration, which I thought was necessary for protecting jobs in the Northeast suburbs. Since then I’ve kept an eye on how those promises are playing out. I’m still convinced that lower taxes are crucial for retirees like me, but I’m a bit annoyed that the GOP hasn’t been more consistent on protecting Social Security benefits.

I’m definitely going to the polls for the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are the property tax bite on my home and the rising cost of prescription drugs. The Democrats in the state seem to want to raise taxes to fund new programs, which feels like a step backward for people who are already watching their retirement income. The Republicans, on the other hand, talk about fiscal restraint but sometimes ignore the needs of older voters when they push for cuts to Medicare. Overall I’m frustrated with both sides: the Democrats feel out of touch with suburban concerns, and the GOP sometimes feels like it’s chasing cultural battles rather than solid economic policy.
