## Background  
I'm 35 and work as a research scientist for a mid‑size pharmaceutical company in Newark, Delaware. I went to college in Boston, got a master’s in molecular biology and a PhD in pharmacology, then moved back east for a job that lets me stay close to my parents, who still live in a modest house in Wilmington. I’m married to a mechanic who runs his own shop out of a rented garage, and we have a six‑year‑old daughter who goes to the public elementary school down the street. Most mornings I’m up around 5:30 a.m., make coffee, and squeeze in a quick 20‑minute run before dropping my daughter off at school. I commute to the lab by car; the drive takes about 30 minutes, and I usually listen to the news on the way in. My workday runs from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., with a lunch break that I often spend in the break room scrolling through a mix of scientific journals and the headlines from the outlets I follow. After work I pick up my daughter, help her with homework, and we eat dinner around 7 p.m. My husband usually does the dishes while I catch up on a Netflix series or read a novel. Weekends are a mix of grocery shopping at the local market, a soccer game for my daughter on Saturday mornings, and occasional visits to my parents. Money’s tight enough that we keep a close eye on the budget, but we’re not living paycheck‑to‑paypay; we have a modest emergency fund and are trying to save for a down‑payment on a house.

## Politics and this election  
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one taking climate change seriously and pushing for more funding for medical research—things that matter to me both at work and at home. I also liked the candidate’s stance on expanding Medicaid, which helped a neighbor who’s been struggling with diabetes. That election felt like a chance to push back against the constant attacks on science and to protect the safety net that keeps my parents’ rent affordable. Since then I’ve kept an eye on how the administration follows through; I’m not thrilled with every rollout, but I still think the Democrats are closer to my priorities than the GOP. I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms; I’ve already marked the date on the calendar and plan to go to the polling place after my daughter’s soccer practice. My biggest day‑to‑day concerns right now are the rising cost of groceries and the looming increase in property taxes that the state legislature is debating. If those go up too much, we’ll have to cut back on things like my daughter’s extracurriculars or my husband’s ability to upgrade his shop equipment. I’m pretty annoyed with the Republican Party—most of their messaging feels like it’s aimed at voters who don’t care about science or public health, and they keep pushing for cuts to programs that help working families. The Democrats, for all their infighting, at least talk about issues that affect me directly, even if I sometimes think they’re too focused on identity politics and not enough on concrete economic relief.
