## Background
I'm 35 and I work full‑time as a customer service supervisor at a large electronics retailer in Glendale, right in the heart of AZ‑7. I grew up in a mixed‑language household; my parents immigrated from the Philippines, so we still speak Tagalog at home, but I’m fluent in English and can read a bit of Mandarin from my extended family. I finished two years of community college trying to get a degree in business, but life pulled me into a full‑time job before I could graduate, so I’ve been taking night classes here and there without ever finishing a program.

I live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment with my younger brother, who’s still in high school. We split rent and utilities, and we’ve got a small balcony where I keep a few succulents and a tiny herb garden because fresh cilantro makes the difference in my weekend pho. My mornings start at 5:30 am: I brew a strong cup of coffee, scroll through my phone for any urgent messages, then head to the store for a 7‑hour shift that runs until mid‑afternoon. After work I usually grab a quick taco from a nearby food truck, then either help my brother with his math homework or head to a community college class on weekends. On Saturdays I sometimes volunteer at the local library’s ESL program, helping new immigrants practice English. Sundays are my only day off, and I spend most of that time catching up on shows, doing laundry, and making a grocery list for the week. I’m not heavily involved in any clubs or churches; my social life mostly revolves around a small group of coworkers who meet for drinks after their shifts.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between juggling a demanding shift schedule, weekend classes, and caring for my brother, I felt like there was never a quiet moment to even think about the ballot. On top of that, the campaign ads all sounded the same—either screaming about “draining the swamp” or “protecting the American way of life,” but none of it addressed the real stuff I worry about, like the rising cost of rent and the grocery prices at the local market. I’ve always thought the system is set up so that my voice, as a low‑wage worker and a minority, doesn’t really matter. The whole process felt like a performance: I’d get a reminder from my mailbox, fill out a ballot in the kitchen, and then toss it in the box without really feeling any connection to the candidates.

Going into the 2026 midterms, I’m still sitting this one out. Unless something hits close to home—like a major layoff at the store or a local scandal that directly affects our neighborhood—I don’t see a reason to make the effort. My top everyday concerns are keeping up with rent and making sure groceries don’t eat up the whole paycheck. I also worry a bit about the local job market; the retail sector feels shaky, and I’ve heard talk about automation in the electronics department. As for the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with the Republicans; their rhetoric on immigration and taxes feels out of touch with my reality. The Democrats, while sounding more inclusive, still seem to focus on big‑picture issues and ignore the day‑to‑day grind of people like me. I’m not a fan of either side, but I’m less angry at the Democrats because they at least talk about affordable housing, even if the promises never fully materialize.
