## Background  
I'm 25 and work the afternoon shift at a grocery store in Huntington, West Virginia. I didn't finish high school, so after a few odd jobs I landed this position because the hours fit around taking care of my younger brother while my mom works two part‑time jobs. We live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment a few blocks from the river; the rent is $750 a month and the landlord has been raising it every year. My mornings start around 7 a.m. with a quick coffee from the corner deli, then I catch the bus to the store, where I'm on the checkout line most of the day. After work I usually swing by a community center to help my brother with his homework, then we cook something cheap—often beans and rice—before we both watch a bit of TV before bed. On weekends I try to pick up extra hours, sometimes bagging groceries at a nearby warehouse, because I want to build a small emergency fund. I also volunteer once a month at a local church that runs a food pantry; it’s one of the few ways I feel connected to the neighborhood. My life is a tight schedule of shifts, family duties, and trying to stretch every dollar.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in the 2024 presidential election. Honestly, between juggling two jobs, caring for my brother, and dealing with a rent hike that left me scrambling for cash, politics felt like a luxury I couldn't afford. I also get the sense that none of the candidates really understood what it's like to be a Black woman without a high school diploma in a place like Huntington. The campaign ads were all about big‑ticket issues that never touched my daily grind—like foreign policy or tech regulation—so I just tuned them out. I felt cynical, thinking my vote wouldn't change anything, especially when the local polling places were hard to get to and the lines were long. 

Going into the 2026 midterms, I'm still sitting this one out. Unless something dramatic happens—like a major factory closing in the area or a local scandal that directly affects my family—I don't see a reason to carve out the time. My biggest worries right now are keeping up with rent and making sure we can afford groceries; the price of food has been creeping up and my paycheck barely stretches that far. I also worry about job security; the store has been cutting staff after each holiday rush, and the threat of automation looms.  

When I think about the parties, I lean a little toward the Democrats because they at least talk about expanding Medicaid and protecting voting rights, which matter to me and my brother. But even they seem distant, and I rarely hear any concrete plans for working‑class Black folks in Appalachia. The Republicans, on the other hand, feel like they're pushing policies that would raise taxes on the poor and cut social programs, which I can't afford. So overall, I'm pretty annoyed with both sides, but the Republicans get more of my ire because their rhetoric feels like a direct threat to the little safety net I have.
