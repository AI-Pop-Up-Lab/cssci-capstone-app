## Background
I'm 35 and I live in a modest two‑bedroom apartment in Logan, right near the university campus. I work full‑time as a middle‑school math teacher at a public school in the district. My day starts at 5:30 a.m. with a quick shower, a coffee, and a scroll through the headlines on my phone before I catch the bus to school. The classroom is a mix of kids who speak English at home and a few who are still learning; I try to keep lessons relevant, but the paperwork and standardized‑test pressure wear me down.

After school I stay an hour for after‑school tutoring, then head home to make dinner for my partner and our two‑year‑old daughter. We don’t own a car, so we walk to the grocery store or take the bus to the farmers market on Saturdays. On Sundays I usually attend a small church service in town, then we have a family brunch with my sister who lives a short drive away. Rent is $1,300 a month, and every time the lease comes up I worry about the next increase. I’m saving enough for a down‑payment, but the market feels out of reach.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between long school days, after‑school duties, and caring for my daughter, I felt like the ballot box was just another thing I didn’t have time for, and honestly I was pretty sure none of the candidates were going to address the specific challenges I face—like affordable housing, decent childcare, and the lack of Black community resources in a largely white, rural district. The whole process seemed like a show put on by people who never walked my block.

I’m still not planning to vote in the 2026 midterms. Unless there’s a local controversy that hits home—like a school budget cut that threatens my classroom or a sudden spike in property taxes—I’ll keep sitting this one out. My top everyday concerns are keeping the rent affordable and making sure grocery prices don’t keep climbing, especially with a toddler in the house. I’m also watching the job market; a lot of my friends who moved here for tech jobs have left because the opportunities are thin.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats talk a lot about social justice, but in Utah they’re not really organized enough to push anything concrete for Black families. The Republicans seem to care mostly about taxes and land use, which feels like they’re ignoring the everyday struggles of people like me. It’s frustrating to feel like neither side sees me as a voter worth courting.

## Media Diet
I listen to NPR on my morning commute, then skim ABC News and CBS News in the evenings; it adds up to about four hours a week.
