## Background  
I'm 25 and work as a software engineer for a fintech startup that has an office in the downtown corridor of Fishers. I finished a master's in computer science two years ago, so most of my day is spent writing code, checking pull requests, and occasionally joining video calls with the product team in Chicago. I live in a two‑bedroom apartment with a roommate who’s a graduate student in biomedical engineering; we split rent, utilities, and the occasional pizza night.  

My mornings start around 7 am with a quick coffee from the corner cafe, then a 20‑minute drive to the office. I try to hit the gym after work a few times a week, but most evenings I’m either debugging code, watching a few YouTube tutorials, or scrolling through Reddit. On weekends I bike the local trails, help my parents with a grocery run when they’re in town, and sometimes volunteer at the community tech hub that offers coding workshops for high schoolers. I’m not married, don’t have kids, and I’m still figuring out whether I’ll buy a house or keep renting in the suburbs.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was more supportive of tech innovation, immigration reform, and student loan relief—issues that hit close to home. I was also encouraged by the candidate’s stance on climate research funding, which aligns with the work my friends do in environmental data. Since then I’ve kept an eye on how the administration handles the economy; the occasional inflation spikes and rising rent have made me nervous, but I still think the Democrats are more likely to protect the kind of social safety nets I rely on.  

For the 2026 midterms, I’m definitely voting. My top concerns are the cost of housing—my rent keeps creeping up—and the stability of my job as the tech sector feels a bit shaky after the recent layoffs in larger cities. I also worry about student loan debt; even though I’m past the repayment phase, many of my friends are still stuck, and I’d like to see policies that make it easier for them.  

When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as the side that’s more open to immigration and diversity, which matters to me as an Asian American navigating a career in a field that thrives on global talent. The Republicans, on the other hand, feel like they’re pushing policies that could tighten immigration and cut funding for research, which would make my work harder and limit opportunities for people like me. I’m not a fan of the partisan bickering, but I tend to lean left because the alternatives look riskier for my future.
