## Background  
I'm 55, and I live in Austintown, a quiet suburb of Youngstown in Ohio's 11th district. I work as a dental hygienist at a family‑run practice downtown, a job I’ve held since I got my associate's degree in dental assisting about ten years ago. My husband, a retired factory foreman, spends his days tinkering in the garage or helping my parents with errands; we both grew up in the area, so most of our family is still nearby. Our two kids are in their late twenties—one lives in Chicago, the other just moved to Columbus for a tech job—so the house feels a lot emptier than it used to be.  

Most mornings I’m up by 6:00 a.m., make a quick bowl of oatmeal, and head out the door with a thermos of coffee. I drive the 15‑minute stretch to the clinic, listening to NPR’s hourly news updates on the radio. After work, I swing by the grocery store, pick up a few things for dinner, and sometimes stop at the community center to check the schedule for the seniors' bingo night that my mom attends. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs, a bit of yard work, and occasional visits to my parents’ house in nearby Warren. I don’t have a lot of extra time for hobbies; when I do, it’s usually a short walk around the neighborhood or catching up on a Korean drama on streaming.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between the long lines at the polling place, the feeling that none of the candidates really understood what people like me needed, and a work schedule that left me exhausted by the time the polls closed, I just stayed home. The whole process felt like a reminder of how distant the political system is from my daily grind—paying rent, covering health‑care co‑pays, and watching the price of groceries creep up each week. I told myself that my single vote wouldn’t change anything, and that sentiment stuck.  

I’m sitting this one out, too. The midterms don’t feel any more urgent, and unless something dramatic happens—say a local factory closing or a sudden spike in property taxes—I’m not planning to make the trip to the booth. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of groceries and the ever‑increasing health‑care premiums that eat into my paycheck. Property taxes are also a worry because they keep going up, and I’m already stretched thin with the mortgage on our house.  

When I think about the parties, I’m mostly annoyed. The Democrats seem to talk a lot about climate and social issues, but they rarely address the concrete cost‑of‑living pressures that hit people like me. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep pushing for tax cuts that feel like they benefit the wealthy while ignoring the fact that my husband’s pension barely covers our bills. Both sides feel like they’re playing a game that doesn’t include folks who are just trying to get through the month.

## Media Diet  
I catch up on the headlines on abcnews.com while I’m having coffee in the morning, and I tune into NPR’s hourly news briefs during my commute and sometimes in the evenings when I’m relaxing after dinner.
