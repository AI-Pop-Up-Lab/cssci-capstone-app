## Background  
I'm 30 and work as a data analyst for the research department at the University of Kansas Medical Center in Lawrence. My day starts around 6 a.m. with a quick coffee from the corner diner, then a 20‑minute drive to the campus parking lot where I spend most of my morning cleaning up datasets for a federally funded health outcomes project. After lunch—usually a sandwich I bring from home—I head back to the lab for a few more hours of coding, then I’m done by 5 p.m. Most evenings I either hit the gym across the street or meet up with my roommate, Maya, to cook dinner and binge‑watch a Netflix series. We split rent on a two‑bedroom apartment near the downtown area; the rent’s high for a college town, but it’s close enough to walk to the farmer’s market on Saturdays. My parents live in Overland Park and visit once a month, bringing home dumplings and stories about the new electric‑car dealership they’re considering. I don’t have kids, and I’m not married, but I do have a small circle of friends from graduate school that I see on weekends for brunch or a quick hike in the Flint Hills.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between juggling a demanding job, paying rent that keeps creeping up, and trying to finish a certification for a promotion, I felt like my ballot wouldn’t change anything. The campaigns felt like they were shouting at each other about issues that didn’t touch my day‑to‑day life—foreign policy debates, the whole “tax the rich” thing, or the endless fights over abortion. I’m also pretty skeptical of both parties; the Democrats seem to think a few more student‑loan forgiveness programs will solve everything, while the Republicans keep talking about “family values” but vote for policies that make my grocery bill higher. I’ve watched a lot of the same news cycles on NPR and CNBC, and they just keep circling the same talking points without offering concrete solutions for people like me who are trying to get a mortgage approved and keep my car running.

I’m sitting this one out too. Unless something really local—like a plan to close the community health clinic where a lot of my research participants get care—happens, I don’t see a reason to go to the polls. My top concerns right now are the rising cost of rent and the price of groceries; everything else feels secondary. I think the Democratic Party is too focused on identity politics and not enough on middle‑class economics, and the Republican Party is stuck in an old‑school playbook that ignores the realities of a tech‑driven workforce. Both sides seem to forget that people like me just want stable jobs, affordable housing, and a healthcare system that actually works for the everyday patient.
