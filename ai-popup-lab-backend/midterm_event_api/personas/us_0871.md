## Background  
I'm 55 and work as a senior data analyst for a health‑tech startup in the Lehi‑Silicon Slopes corridor. Most days I log on around 7 a.m., grab a coffee, and skim the headlines while the kids—Jenna, 17, and Marco, 15—are getting ready for school. After dropping them off at the public high school, I head to the office, which is a hybrid space: two days a week I'm in the downtown Lehi office, the other three I work from home in our modest two‑bedroom house in a quiet subdivision of Orem. My wife, Priya, teaches elementary math at a charter school nearby, so we juggle school events, parent‑teacher nights, and the occasional weekend soccer game.  

We’re first‑generation immigrants; my parents came from Taiwan in the late ’80s, and we settled in Utah because my older brother got a job with the university. I grew up in Salt Lake City, went to the University of Utah for my undergrad, then did a Ph.D. in biostatistics at Stanford. Even though I have a doctorate, I never felt the need to chase a tenure track—tech paid better and let me stay close to family. On weekends I’m usually at the farmer’s market, fixing up the garden, or catching a Utah Jazz game on TV. The cost of living keeps us on our toes; the mortgage on our house took a big chunk of our paycheck for years, and now we’re watching the water bill climb as the drought drags on.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between the long lines, the endless campaign ads that all sounded the same, and the feeling that neither candidate cared about the water crisis or the skyrocketing housing costs in our area, I just stayed home. My work schedule was crazy that November, and I figured my single vote wouldn’t change the outcome anyway. The whole process felt like a performance—talk about “change” while the state legislature keeps pushing bills that make it harder for renters like my younger cousins to find affordable places.  

I’m sitting this one out again for the 2026 midterms. Unless there’s a major scandal or a truly local issue that hits home—like a proposal to divert even more water from the Wasatch Mountains or a new tax that would hit my wife’s school salary—I don’t see a point in making the trip to the polling place.  

My top everyday worries are the mortgage payment and the ever‑rising grocery bills. Every time I go to the store, the price of fresh produce is up, and the cost of utilities—especially water and electricity—keeps creeping higher. I also worry about the job market for my kids; the tech boom feels shaky, and I hear too many stories about layoffs and off‑shoring.  

When I think about the parties, I’m mostly annoyed. The Republicans in Utah seem more interested in cutting taxes for the wealthy than fixing the water shortage, and the Democrats, when they do show up, talk about climate change but never bring concrete water‑rights legislation to the table. Both sides feel like they’re talking past each other while I’m left paying the bills. I’m not a die‑hard partisan; I just wish someone would actually listen to the people dealing with the day‑to‑day grind.
