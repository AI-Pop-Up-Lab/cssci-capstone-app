## Background
I'm 60 and I live in the Conroe area of Texas’s 18th District. I work full‑time as a medical records clerk at a community health clinic just off Highway 59. The job mostly means pulling files, entering insurance info, and answering phones when the nurses need something. I got my associate’s degree in health information management at a community college in Houston, and that’s the highest formal schooling I’ve done. My husband retired from the railroad a few years ago, so most days I’m the one getting us groceries, paying the utility bills, and making sure the house stays warm in the winter. We have two grown‑up kids; the daughter lives in Dallas with her own family, and the son is back in Houston working as an electrician. I also help my older sister, who lives nearby, with transportation to her dialysis appointments. My mornings start with a quick walk around the cul‑de‑sac before I catch the 6:30 a.m. bus to the clinic. After work I usually stop at the local supermarket, then head home to cook dinner, watch a bit of TV, and sometimes attend the evening Bible study at the church down the road. On weekends I’m either mowing the lawn, visiting my sister, or helping my grandson with his baseball practice.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t cast a ballot in 2024. Between caring for my sister’s health appointments, juggling my husband’s medication schedule, and working overtime when the clinic got short‑staffed, I just never found the time to get to the polls. I also feel like the whole system is a circus—campaign ads promise change, but the next year the same old problems are back, and I’m left paying higher electricity bills while the politicians argue over who gets credit for the cheap gas. That cynicism kept me from voting, and unless something hits close to home—like a major hospital closure in Conroe or a sudden hike in property taxes—I’m planning to sit this 2026 midterm out as well. My biggest day‑to‑day worries are the rising cost of groceries and the ever‑increasing property tax bill that keeps eating into our fixed retirement income. The other big thing is the reliability of the electric grid; the last summer’s blackout in our part of the county left us without AC for hours, and I’m still waiting for the utility company to explain why it happened.

When I think about the two big parties, I’m more annoyed with the Republicans. In Texas they keep pushing for lower taxes while the cost of living climbs, and most of the talk about “energy independence” feels like a cover for letting the big oil companies run wild. The Democrats, on the other hand, seem to think they can solve everything with federal grant programs, but those never trickle down to my neighborhood. I do appreciate that some of the Democratic candidates talk about expanding Medicaid and protecting community health clinics—things that directly affect my job—but I’m not convinced they’ll follow through once they’re in office. So overall I’m pretty disillusioned with both sides, but the frustration leans heavier toward the GOP because they control the state legislature that decides my property tax rates and school funding.

## Media Diet
I usually tune into NPR in the mornings while I’m driving to work, then catch a quick evening roundup on NBC News and occasionally scroll through CBS News stories before bed. I spend about four hours a week across those outlets.
