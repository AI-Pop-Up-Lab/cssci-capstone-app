## Background
I'm 30 and work as a data analyst for a regional health‑care provider. I live in a small rented duplex on the edge of Oxford, right near the town square but far enough that I can hear the wind through the pines at night. My roommate, a grad student in history, and I split the utilities and take turns cooking—usually me, because I’m better at making a decent stir‑fry than she is at boiling water. Most mornings I wake up at 6:30, grab a coffee from the corner deli, and hop on a Zoom call for the first half of the day. After a quick lunch of a sandwich I made at home, I spend the afternoon pulling data from hospital records, cleaning it, and feeding it into a predictive model that the execs use to forecast staffing needs.

When I’m not at my laptop, I’m either hitting the local gym for a quick lift session or walking my Labrador, Duke, around the campus trail. On weekends I sometimes help out at the farmers market, selling homemade salsa my aunt taught me to make, or I catch a football game at the stadium if the Ole Miss Rebels are playing. I don’t have kids yet, but my parents live a few hours away in Jackson and I visit them a couple of times a year for Thanksgiving and Christmas. My life is pretty routine, but I like the mix of tech work and small‑town Southern vibe.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my ballot didn’t matter—there were endless ads, the same old talking points, and none of the candidates seemed to address the real day‑to‑day grind of someone trying to pay rent, keep health insurance, and maybe save a little for a house someday. Between long work hours and trying to keep up with Duke’s vet appointments, I just never got around to filling out a ballot. The whole process felt like a chore, and I was skeptical that any of the promises would change anything for a guy like me who’s stuck in a middle‑class job in a state that doesn’t get a lot of national attention.

Going into the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out as well. Unless there’s a scandal that hits close to home—like a hospital shutdown that would affect my job—or a local issue that directly hits my rent or the cost of gasoline, I don’t see a reason to make the extra effort. My top everyday concerns are keeping my rent affordable and making sure my health‑care coverage stays intact, especially with the rising cost of prescriptions. I also worry about the state’s economic outlook; if the big factories keep pulling out, jobs get scarcer and wages stay flat.

My view of the parties is pretty blunt. The Democrats, in my experience, talk a lot about social justice and climate change, but they never seem to understand the realities of living in a place where a lot of people still rely on agriculture and low‑wage service jobs. The Republicans keep promising lower taxes and “real American values,” but they also push policies that make it harder for me to get a decent health plan or to see any meaningful investment in the tech sector here. Both sides feel distant, and I’m not convinced either will put my concerns—rent, health care, and a stable job—at the top of their agenda.
