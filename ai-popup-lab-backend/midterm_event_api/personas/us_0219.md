## Background
I'm 60, and I live in Hilo on the east side of the Big Island, right near the Lyman Museum. I work as a senior research scientist at the University of Hawaii's Institute for Marine Biology, which means most days I’m in a lab or on a boat out on the Kona coast collecting coral samples. My wife, who retired from the local school district a few years ago, runs a small home‑based bakery that sells sweet poi pastries at the farmers market. Our two kids are grown—one is a software engineer in Seattle, the other a nurse in Phoenix—so the house is fairly quiet except for the occasional video call.

My mornings start with a cup of Kona coffee while scrolling through NPR headlines on my tablet. I head to campus around 7:30 am, spend the day in meetings, fieldwork, and grading graduate theses. Lunch is usually a poke bowl from the food truck down the street. After work I either take my bike to the beach for a quick surf before dinner, or I help my wife prep for the weekend market. Weekends are a mix of family visits, hiking the Akaka Falls trail, and catching a local baseball game. I try to keep a modest budget because housing prices have surged, even though we own our home outright.

## Politics and this election
I voted Democratic in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one pushing seriously for climate action that matters to us on the islands, and they were also defending Medicare and funding for our university. The candidate’s stance on protecting our reefs and supporting renewable energy projects convinced me that my vote mattered for the future of Hilo’s tourism and the fishing industry my colleagues rely on.

Now that the 2026 midterms are here, I’m definitely voting. My top concerns are the rising cost of groceries—especially imported goods that keep getting pricier—and the threat of more frequent hurricanes that could devastate our coastal neighborhoods. I’m also keeping an eye on the federal budget for the university; any cuts would hit research jobs and student scholarships hard.

I still think the Democrats are the better choice for environmental protection and social safety nets, even if the party sometimes feels disorganized in Washington. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more interested in tax cuts for big mainland corporations and less in the unique challenges Hawaii faces, like sea‑level rise and the need for sustainable tourism. Their rhetoric about “less regulation” sounds nice until you realize it could mean fewer protections for our coral reefs.

## Media Diet
I listen to NPR’s morning news with my coffee, read the top stories on ABCNews.com in the afternoon, and skim a few feature pieces on Newsweek.com in the evening. It adds up to about four hours a week.
