## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, the whole thing just felt like a circus. Between pulling double shifts, paying rent, and trying to keep enough food in the fridge, I didn’t have the energy to figure out which candidate was “better.” The ballot looked the same as every other year, and I kept hearing the same promises about “building back better” or “cutting taxes” without any real explanation of how it would affect someone like me. It seemed like the big‑ticket races were all about the elite, not about the people who are stuck working night shifts and worrying about a rent hike. I felt like my vote wouldn’t change anything, so I stayed home.

That feeling hasn’t really shifted for 2026. I’m still juggling a tight budget and a schedule that leaves me exhausted. Unless something dramatic hits my neighborhood—like a factory closing or a sudden spike in utility rates—I’m planning to sit this one out too. My top everyday worries are keeping a roof over my head and making sure the grocery bill doesn’t blow up. The cost of living is climbing faster than my paycheck, and I’m constantly watching the price of milk and gas.

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they’re talking down to us, promising free college and health care while ignoring the fact that a lot of us can’t even afford basic rent. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep shouting about “law and order” and “lower taxes,” but I don’t see any real jobs coming back to the area. Both sides seem more interested in scoring points than solving the day‑to‑day problems that keep me up at night. If I had to pick, I’m more annoyed by the Democrats’ “big‑government” vibe than the GOP’s talk about cutting taxes that never seem to reach the low‑wage workers.
