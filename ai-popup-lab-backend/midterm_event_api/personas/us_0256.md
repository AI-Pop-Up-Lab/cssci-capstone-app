## Background
I’m 60, and I teach environmental policy at Idaho State University in Pocatello, which sits right in the middle of District 2. I grew up on the Fort Hall Reservation, went to a community college in Twin Falls, then earned a master’s and a Ph.D. in public policy on a scholarship. My wife, who works part‑time at the local health clinic, and I have two grown kids who live out of state, so most evenings the house is just the two of us. My day starts early: I’m up by 5 a.m., make a quick coffee, and skim the headlines on my phone while the kettle boils. I drive the 30‑minute commute to campus, where I spend the morning lecturing and meeting with graduate students on research projects about water rights and land management. After lunch I usually head to the tribal council office for an hour or two, helping draft grant proposals for clean‑water infrastructure on the reservation. I’m on call for a few evenings a week to supervise a field study on native plant restoration, which often means late‑night data entry in my home office. When I’m not working, I’m out on the ranch that my family has tended for generations, fixing fences, checking the herd, or hunting elk in the nearby Sawtooth foothills. Weekends are a mix of family video calls, a Saturday morning powwow with the tribe, and a quick trip to the grocery store in Pocatello to stock up on supplies before the next long drive.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I didn’t vote for either of the major parties; I cast a ballot for an independent candidate who ran on a platform of tribal sovereignty and renewable‑energy investment. I felt the Democratic nominee was too focused on urban issues and the Republican one was dismissive of treaty rights, so the third‑party option felt like the only way to send a message that my community’s concerns weren’t being heard. I was also tired of the endless campaign ads that never mentioned water‑rights disputes that affect my tribe daily. That same frustration is why I’m still planning to vote in the 2026 midterms, but I’m looking at the congressional race more closely. A newcomer in the GOP primary has promised to work with tribal leaders on a joint water‑management plan, and the Democratic challenger is pushing for increased federal funding for tribal health clinics. I’m leaning toward the GOP candidate because, for the first time, he’s actually meeting with our council and talking about specific water‑allocation agreements, even if I’m still skeptical about his other positions. I’m not voting for the party; I’m voting for the person who will at least sit down and listen.

My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of health insurance for my wife and the ever‑increasing price of gasoline for the ranch trucks. Both of those things bite into the modest pension I get from the university and the modest income from our small herd. I also worry about water‑rights litigation that could limit the irrigation we need for our crops. When I think about the two parties, the Democrats feel like they’re all about big‑city social programs that never trickle down to places like mine, while the Republicans seem to treat tribal treaties as an afterthought. Neither side gets my vote automatically; I’m looking for a candidate who can bridge that gap, even if it means crossing party lines.
