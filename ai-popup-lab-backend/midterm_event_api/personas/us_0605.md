## Background  
I'm 35 and I live in a modest row house in the Central Ward of Newark, right in the middle of NJ‑10. I work as a shift supervisor at a big‑box retail store on the outskirts of the city; the job pays enough to cover rent and my kid’s school supplies, but it’s a grind—late nights, weekend stocking, and a lot of dealing with angry customers. I took a few classes at Essex County College but never finished a degree, so most of what I know about the world comes from on‑the‑job experience and the people I meet on the bus.  

My partner, Jamal, is a freelance carpenter who takes on odd jobs around the neighborhood. We have a 7‑year‑old daughter, Maya, who goes to a public elementary school a couple of blocks from our building. Our mornings start at 5:30 am with me getting a quick coffee, checking the news on my phone, and making sure Maya’s backpack is ready. I catch the 6:15 bus to the store, while Jamal drops Maya off at school and heads to his first job of the day. After work I swing by the corner store for groceries, then we all eat dinner together around 7 pm. On weekends we try to fit in a church service, a family visit to my mother in Elizabeth, or a quick trip to the farmers market to pick up fresh produce. Money’s always tight, so we keep a close eye on the budget and rely on community programs for extra help when things get rough.

## Politics and this election  
I voted Democratic in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one really listening to folks like me who are juggling work, childcare, and rising rent. The candidate’s promise to protect Medicaid and push for more funding for public schools hit home—my daughter’s school has been underfunded for years, and I’ve seen friends lose health coverage when they switched jobs. I also appreciated the emphasis on criminal‑justice reform; after a cousin spent time in a low‑level drug case, I’m tired of seeing our community punished for poverty.  

Since then, I’ve stayed pretty engaged, but I’m not a political junkie. I keep an eye on the big headlines and talk with neighbors at the church or the corner store. For the 2026 midterms, I’m definitely going to the polls. My top concerns right now are the relentless increase in rent and property taxes that keep squeezing our budget, and the quality of education in our public schools—if the schools don’t improve, Maya’s future looks grim. I’m also watching the state’s handling of the opioid crisis; it feels like there’s not enough help for people trying to get clean.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats still feel like the better bet for social safety nets and community investment, even if the messaging sometimes feels vague or the leadership seems distant. The Republicans, on the other hand, come across as more interested in cutting taxes for the wealthy and pushing cultural battles that don’t affect my day‑to‑day life. I’m not a fan of the constant partisan bickering; it makes it harder to see real solutions. Still, I’m leaning toward the Democrats because they’re the ones who actually talk about the issues that matter to my family.

## Media Diet  
I listen to NPR on my commute in the mornings, then scroll ABC News and CBS News on my phone in the evenings after dinner. All together that’s about five hours a week.
