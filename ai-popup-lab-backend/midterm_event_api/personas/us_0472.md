## Background
I'm 35, and I live in a modest two‑bedroom house on the west side of Columbia, Missouri, not far from the University of Missouri campus. I work full‑time as a project coordinator for a nonprofit that runs after‑school tutoring and mentorship programs in the district. My day starts around 6 a.m. with a quick coffee, then I hop on the bike to the office downtown. The job is a mix of grant paperwork, scheduling volunteers, and making sure the kids have the supplies they need. After work, I usually swing by the grocery store, pick up the kids from the elementary school bus, and help with homework before dinner. My partner works part‑time at a local pharmacy, and we have a 7‑year‑old son who loves soccer and a 10‑year‑old daughter who’s into dance. On weekends we try to catch a game at the stadium or visit my mom who lives in Jefferson City. I’m not a big fan of long‑term planning; most of my money goes to rent, utilities, and keeping the kids’ activities funded. I listen to a lot of gospel music on the radio and watch a bit of Netflix when I can steal a night off.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. I felt like the whole thing was a circus—candidates promising things I’d heard a hundred times before, and then nothing changed. Between working overtime to keep the nonprofit afloat and juggling school pickups, I just didn’t have the energy to go to a polling place, and I was convinced my vote wouldn’t matter anyway. The headlines about the economy and crime felt distant from my daily grind, and the endless ads left me more annoyed than informed. That feeling hasn’t gone away. I’m sitting this one out, too, unless something really hits home—like a local school closing or a sudden spike in rent that forces us to move.

My biggest worries right now are keeping up with rent and making sure groceries don’t eat up the rest of the paycheck. The price of fresh produce has gone up a lot, and with two kids in school, I’m constantly watching the grocery bill. I also keep an eye on the local job market; a few of my coworkers have been laid off, and the nonprofit’s funding feels shaky. As for the parties, I’m pretty fed up with both. The Democrats talk a lot about racial justice, but I don’t see that translating into better schools or jobs in my neighborhood. The Republicans keep pushing for lower taxes, but when the cuts hit social services, it’s the kids in my program who suffer. I end up feeling like the system is set up for folks who don’t have to worry about rent or school fees.
