## Background  
I'm 44 and I live in Scottsbluff, right on the edge of the Great Plains. I work as a program coordinator for a regional health services nonprofit that partners with the local tribal clinic. My job is mostly paperwork and occasional site visits, so I’m usually at a desk by 8 a.m., checking grant reports and making sure the community health workers have the supplies they need. After lunch I often hop on a conference call with the tribal council to discuss upcoming vaccination drives.  

At home I share a modest ranch‑style house with my husband, who drives a delivery truck for a grocery chain, and our two kids, a 13‑year‑old who loves skateboarding and a 10‑year‑old who is obsessed with video games. Mornings are a rush: I drop the kids off at the public elementary school, grab a coffee from the diner down the street, and try to squeeze in a quick scan of the news before the bus pulls away. Evenings are a mix of homework help, dinner that usually involves whatever’s on sale at the store, and a short walk around the neighborhood when the kids are in bed. On weekends I help out at the tribal cultural center, teaching a small group of elders how to use smartphones so they can stay in touch with family members who live far away.  

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in 2024. Between long hours at work, caring for the kids, and feeling like the candidates were all talking about issues that never touched my community, I just let the day pass. I kept thinking, “What difference does my vote make when the big parties keep ignoring tribal sovereignty and the real cost of health care?” That cynicism has stuck with me, and unless something dramatic happens—like a major cut to the tribal health grant or a local scandal that directly affects my family—I’m planning to sit this one out too. So, “I’m sitting this one out.”  

My biggest everyday worries are the rising price of groceries and the ever‑increasing cost of health insurance premiums. When the pharmacy raises the price of insulin, it hits my husband’s paycheck and my kids’ school lunches. I also worry about the stability of the tribal clinic’s funding; losing that would mean longer drives for basic care, which isn’t an option for many families here.  

When I think about the parties, I’m mostly annoyed. The Republicans in my district keep pushing for cuts to Medicaid and seem indifferent to the historical treaties that affect my tribe. The Democrats claim to be allies but often forget to follow through on the promises they make to Native communities. I wish there were more candidates who actually listened to tribal leaders instead of just using our votes as a footnote.  

## Media Diet  
I start my day with NPR’s morning news while I sip coffee, then catch up on longer pieces from Newsweek in the evening after the kids are asleep. On weekends I scroll through CBS News videos when I have a spare half‑hour. All together it comes to about five hours a week.
