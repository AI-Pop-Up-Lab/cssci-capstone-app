## Background
I'm 55, and I live in the little town of Clinton, right in the heart of Mississippi's 4th district. I work full‑time as a maintenance supervisor at the local community college; the job mostly involves fixing HVAC units, doing a lot of plumbing, and keeping the campus safe for students. I never finished my associate's degree, but the hands‑on training I got after high school helped me move up from a custodian to where I am now. My wife, Maria, runs a small taquería out of our house, and we have two grown kids—one in college in Atlanta and the other working as a truck driver out of Memphis. My mother lives with us; she's in her late seventies and needs help with medication and getting around, so I’m on call most evenings. My day starts around 5 a.m. with a cup of coffee and a quick scan of the local radio for the weather, then I head to work. After my shift ends around 4 p.m., I pick up groceries, drop off the kids at the community center, and help Mom with her dinner. Weekends are mostly family: church on Sunday mornings, a barbecue with neighbors, or helping Maria restock the kitchen. I drive a beat‑up pickup that’s been in the family for years, and I’m always watching the price of diesel at the station near the highway.

## Politics and this election
I voted Republican in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one looking out for the kind of folks who keep the local economy humming—small business owners, folks on the shop floor, and people who rely on low taxes to stay afloat. The Democratic candidate seemed more interested in big‑city issues, and I didn’t trust promises about expanding government programs to actually reach my community. I also liked the talk about protecting the border and keeping crime down; those were big concerns for me after a few break‑ins at nearby farms. Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how that administration handled the hurricane season and the farm subsidies. Some things went okay, but I’m still annoyed that the federal response felt slow and that the media kept yelling about “culture wars” instead of fixing roads. I’m planning to vote again in the 2026 midterms because the Senate race has a candidate who’s pushing for more infrastructure money in the Delta, and I want that to happen. My top everyday worries are the rising cost of gasoline—my pickup drinks a lot, and I’m hauling my wife’s groceries and Mom’s meds—and the price of health insurance for Mom. I’m not a fan of either party’s whole platform, but I lean heavily toward the GOP because they talk about cutting taxes and giving more control back to local officials. The Democrats, in my view, are too focused on identity politics and seem to think the federal government can solve every problem, which just makes things more complicated for people like me who just want a stable paycheck and safe streets.
