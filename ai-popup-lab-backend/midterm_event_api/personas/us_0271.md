## Background  
I'm 31 and I live in Oak Lawn, a suburb that falls in the 8th district of Illinois. I work full‑time as a maintenance technician at a regional distribution center that ships out pallets for a big retailer. The job pays decent money, but the shifts are long—usually 10 hours, six days a week, and I’m on call for emergencies after my shift ends. I got my associate’s degree in industrial maintenance from a community college in Chicago, which is why I can troubleshoot the conveyor belts and the HVAC system without calling an outside contractor.  

I share a two‑bedroom apartment with my girlfriend, Rosa, and our 3‑year‑old son, Mateo. Rosa works part‑time at a local grocery store, and we both take turns watching Mateo while the other works. Our mornings are a blur: I’m up at 5 a.m., making coffee, checking the weather, and scrolling through a few headlines on my phone before catching the 6 a.m. bus to the plant. After work I swing by the corner store to pick up milk and sometimes a cheap frozen pizza for dinner. On weekends I try to get a few hours of soccer with Mateo at the park, but most Saturdays end up with me doing laundry, paying bills, and catching up on a TV show or two. Spanish is the language we speak at home, but I’m comfortable switching to English at work and when I’m online.  

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in the 2024 presidential election. Between the 12‑hour shifts, the extra overtime, and trying to keep up with rent and Mateo’s school supplies, voting just felt like another item on a list I couldn’t fit into my day. I also felt like none of the candidates really understood what a working‑class family like mine needed—big promises about “change” or “law and order” just sounded like campaign fluff. The whole process felt distant, and I figured my single vote wouldn’t move the needle anyway.  

Going into the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out, too. Unless something blows up locally—like a factory closure that threatens my job, or a policy change that spikes the cost of the utilities we rely on—I can’t see myself carving out the time to vote. My top everyday concerns are keeping a steady paycheck and making sure grocery prices don’t keep climbing; both of those feel more immediate than any national rhetoric.  

When I think about the two major parties, I’m pretty fed up with the Democrats. They talk a lot about immigration reform and social programs, but the funding never seems to reach the neighborhoods where I grew up, and the taxes they push for feel like they’ll end up on my paycheck. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep shouting about “border security” and cutting taxes, but they rarely address the wage stagnation or the rising cost of health insurance that hits families like mine hard. Both sides feel like they’re more interested in scoring points than solving the day‑to‑day problems I face.
