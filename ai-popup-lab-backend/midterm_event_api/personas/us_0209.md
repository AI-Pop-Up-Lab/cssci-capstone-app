## Background
I'm 79 and I live in a small apartment on the north side of Honolulu, just a few blocks from the bus depot. I grew up in a farming family on the Big Island, but I moved to the city when I was 30 to work as a housekeeper for a local hotel. I never finished high school; after my first child was born I quit school and stayed home to raise my kids. My husband died ten years ago, and now my daughter, who works as a nurse, lives with me most days so we can look after each other. I spend my mornings making coffee, checking the mail, and watching the news on the TV while I eat a bowl of oatmeal. After that I usually take a short walk to the market to buy fresh fruit and some fish for lunch. In the afternoons I tend to the plants on my balcony, water the orchids, and sometimes help my neighbor, Mrs. K, carry her groceries up the stairs. Evenings are quiet; I watch a sitcom or a drama, then read the local paper before going to bed around 10 p.m. I don't drive much anymore, so the bus is my main way to get around, and I rely on my daughter for doctor appointments and any big errands.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. By the time the primaries rolled around I was already exhausted from a week of caring for my daughter after she caught a bad flu, and the polling place was a half‑hour bus ride away. I felt like none of the candidates really understood what a senior on a fixed income deals with—high utility bills, rising grocery prices, and the constant worry about health care coverage. The whole process just seemed like a show; I figured my single vote wouldn't change anything, so I stayed home. The same feeling is still with me now. I plan to sit this 2026 midterm out as well unless something dramatic happens—like a sudden cut to Medicare or a local scandal that directly affects my building. My biggest everyday concerns are the cost of food and my medical bills. Every week I have to stretch the grocery budget, and the co‑pay for my prescription keeps creeping up. I also worry about property taxes; the city keeps proposing new assessments and I don't see where the extra money is going.

When I think about the parties, I feel pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats, who dominate here, keep talking about climate goals and tech jobs while I’m just trying to keep my rent affordable and my blood pressure under control. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to ignore the reality of life in Hawaii—high housing costs, tourism dependence, and the need for better health services for seniors. Neither side feels like they’re listening to folks like me who are just trying to get by.
