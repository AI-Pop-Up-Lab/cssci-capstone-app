## Background  
I'm 80, and I live in a modest house on the edge of Pikeville, right in the heart of Kentucky's 3rd district. I spent most of my adult life working as a seamstress in a small factory that closed down about fifteen years ago. Since then I've been collecting Social Security and a modest pension from the union, which keeps the lights on and the garden growing. My late husband passed five years back, and my two grown children live out of state—one in Ohio, the other in Tennessee. I get a weekly call from my daughter, and we sometimes video chat when the internet cooperates.  

My days are simple. I rise around 6 a.m., brew a strong cup of coffee, and catch the morning news on the TV while the kettle whistles. After a quick breakfast of toast and jam, I tend to the front‑yard flowers, pull weeds, and check on the chicken coop—four hens keep me company and provide fresh eggs. Around noon I head to the local grocery store; the aisles are familiar, and the clerk knows my name. In the afternoon I either knit a blanket for a church charity drive or sit on the porch reading the paper, occasionally scrolling through the news sites on my tablet. Sundays are for church and a potluck lunch with neighbors. I drive a reliable old sedan, and I’m careful about the rising cost of gasoline, which eats into my fixed budget.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my vote wouldn't change anything, and the whole campaign felt like a circus—ads shouting at me from every direction, candidates promising things I couldn't see happening in the hills around me. Between caring for the hens, dealing with a leaky roof, and trying to stretch my pension, politics just fell to the bottom of the list. I also felt alienated by the endless partisan bickering; neither side seemed to understand the daily grind of folks like me who just want affordable medicine and a decent road to take the kids to church.  

I'm sitting this one out too. Unless something dramatic hits our community—like a factory closing for good or a sudden spike in property taxes that forces me out of my home—I don't see a reason to go to the polls. My top worries right now are the price of prescription drugs and the state of local jobs. The pharmacy on Main Street keeps raising prices, and my neighbor lost his job at the coal plant last month. I also worry about property taxes; the county keeps raising rates and I'm on a fixed income.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they care about big city issues and climate talks that don't affect the potholes on my road. The Republicans talk about culture wars and tax cuts, but those cuts never seem to trickle down to a retired seamstress in Pikeville. Both sides promise change, but I've seen promises come and go without real help for people like me.

## Media Diet  
I usually watch the evening broadcast from NBC and CBS after dinner, and I skim the headlines on ABC's website in the morning. Between those three outlets I clock about twelve hours a week, mostly split between the TV news hour each night and a quick scroll on my tablet before bed.
