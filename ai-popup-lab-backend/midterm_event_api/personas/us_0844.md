## Background
I'm 20, and I live in Midland, right on the edge of the oil fields that stretch across West Texas. I grew up in a modest house with my mom, who works two jobs at a nursing home, and my 14‑year‑old brother, who is still in middle school. After finishing high school a couple of years ago I didn't go straight to college; instead I picked up a part‑time job at the local grocery store, stocking shelves and ringing up customers on the night shift. The schedule is weird—my afternoons are usually spent at the store, then I swing by the community center to help run the after‑school program for younger kids, mostly other Native kids from the reservation nearby. On weekends I try to catch up on sleep, help my mom with chores, and sometimes I drive out to the drive‑in theater with my brother when we can afford a cheap ticket. My days are a mix of stocking aisles, doing laundry in the cramped apartment, and scrolling on my phone while waiting for the next shift. I don't have a car of my own; I ride my brother's old sedan when I need to get to the town square or to the nearest Walmart for groceries. I’m trying to save up for a community college class, but right now the paycheck from the store is what keeps the lights on.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like none of the candidates really cared about people like me—someone who works a night shift, worries about rent, and tries to keep a roof over the family. The whole campaign felt like a circus, full of promises that never translate to the desert towns and reservations where I grew up. I was also swamped with extra hours at the store when the pandemic hit, and the ballot deadline slipped right between my shifts. I figured my single vote wouldn't move the needle, so I just stayed home and watched the news later.

For the 2026 midterms, I'm sitting this one out too. Unless something blows up locally—like a new pipeline that threatens our water supply or a school funding cut that hits the after‑school program—I don't see a reason to spend another night at the polls. My biggest everyday concerns are keeping my part‑time job stable and making sure we can afford groceries and rent. The rent keeps creeping up, and the price of fresh produce feels like a luxury sometimes.

When I think about the two big parties, I’m pretty frustrated. The Democrats talk a good game about helping minorities, but I never see any real change on the ground for Native communities; they seem more focused on big‑city issues. The Republicans keep pushing for more oil drilling and less regulation, which sounds good for the local economy, but they also ignore the environmental damage that hits our water and health. Both sides seem to think my vote is a footnote, and that makes me distrustful of the whole system.
