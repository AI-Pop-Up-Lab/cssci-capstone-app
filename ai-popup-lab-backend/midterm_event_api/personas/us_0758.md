## Background
I'm 80, and I live in a modest brick house on a quiet street in West Reading, right in the middle of Pennsylvania’s 11th district. I spent most of my working life as an electrician for a regional contractor, a job I got after two years at a community college where I learned the basics of wiring and safety codes. I retired about five years ago, but I still do occasional handyman gigs for neighbors—fix a leaky faucet, replace a light fixture—just to stay busy and bring in a little extra cash for my meds.

My wife passed away three years back, and our two kids live up in the suburbs of Philadelphia. They call me every Sunday, and I try to visit them at least once a year, usually in the summer when the heat isn’t too oppressive. My eldest grandson, who’s 19 now, stays with me during his summer break and helps me tend the vegetable garden out back. I’m part of a senior center that meets three mornings a week for bingo, low‑impact exercise, and a potluck lunch. After that I usually swing by the local library to return books and catch up on the crossword.

My day starts early: a cup of black coffee, the morning paper, and a quick scroll through the news on my tablet while I wait for the bus that takes me to the center. Afternoons are mostly for the garden, a little yard work, or watching the news again before dinner. Evenings are quiet—usually a TV dinner, a bit of TV, and then I’m in bed by nine because I need the rest.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my voice was just noise in a system that never seemed to care about folks my age. Between my health appointments, the cost of my prescriptions, and the endless stream of campaign ads that all sounded the same, I just tuned out. I figured, “Why bother?” when the candidates kept promising the same thing—lower taxes for the rich, more jobs for the young—while I was fighting just to keep my blood pressure under control. The whole process felt like a circus, and I was too tired to sit in a crowded polling place.

I’m sitting this one out too. Nothing in the current headlines has pulled me back in. If a major scandal broke that directly affected my community—say, a hospital closure or a sudden hike in property taxes—I might reconsider, but right now I’m just trying to keep my health stable and my bills paid.

My biggest everyday worries are prescription drug prices and my property tax bill. The pharmacy keeps raising the cost of my heart meds, and the county assessor’s office seems to think my modest house is worth way more than it is, which drives my taxes up. I also worry about the reliability of the local hospital; the ER wait times are getting longer, and I can’t afford to be stuck there for hours if something goes wrong.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats keep talking about “expanding healthcare” but the rollout of any real help feels miles away, and they’re always pushing for higher taxes that would hit retirees like me. The Republicans brag about “cutting taxes” and “supporting small businesses,” yet they rarely mention the seniors who rely on fixed incomes. I guess I’m more of a “who’s actually going to lower my prescription costs” kind of voter than a partisan.
