## Background  
I'm 35 and I work as a compliance analyst for a midsize insurance firm in the suburban office park near Mount Laurel. I went to Rutgers for a four‑year degree in business administration, then got a certification in risk management. My wife and I bought a three‑bedroom townhouse in a quiet cul‑de‑sac a few years ago; we have a six‑year‑old daughter who goes to the local public elementary school. Most mornings I grab a coffee from the corner deli, hop on the Garden State Parkway, and spend about 45 minutes in traffic before my desk. After work I usually swing by the grocery store to pick up dinner staples, then help my daughter with her homework and try to fit in a quick workout at the community center. On weekends we’re either at the park, at my parents’ house in Camden County, or doing a bit of DIY around the house—painting the patio, fixing a leaky faucet. I’m not a big traveler; most of my free time is spent watching my daughter’s soccer games or scrolling through memes on my phone while I wait for the laundry.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was the only one taking health‑care affordability seriously—my sister lost her job and was struggling with insurance, and the Republican platform seemed indifferent. I also liked the emphasis on expanding broadband in rural parts of New Jersey, which would help my cousin who runs a small business from his home. Since then I’ve stayed fairly engaged; I’ve signed up for a couple of neighborhood canvassing groups and I keep an eye on local council races. I’m definitely going to the polls again this November for the midterms—“I’m voting,” I tell anyone who asks. My biggest everyday worries are the rising cost of rent for our townhouse and the price at the pump; gasoline feels like it’s always creeping up, and it eats into the budget for groceries and my daughter’s school supplies. I’m also nervous about the local job market—our firm has hinted at possible layoffs if the insurance market stays volatile. When I think about the parties, I see Democrats as more in touch with everyday struggles, even if they sometimes talk in buzzwords I don’t fully get. Republicans, to me, feel like they’re more focused on tax cuts for big corporations and less on the kind of public services my family relies on. I’m not a fan of the partisan shouting, but I’m leaning toward supporting whoever promises to keep my rent stable and gas prices from spiraling.
