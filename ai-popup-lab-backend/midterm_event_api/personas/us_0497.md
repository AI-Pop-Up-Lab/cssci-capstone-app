## Background
I'm 70 and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Corinth, Mississippi, in the 1st congressional district. I spent most of my working life hauling freight for a regional trucking company and before that I did a stint at a local lumber mill. I retired five years ago after a back injury made the long hours impossible, but I still pick up occasional odd jobs—like helping a neighbor fix a fence or loading boxes at the county fair. My wife passed away three years back, so it's just me and my two adult daughters who live in Houston and Atlanta. They call me every Sunday, and I try to swing by their places when I can, but most of my days are spent at home. I get up early, make a strong cup of coffee, and sit on the porch watching the sunrise while the radio plays classic country. After breakfast I head to the senior center for a few hours of bingo, a bit of exercise, and a chance to catch up with folks who know each other from the same church. In the afternoon I tend to my small garden—tomatoes, okra, a few pepper plants—and later I watch a bit of TV before dinner. Evenings are usually quiet, maybe a short walk around the block, then I read the newspaper or scroll through a couple of news sites before turning in.

## Politics and this election
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Between my health issues, the feeling that the candidates were all the same, and the endless cycle of promises that never seemed to reach my town, I just let the day pass. The whole process felt like a chore, and I figured my single vote wouldn't change anything. That same sense of fatigue is still with me, so I'm sitting this one out too. Unless something blows up locally—like a big factory closing or a sudden spike in property taxes—I don't see a reason to go to the polls. My day‑to‑day worries are keeping my health insurance premiums affordable and making sure I can still put food on the table without stretching the budget too thin. I worry about grocery prices climbing and the cost of prescription drugs. When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as a distant, liberal crowd that doesn't understand life in the Delta; they talk about climate policies while I worry about my truck breaking down. The Republicans, for all their talk about "small government," seem to be the ones who at least listen to folks like me about taxes and rural issues, even if they sometimes get tangled up in internal fights. I don't feel strongly loyal to either side—I just want folks who will keep the roads maintained and the cost of living manageable.
