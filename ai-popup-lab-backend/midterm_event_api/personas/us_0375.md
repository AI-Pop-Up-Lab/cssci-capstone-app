## Background  
I'm 84 and I live in an older brick walk‑up on the west side of Cambridge, just a few blocks from the Charles. I spent most of my working life as a political science professor at a nearby university, so I still have a stack of books and a habit of reading scholarly articles even though I'm officially retired. My wife passed away three years ago, and our two adult children live in Boston and Portland, so most evenings are quiet except for the occasional phone call. I get up around 7 a.m., make a strong cup of coffee, and spend a half hour scrolling through the news on my tablet before heading out for a short walk with my golden retriever, Max. After the walk I tend to the little garden on the balcony—herbs, a few tomatoes, and a dandelion that keeps coming back.  

Most days I volunteer at the community center, helping run a weekly discussion group for other seniors about local history. I also sit on the board of a nonprofit that promotes affordable housing in the city, which keeps me out of the house a few evenings a week. When I'm not at the center, I’m usually at the library, picking up a new novel or a biography. My health is generally good, but I have to keep an eye on my blood pressure and my arthritis, so I try to stay active and keep a regular routine.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I didn’t vote for a Democrat or a Republican; I cast my ballot for the Green Party’s presidential ticket. After decades of watching the two major parties trade slogans and trade‑offs, I felt none of them really cared about the climate crisis or the aging‑population issues that hit me every day. I was also frustrated by the way campaign money seemed to drown out ordinary voices. The Green candidate’s platform on renewable energy, universal prescription‑drug coverage, and elder‑care funding resonated more than any promise from the mainstream parties.  

That said, I’m not a die‑hard third‑party activist. I voted because I wanted to send a message, not because I thought the Green ticket could win. Since then I’ve kept an eye on local races, especially for the state senate seat that will affect Medicaid funding in our district. I’m definitely going to the polls for the 2026 midterms—“I’m voting.” My top concerns right now are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the property‑tax hike the city council is proposing to fund a new office building. I’m also watching the debate over the state’s plan to expand broadband to senior housing, because without reliable internet I feel cut off from family and information.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they’ve moved too far toward the center; they talk about climate but backtrack when a big corporation threatens jobs. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to ignore the needs of older voters completely, focusing on tax cuts that mostly help the wealthy. Both sides, in my view, treat seniors like a voting bloc rather than a group with real, everyday problems. I’m hoping a moderate independent or a progressive Democrat who truly backs universal drug pricing will get my vote this time.

## Media Diet  
I listen to NPR’s hourly news brief in the morning while I’m making coffee, scroll through ABC News articles in the evening after dinner, and read a feature or two on Newsweek over the weekend; that adds up to about ten hours a week.
