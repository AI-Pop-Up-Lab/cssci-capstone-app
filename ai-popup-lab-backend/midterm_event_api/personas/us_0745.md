## Background
I'm 50 and I work as a warehouse supervisor at the distribution center just outside of Lancaster. My husband and I have been married for 22 years; we have two kids—Jenna, 18, who just started her senior year at the local high school, and Marco, 24, who lives in a roommate situation near Harrisburg while finishing his associate's degree. Most mornings I wake up around 5:30 a.m., make coffee, and get the kids ready for school before catching the 6:45 a.m. bus to the plant. My shift runs from 7 a.m. to 3:30 p.m., and I spend a lot of time coordinating shipments, making sure the forklift crew stays on schedule, and handling the occasional paperwork nightmare when a delivery gets delayed. After work I swing by the grocery store, pick up a few things for dinner, and help the kids with homework. Weekends are a mix of family chores—cleaning the garage, doing laundry, and sometimes taking the kids to their soccer practice or a quick trip to the farmer’s market. I’m pretty involved in the local church’s food pantry, but otherwise I keep a low profile in the neighborhood. In my spare time I watch a bit of TV, scroll through my phone, and try to keep up with the kids’ social media feeds.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between long work hours, caring for my teenage daughter’s school projects, and juggling the bills that keep piling up, politics just felt like another thing I didn’t have the energy for. The candidates all sounded the same, and I was convinced my vote wouldn’t change anything—especially when the ballot felt like a choice between two sides that both promise tax cuts or more spending but never address the real pain of grocery prices and the property tax hikes on our home. That feeling of cynicism has only deepened; I’m still not convinced the system cares about folks like me who are just trying to keep a roof over our heads and food on the table. Because of that, I’m sitting this one out too. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of groceries and the ever‑increasing property taxes that make it harder to save for retirement. When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem to talk a lot about climate and social issues but rarely mention the price at the checkout line. The Republicans keep promising lower taxes, but the cuts never seem to reach a working‑class family in a small town. I’m not a fan of either side, and unless something hits close to home—like a local plant closure or a school funding crisis—I’ll stay home on Election Day.
