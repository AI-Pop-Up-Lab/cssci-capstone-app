## Background

I'm 25 and work as a junior IT support specialist for a regional health network out in Springdale, Arkansas. I grew up in a small town near Bentonville, the son of immigrant parents who came here to work in the automotive plants. After high school I got a scholarship to the University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, where I finished a bachelor's in computer information systems. I moved back to Springdale right after graduation because my parents needed help with the family’s small electronics repair shop, and I liked the idea of staying close to home.

I rent a two‑bedroom apartment above a laundromat with a roommate I met in a coding bootcamp. Our rent is $950 a month, which feels tight when my paycheck comes in after taxes and I still have to send $300 a month toward my student loans. I usually get up around 6:30 am, grab a coffee from the corner Denny’s, and hop on the bus to the hospital campus where I spend most of the day troubleshooting network glitches and helping nurses with their tablets. Lunch is usually a quick sandwich from the food court, and after work I sometimes grab a shift at the family shop on weekends to bring in a little extra cash.

Evenings are a mix of streaming a show on Netflix, scrolling through Reddit tech threads, and trying to keep up with my parents’ expectations about “saving for a house.” My weekend routine often includes a trip to the farmers market with my roommate, a quick hike on the Ozark trail, and a video call with my younger sister who’s still in college in California. I’m not married, I don’t have kids, and my social life centers around a small group of friends from work who meet up for board games or a few beers at the local brewpub.

## Politics and this election

I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus. The ads were all the same—talking about “draining the swamp” or “building a better future,” but none of it seemed to touch my day‑to‑day concerns. Between juggling a full‑time job, paying rent, and trying to stay on top of my loan payments, I just didn’t have the energy to go to the polls, and the ballot didn’t feel like it mattered for someone like me. I also thought the candidates were all part of the same establishment, just swapping slogans. That cynicism left me pretty disengaged, and I’ve been leaning toward sitting out again in 2026 unless something huge blows up locally—like a major factory closure or a scandal that directly hits my community.

I’m sitting this one out. My top everyday worries are keeping up with rent and the rising grocery prices; the last time I went to the store, a loaf of bread cost $4.50, and it’s hard to watch that kind of inflation chip away at a modest paycheck. The other big thing is job security—our health network keeps cutting overtime, and I worry about what will happen if there’s a big budget cut.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with the Democrats. They keep talking about “student debt forgiveness” but never seem to understand the reality of a low‑income immigrant family trying to make ends meet. The Republicans, at least in Arkansas, feel more like the “local” side—they talk about keeping taxes low and protecting small businesses, which is something I can relate to. Still, I don’t feel any party truly represents the mix of tech‑savvy, middle‑class, immigrant‑background folks like me. It’s more like “pick the lesser of two evils” than genuine representation.
