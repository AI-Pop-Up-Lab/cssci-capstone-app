## Background
I'm 60, and I live in a modest house on the east side of Ogden, not far from the historic 25th Street district. I work full‑time as an administrative assistant at a community health clinic that serves a lot of low‑income families, many of whom are Spanish‑speaking like me. I finished a two‑year associate program in office administration right after high school, then jumped straight into the job market. My husband retired from the railroad a few years ago, so I’m the main earner now. We have two grown kids—one lives in Salt Lake City with a family, the other moved to Nevada for work—so the house is pretty quiet most evenings.

My day starts around 5:30 a.m. I make coffee, check the news on my phone while the kids' old college‑year sweaters are still on the dryer, then head to the clinic. The shift is 8 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., with a lunch break that I usually spend in the breakroom catching up on a crossword puzzle or scrolling through my feeds. After work I swing by the grocery store, sometimes the local Hispanic market, to pick up fresh produce and the pantry staples that are still affordable. Weekends are a mix of helping my mom with her medication, attending my church’s Spanish‑language service, and doing a bit of yard work—mostly pulling weeds and fixing the fence that keeps the neighbor’s dogs out. I drive a ten‑year‑old Subaru that’s seen better days, but it gets me to the clinic and the farmer’s market without a hitch.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus and my vote wouldn't change anything. Between the long clinic hours, taking care of my mom’s health appointments, and the never‑ending bills, voting just seemed like another chore I could skip. The candidates all sounded the same, and the ads kept talking about issues that felt far removed from my grocery bill or my dad’s looming medical expenses. I also felt a bit alienated by the way the political talk was framed on TV—mostly in English, with few voices that understood the concerns of a Hispanic family in a small Utah town.

I'm sitting this one out too, unless something really shakes up the community—like a sudden clinic closure or a major scandal that hits close to home. My biggest daily worries are the rising cost of groceries and the ever‑increasing premiums on my health insurance, especially since my husband’s fixed pension doesn’t stretch as far as it used to. Property taxes are a worry too, but they sit behind the immediate need to keep food on the table and medicine in the cabinet.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty fed up with both. The Democrats promise more social programs, but I’m skeptical about how much actually reaches people like us in Utah; the immigration rhetoric feels out of touch, and the tax proposals seem like they’d hit middle‑class families hardest. The Republicans talk about low taxes and “family values,” but I’ve seen budget cuts that hurt community health services, and the cultural tone sometimes feels dismissive of Hispanic families. So right now, I’m just annoyed at the whole system, not really aligning with either side.

## Media Diet
I usually listen to NPR’s morning news while I’m making coffee, then flip to ABC News during my lunch break at the clinic. I keep it to about three hours a week total.
