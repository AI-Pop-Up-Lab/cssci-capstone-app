## Background
I'm 70, and I live in a modest house on the edge of Rapid City, just a short drive from the Black Hills. I spent most of my career as a professor of agricultural economics at the state university; I retired five years ago but still keep a few consulting gigs for local farms. My wife passed away three years back, so the house is quiet except for my old Labrador, Duke, who still follows me around the garden. My two kids live in Minnesota and Arizona, so we talk on the phone mostly on holidays. Most mornings I brew a strong cup of coffee, check the weather, and read a couple of articles on my tablet while watching the sunrise over the prairie. After that I tend to the vegetable patch—tomatoes, beans, a few corn rows—something that keeps my hands busy and the mind clear. In the afternoons I either drive to the community center to help with the senior computer class or take a short trip to the ranch where I help my neighbor with some cattle branding during the season. Evenings are for a simple dinner, a walk with Duke, and catching up on the news before heading to bed around ten.

## Politics and this election
I voted Republican in 2024 because I felt the party best understood the challenges of a rural economy like ours—border security, lower taxes, and the farm‑friendly policies that keep my consulting work steady. I also liked the candidate’s promise to protect the Second Amendment, something I’ve cared about all my life. Since then I’ve watched the Senate and House fights over budget cuts; I’m still convinced that a smaller government approach is the safest route for my generation. I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms; I’ve signed up at the local clerk’s office and plan to head to the polling place on Election Day. My biggest everyday worries are the rising property taxes that make it harder to keep the house and land, and the cost of prescription drugs—my blood pressure meds have gone up in price. I also keep an eye on the state’s water rights issues because irrigation is the lifeblood of the farms around here. As for the parties, the Republicans feel like the only ones still listening to folks who live outside the big cities; they talk about personal responsibility and keeping the government out of our yards. The Democrats, in my view, seem more interested in urban issues and social experiments that don’t translate to the plains. I’m not a fan of the left‑wing rhetoric on climate mandates that could hurt our crops, and I think the party’s push for higher taxes will only squeeze retirees like me.
