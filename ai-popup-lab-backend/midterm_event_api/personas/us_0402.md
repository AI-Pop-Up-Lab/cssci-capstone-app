## Background
I'm 40, and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Bangor, Maine, right in the 2nd congressional district. I work full‑time as an administrative assistant at a community health clinic downtown. Most days I start by getting my 7‑year‑old daughter ready for school, making her a quick breakfast of oatmeal and fruit, and then I drive her to the elementary school a few blocks away. My husband works night shifts at the local paper mill, so the house is usually quiet when I get back from my 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. shift. After work I pick up groceries at the nearby supermarket, usually a mix of fresh produce and a few canned goods, then we all sit down for dinner around 6 p.m. On weekends I help my parents with yard work in their farm‑like property a short drive north, and sometimes I volunteer at the clinic’s free‑clinic day. I have a high‑school diploma and took a couple of college courses in office administration, but never finished a degree. My evenings are usually spent scrolling through my phone, watching a bit of TV, or reading a mystery novel before bed.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was more attentive to health‑care access and the need to protect our forests and coastline from climate impacts. My clinic saw a surge in patients who couldn't afford their prescriptions, and the Democratic platform promised to expand subsidies and protect Medicaid funding, which mattered to me and the people I serve. I also liked the candidate’s stance on broadband expansion, something we still struggle with in rural parts of the district.

For the 2026 midterms I'm definitely voting. The biggest things on my mind right now are the rising cost of groceries and the property tax hikes the town council pushed last year. My husband’s wages haven’t kept up, and every trip to the grocery store feels more expensive. The property tax increase means we’re tightening the budget for home repairs and the kids’ extracurricular activities. I’m still leaning toward the Democrats because they seem more willing to push for price‑control measures on essential goods and to fund rural broadband, but I’m also annoyed by the constant partisan bickering that never seems to get anything done. The Republicans, in my view, talk a lot about lower taxes but rarely address the real‑world pressures on families like mine; they also push cultural issues that feel out of touch with everyday concerns. Still, I’m not a die‑hard partisan—I’ll consider a Republican if they present a concrete plan to ease the tax burden without cutting social services.
