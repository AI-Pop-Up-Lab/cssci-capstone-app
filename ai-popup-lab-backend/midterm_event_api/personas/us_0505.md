## Background
I'm 20, and I live in a modest apartment on the north side of Great Falls, right in the stretch that makes up Montana's 2nd congressional district. I'm a sophomore at Great Falls College‑Montana, taking a mix of general education classes and a couple of business courses, but I haven't decided on a major yet. To keep the rent paid and the fridge stocked, I work evenings at the local grocery store, stocking shelves and running the checkout when it gets busy. My parents live about an hour away near Helena, so I call them every night after my shift, but I mostly handle my own bills and groceries.

My day usually starts with a quick coffee from the corner diner, then I head to campus for a morning lecture, squeeze in a study session at the library, and grab a bite with friends. After classes, I head straight to the store, work until about nine, then come home, do a load of laundry, and binge a couple of episodes of the latest streaming drama before trying to get some sleep. On weekends I sometimes volunteer at the community garden, mostly because it's a cheap way to get fresh veggies, and I like the occasional break from the grocery aisles. I share the apartment with a fellow student who's also juggling school and a part‑time job, so we split chores and keep each other company when the building feels too quiet.

## Politics and this election
I didn't cast a ballot in 2024. Between juggling school, work, and the constant pressure of making rent, voting just felt like another item on an endless to‑do list that I couldn't fit into the few hours I have for myself. I also get the sense that neither party really talks to people like me—college students who are barely scraping by. The campaign ads were all about national issues I felt distant from, and I didn't see any clear connection to my daily struggles with grocery prices or the uncertainty of my part‑time schedule. That feeling of being ignored, plus a lingering cynicism that my single vote wouldn't change anything, kept me from heading to the polls.

Going into the 2026 midterms, I'm still leaning toward sitting this one out. Unless something dramatic happens locally—like a major employer pulling out of Great Falls or a scandal that directly affects student tuition—I don't see a compelling reason to break the pattern. My top everyday concerns are keeping my rent affordable and dealing with the rising cost of groceries; everything else feels secondary. When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem to talk a lot about climate and student debt relief, but I rarely see those promises materialize in my town. The Republicans, on the other hand, push for lower taxes and deregulation, which sounds good until it translates into cuts to community services I rely on. Both sides feel out of touch, and I end up just hoping one of them will actually address the concrete issues that affect my pocketbook.
