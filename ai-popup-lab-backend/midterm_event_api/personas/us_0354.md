## Background  
I'm 30 and work as a junior software developer for a midsize tech firm that does contract work for a few regional hospitals. I live in a two‑bedroom apartment on the west side of Shreveport, in the part of the city that falls in LA‑4. My roommate, also a coder, and I split the rent and utilities, which keeps the monthly cost around $1,200. My parents immigrated from Vietnam and live in Houston; I fly home for holidays and occasionally help them with paperwork, but most of my time is spent at the office or at the coworking space downtown when the office is quiet.

My day starts at 7 am with a quick coffee and a scan of the NPR morning brief on my phone while I wait for the bus. I work from 9 am to 5 pm, mostly writing code, debugging, and attending short stand‑up meetings. After work I either hit the gym for a half‑hour or play a few rounds of League of Legends with friends online. Dinner is usually cheap take‑out or a frozen meal because cooking feels like a chore after a long day. On weekends I try to catch up on errands—grocery shopping, laundry, and the occasional meetup with other Asian‑American friends at a local dim sum spot. I’m not married, I don’t have kids, and I’m still figuring out whether I want to buy a house here or move to a bigger city for career growth.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in the 2024 presidential election. Honestly, I felt like none of the choices represented anything that mattered to my day‑to‑day life. Between long hours at work, trying to save enough for a down payment, and dealing with a rent that keeps creeping up, politics felt like a distant, noisy backdrop. I also got the sense that my vote wouldn’t change the outcome—my district leans heavily Republican, and the national headlines were all about drama, not the concrete issues I care about. That cynicism, plus a packed schedule of overtime and a few personal health hiccups, meant I just skipped the ballot.

I’m sitting this one out again for the 2026 midterms. Unless there’s a local scandal that directly hits my community—like a school closure or a major employer pulling out of Shreveport—I don’t see a compelling reason to make the trip to the polls. My top everyday worries are keeping up with rent and the rising cost of groceries; everything else feels secondary. I also worry a bit about the tech job market; the company I’m at has been talking about layoffs, and I don’t have a big safety net.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats seem to promise a lot of social programs but rarely address the immigration‑related paperwork headaches my family still deals with, and their tax proposals feel like they’d push more burden onto middle‑class earners like me. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep pushing for cuts to social safety nets and talk about “law and order” in ways that feel dismissive of the discrimination my community sometimes faces. Neither side feels like they’re listening to the practical concerns of a 30‑year‑old coder trying to make ends meet.

## Media Diet
