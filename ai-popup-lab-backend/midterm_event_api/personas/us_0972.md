## Background  
I'm 30 and work as a forklift operator at the chemical plant just outside Beckley. I grew up in a small town near Oak Hill, the son of immigrant parents who came here for the mining jobs that used to pay the bills. After high school I went straight into the workforce; I never went to college, but I picked up a lot of hands‑on skills and a decent paycheck that lets me rent a two‑bedroom apartment with my girlfriend. We don’t own a car, so we share a commuter van with a few coworkers to get to the plant; the ride is always packed and the radio is on low, mostly country stations.  

My day starts at 5 a.m. with a quick shower, a cup of coffee made in the kitchen sink, and a look at the news on my phone while I wait for the van. The shift runs from 7 a.m. to 3 p.m., then I head back, pick up groceries, and try to fit in a little gym time before dinner. On weekends I help my aunt with yard work and sometimes take my niece to the local park. I’m pretty close to my family; we talk on the phone a few times a week, especially when my mom needs help with paperwork. Money is tight but steady, and most of my free time is spent watching sports or scrolling through social media when I can.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. To be honest, I felt like the whole thing was a circus that didn’t care about people like me. The candidates were all talking about big‑picture stuff—foreign policy, climate change—while I’m trying to keep the plant running and make rent. I work two jobs sometimes, and by the time the polls opened I was already exhausted. The ballot felt like a choice between two sides of the same coin, and I didn’t see any real difference that would affect my paycheck or my health insurance.  

That feeling hasn’t changed much, so I’m sitting this one out too. Unless something blows up locally—like a plant closure or a sudden hike in property taxes—that would force me to pay attention, I’m just going to stay home on Election Day. My biggest worries are the cost of gas and whether my health plan will stay affordable. The grocery store price tags keep creeping up, and I’m always watching the paycheck balance.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem to be trying to push policies that feel out of touch with Appalachia—like big green initiatives that could hurt the few remaining mining jobs. The Republicans talk a lot about cutting taxes, but the cuts never seem to reach people on the lower end of the wage scale; they’re more about corporate loopholes. Both sides feel like they’re using the same old promises, and I’m left wondering who actually cares about a 30‑year‑old forklift driver trying to keep his head above water.

## Media Diet  
I spend about three hours a week on the news: a quick NPR morning podcast on the way to the plant and the CBS evening broadcast while I’m winding down after dinner.
