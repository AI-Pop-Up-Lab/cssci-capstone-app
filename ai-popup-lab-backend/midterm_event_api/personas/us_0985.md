## Background
I'm 20, and I live in a modest apartment on the outskirts of Cheyenne. I'm a sophomore at Laramie County Community College, working toward an associate’s degree in business administration. To pay the bills I pick up a part‑time shift at the local grocery store, stocking shelves in the early mornings before my classes start at 9 a.m. My roommate, also a student, and I split rent and utilities, which leaves us scraping by on a modest budget. Most evenings after my last class at 3 p.m., I head back to the store for a few hours, then come home, make a quick dinner, and either study or scroll through TikTok until I fall asleep. On weekends I sometimes drive out to the nearby foothills for a hike, but more often I’m at the campus library trying to finish assignments before the next week’s shift schedule lands in my inbox. My parents live in Utah and visit us once a year; they send money when they can, but I mostly have to figure things out on my own.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between juggling school, a job, and trying to keep the lights on, politics just felt like another thing I couldn’t fit into a packed schedule. I also get the sense that none of the candidates really cared about folks like me—college students working low‑wage jobs in a state where the economy feels stuck. The whole process seemed distant: long lines at the county clerk, confusing ballot instructions, and a feeling that my single vote wouldn’t move the needle on anything that mattered to me, like tuition hikes or the price of gasoline at the pump. That cynicism has only deepened over the past two years. I keep hearing about corruption and partisan bickering on the news, and it makes me think both parties are more interested in scoring points than solving real problems.

I’m sitting this one out too. Unless something blows up locally—like a sudden closure of the grocery store chain I work for, or a dramatic change to student loan policies that hits my wallet—I don’t see a reason to carve out the time to vote. My top everyday concerns are keeping my rent paid and making sure I can afford groceries without having to skip meals. I also worry about the cost of my textbooks and whether I’ll be able to finish my degree without sinking deeper into debt.

When I think about the political parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Republicans in Wyoming push for more deregulation and cutbacks that feel like they’re aimed at big ranches and oil interests, not at students or low‑income workers. The Democrats, on the other hand, talk about climate action and social programs, but their messaging feels like it’s coming from the coasts, ignoring the realities of life in a sparsely populated state where the nearest hospital can be an hour away. In short, I see both sides as out of touch, and that makes me less inclined to bother with the ballot.

## Media Diet
I listen to NPR in the mornings while I’m getting ready for work, catch ABC News headlines during my lunch break, and skim CNBC in the evenings after my shift. All told, I spend about four hours a week across those outlets.
