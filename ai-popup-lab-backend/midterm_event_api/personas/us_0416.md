## Background
I'm 35 and work as a diesel mechanic at a regional trucking company in South Portland. I went to a community college for two years, got my A‑A in automotive technology, and have been on the road for the last eight years. I live in a two‑bedroom apartment with my wife and our six‑year‑old daughter. Most mornings I’m up before the sun, making coffee while the kettle whistles, then loading the truck with pallets of frozen seafood for the day’s deliveries. I drop the kids off at the local elementary school, grab a bagel at the corner diner, and hit the highway. After a twelve‑hour shift I come home around 8 p.m., try to help with homework, and we all eat dinner together—usually something quick like stir‑fry or frozen pizza. On weekends I do a bit of yard work, run errands at the grocery store, and try to catch a baseball game on the TV when I can. My parents live in a small town up the coast; we visit them a few times a year, especially for the lobster bake. I’m not heavily involved in community groups, but I do vote in the occasional local school board meeting when it’s convenient.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between long hours at the shop, the kids’ school schedule, and the feeling that none of the candidates really cared about a mechanic trying to keep a roof over his family’s head, I just let the day pass. The whole process felt like a circus—ads screaming about “change” while the policies they talk about never touch the price at the pump or the cost of a decent health plan for a blue‑collar family. I was also skeptical about the mail‑in ballot system; I thought my vote would get lost in the shuffle or be counted twice. That cynicism has stuck, so I’m sitting this one out too, unless something dramatic hits my town—like a major factory closing or a sudden spike in property taxes that forces us to move.

My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of groceries and the price of gas. Every time I pull into a gas station, the numbers climb a few cents higher, and it eats into my take‑home pay. The grocery aisles feel like a minefield of price tags; even basic staples like milk and rice have jumped noticeably since last year. I’m also watching the local property tax bill; if they raise it again next year, it could push us out of our apartment and into something more expensive.

When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem to talk a lot about climate and social programs, but their proposals often end up as taxes that hit working families like mine. The Republicans push for lower taxes and deregulation, but they rarely address the real cost of living in a place where everything—fuel, food, housing—already feels pricey. Both sides feel like they’re speaking to a different audience, and I’m left listening to the noise.

## Media Diet
I start my day with NPR’s morning news while I’m sipping coffee, then in the evening I scroll through CBS News and CNBC on my phone to see how the markets and any big economic stories are shaping up.
