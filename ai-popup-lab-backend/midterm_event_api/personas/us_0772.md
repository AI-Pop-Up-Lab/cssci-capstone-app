## Background
I'm 35, and I live in North Providence, Rhode Island, in a modest two‑bedroom apartment that I share with my wife and our eight‑year‑old son. I went to a community college and earned an associate’s degree in information systems, then landed a job as a computer repair technician for a small local shop that services schools and small businesses. My day starts around 6 a.m. with a quick coffee and a scan of the news on my phone while I watch the morning feed from NPR. I head to work at 7:30, spend most of the day diagnosing hardware problems, ordering parts, and sometimes helping a kid from a nearby high school who’s doing a tech internship. The shop closes at 5 p.m., so I’m usually home by six, helping my son with his homework and cooking dinner with my wife, who works part‑time at a nearby grocery store. On weekends we try to squeeze in a trip to the farmer’s market and a few hours of baseball at the local park, but most evenings are spent on the couch catching up on the evening news or scrolling through social media. I don’t drive a fancy car—just a used sedan that’s been in the family for a few years, and I’m always watching the gas gauge.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic candidate in 2024 because I felt the party was more attentive to the cost‑of‑living pressures my family faces, especially the rising rent in Providence and the price of groceries. The candidate’s platform on expanding broadband in underserved neighborhoods resonated with my work in tech, and I liked the promise of more funding for community colleges, which would help people like my younger brother who’s still figuring out his path. I also appreciated the candidate’s stance on climate resilience, given how often we see flooding in our coastal town.

Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how the administration has handled inflation and the supply‑chain issues that still make it hard to stretch a paycheck. I’m still leaning Democratic, but I’m a bit wary of the internal squabbles in the party and the way some progressive proposals feel disconnected from the everyday grind of a working‑class family. The Republicans, on the other hand, still seem focused on tax cuts for the wealthy and deregulation that I worry will make housing even scarcer. I don’t feel represented by their rhetoric about “law and order” when the real order I need is a stable rent price and reliable public transportation.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. My top concerns right now are keeping rent affordable and making sure grocery prices don’t keep climbing faster than wages. I’m also watching property tax proposals in the state legislature because they could affect my wife’s part‑time job and our ability to save for my son’s college fund. The parties feel like they’re playing a game of who can promise the most, and I’m just hoping the one I pick this time will actually follow through on the basics that matter to my family.
