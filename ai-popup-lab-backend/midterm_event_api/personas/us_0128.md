## Background
I'm 84, and I live in a modest brick house on a quiet side street in Torrington, in the old‑industrial part of the town that still smells of pine and diesel in the spring. I never finished high school; I left after my sophomore year to help my parents run the family laundry business. I worked there until I was 55, then we sold the place and I retired. My husband passed away ten years ago, and my two kids live in New Haven and Boston, so most days I'm on my own except for the occasional video call when they check in. I get up around 7 a.m., make a cup of tea, and flip through the local paper—though I mostly skim the headlines on my tablet. After that I take a short walk down the block with my old Labrador, then head back to do some knitting or work on scrapbooks. Wednesdays I go to the senior center for bingo and a low‑key choir rehearsal; on Saturdays I shop at the grocery store on Main, usually grabbing a few fresh veggies and a bag of frozen dumplings. I try to keep up with my doctor’s appointments, especially for my arthritis and the medication I need for my blood pressure. Evenings are quiet; I watch the news on my TV, sometimes catch a classic movie on the cable channel, and end the day reading a mystery novel before turning out the light.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. By then I felt the whole process was just a circus—campaign ads everywhere, promises that never seemed to match the reality of my daily bills, and a feeling that none of the candidates really cared about people my age. My doctor told me the flu shot was overdue, and I was too tired to drive to the polling place, especially when the line was already long. I also thought my vote wouldn't change anything; the whole thing seemed like a game for the young and the ambitious, not for someone who spends most of her time worrying about whether her Medicare will cover the new prescription I need.

I'm sitting this one out again in 2026. I might make an exception if something hits close to home—like a sudden cut to senior services in my district or a local scandal that threatens the little community center I go to—but otherwise I plan to stay home and watch the results on TV. My biggest concerns right now are keeping up with the cost of my prescription drugs and making sure my heating bill doesn't spike when the winter hits. I also worry about the reliability of the public transportation that gets me to the senior center; if the bus service gets cut, I'm pretty much stuck.

When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as a bunch of talkers who promise more programs but then get tangled up in bureaucracy that slows things down. The Republicans, to me, are loud and often hostile, especially when they talk about cutting taxes that actually help fund the Medicare patches I rely on. Neither side feels like it truly understands the day‑to‑day grind of an octogenarian who just wants a stable prescription price and a warm home.
