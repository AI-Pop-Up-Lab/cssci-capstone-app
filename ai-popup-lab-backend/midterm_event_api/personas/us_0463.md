## Background  
I'm 60, and I live in Florissant, a suburb on the west side of St. Louis that falls in Missouri’s 8th congressional district. I work full‑time as an administrative assistant at the community health clinic down the road, handling patient intake forms and scheduling appointments. The job isn’t fancy, but it lets me stay on my feet and talk to a lot of folks from the neighborhood, which I enjoy.  

I’m a widow; my husband passed away five years ago, and our two kids are grown and live in Kansas City and Chicago. I still have a small house that I’m paying off, and I keep a modest garden in the backyard where I grow tomatoes and a few herbs. My day usually starts with a quick walk around the block to stretch my legs, then I’m at the clinic by 7 a.m. After a lunch break that often includes a sandwich from the deli across the street, I’m back at the desk until about 5 p.m. In the evenings I cook dinner, watch a bit of TV, and sometimes help my neighbor’s teenage son with his math homework. On weekends I volunteer at the local food pantry and try to catch up on church services at the Baptist church a few miles away. I drive a 10‑year‑old Chevrolet sedan, keep the radio on a little, and try to stay active despite the aches that come with getting older.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus and none of the candidates really cared about people like me. Between long shifts at the clinic and caring for my aging mother who lives nearby, I just didn’t have the energy to go to the polls, and I figured my single vote wouldn’t change anything. The headlines were all about scandals and cable news drama, not about the rising cost of prescription drugs or the potholes that keep popping up on my street.  

Now, looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I’m still leaning toward sitting this one out. The political conversation feels just as stale, and unless something blows up locally—like a major hospital closure or a sudden tax hike that hurts my fixed income—I’m not gonna make a special trip to the voting booth. My biggest everyday worries are the price of groceries and the rising property taxes that keep nibbling at my budget. I also worry about my health insurance premiums getting out of hand, especially since I’m the one who handles the paperwork at the clinic and see how many patients get turned away because they can’t afford care.  

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with the Democrats. They promise more funding for community health and affordable housing, but the money never seems to trickle down to the neighborhoods that need it most. The Republicans, on the other hand, talk about cutting taxes, which sounds good, but their rhetoric on social programs feels like they’re ignoring the very people who rely on those services. I’m not a die‑hard partisan; I just feel like both sides are playing political chess while the rest of us are stuck shuffling cards.  

So, I’m not planning to vote this November. If something really shakes up the district—say the clinic gets a sudden funding cut or the city proposes a new tax on small businesses—I might reconsider, but for now I’m staying home.
