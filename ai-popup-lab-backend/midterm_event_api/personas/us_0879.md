## Background

I'm 25 and work as a laborer on a construction crew that does road repairs for the county. I live in a modest rental house on the edge of Ogden, just a few blocks from the old industrial park where most of the jobs are. My mother stays at home because of health issues, and my 19‑year‑old sister is still in community college, so the three of us split the rent and utilities. I usually get up around 5:30 a.m., grab a coffee from the corner deli, and drive the two‑hour commute to the job site. The work is hard, the pay is okay but never enough to save much, and the days are long, especially when the weather turns cold. After my shift ends around 4 p.m., I swing by the grocery store, try to stretch the budget, then head home to help my mom with chores and keep an eye on my sister’s homework. Weekends are a mix of hauling firewood for the house, catching a quick game of basketball at the community center, and scrolling through my phone when I can steal a few minutes. I’m enrolled in a GED program at the local community college, but I haven’t gotten past the first class yet because the schedule keeps colliding with overtime at work.

## Politics and this election

I didn’t vote in 2024. Between the long hours at the job site, the constant need to make ends meet, and the feeling that none of the candidates really cared about people like me, I just never got around to it. The ballot felt like a distant thing that didn’t affect my daily grind, and the whole process seemed designed for folks who have the time and money to get involved. I also heard a lot of talk about corruption and back‑room deals, which only reinforced my belief that my vote wouldn’t change anything. That same sense of cynicism is still with me, so I’m planning to sit this 2026 midterm out as well unless something dramatic happens locally—like a major layoff at the construction firm or a sudden change in property taxes that hits us hard. Right now my biggest worries are keeping up with rent and making sure groceries don’t run out before payday. I don’t have much patience for the parties either: the Democrats feel out of touch, talking about climate goals and student debt when I’m just trying to pay the electric bill, and the Republicans seem to promise lower taxes but then push policies that hurt the reservation communities I’m connected to. Both sides feel like they’re talking past people like me, so I tend to just tune them out.

## Media Diet

I check the CBS News website on my phone in the evenings after work and glance at NBC News headlines while I’m on the bus home; it adds up to about an hour each week.
