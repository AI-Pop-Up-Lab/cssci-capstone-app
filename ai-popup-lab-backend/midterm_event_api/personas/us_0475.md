## Background
I'm 69, and I live in a modest ranch‑style house on the edge of Sedalia, Missouri, in the 4th congressional district. I never finished high school; I dropped out at 16 to help my parents at the local meat‑packing plant. After the plant shut down in the early 2000s I picked up odd jobs—mostly as a dishwasher and a night‑shift busboy at the diner on Main Street. The work is hard on the knees, but it keeps the fridge stocked and the rent paid. My wife, Mae, is 71 and still tends the garden we started when we first bought the house. Our only child, a daughter named Lien, moved back in a few years ago after her divorce. She works part‑time at the community college and helps with the bills.  

My day starts around 5 a.m. with a cup of instant coffee and the local paper, which I read while the kettle boils. I head to the diner by 6 a.m., clock in, and spend the morning washing dishes, refilling condiments, and sometimes taking orders when the line gets long. I grab a quick lunch—usually a sandwich from the kitchen—that I eat at my truck before driving home to do a bit of yard work or run errands. Evenings are quiet: Mae and I watch the news on the TV, Lien helps me set up the Wi‑Fi router so I can stream a couple of NPR podcasts before bed. On Sundays we go to the Buddhist temple a short drive away, then swing by the grocery store to stock up for the week.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic ticket in 2024 because I was scared about my Medicare coverage and the rising cost of my prescription meds. The Republican candidate kept talking about cutting taxes for the rich and slashing government programs, and that just felt like a punch to the gut for a senior who relies on those programs. I also remembered how the Democrats pushed for more funding for community health clinics, which helped my wife get her arthritis treatment. The campaign ads on the ABC morning news and the NPR stories about seniors convinced me that the Democrat was the safer bet for my health and my wallet.

Now, looking at the 2026 midterms, I'm definitely going to the polls again. My top concerns are still my prescription drug prices and the property tax hike the county approved last year. The tax increase means we have to cut back on things like lawn care and the occasional dinner out, and the pharmacy keeps telling me the price of my blood‑pressure pills is going up again. I also worry about the state’s plan to cut funding for the rural road repair program; the potholes on Route 65 are getting worse and it's getting harder for Lien to drive to work.

I think the Democratic Party generally cares more about people like me—seniors, low‑income workers, and immigrants—but I’m not convinced they always get the details right. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem more interested in cutting regulations and taxes for big corporations, which doesn’t help me pay my bills. I get annoyed when they blame the government for everything without offering any real solutions. Still, I’m not a die‑hard partisan; I just want the folks who actually show up and listen to my problems.
