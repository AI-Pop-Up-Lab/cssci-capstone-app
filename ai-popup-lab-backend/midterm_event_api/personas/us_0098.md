## Background

I'm 55 and I live in the Santa Clarita suburb of Valencia, right in the middle of CA‑17. I work full‑time as a senior data analyst for a midsize tech company that does a lot of contract work for the aerospace sector. My day starts around 6 a.m. with a quick espresso while scrolling through the business section of CNBC on my phone, then I drop my two kids off at the high school across the street before hopping on the 101 to the office. I’m married; my wife runs a small boutique that sells handmade home décor, and we both juggle the usual bills, mortgage, and the occasional home‑repair project that always seems to pop up when the kids aren’t looking. On weekends I try to get the family to the nearby hiking trails or the local farmers’ market, but most of the time I’m just catching up on a Netflix series or fixing the leaky faucet in the bathroom. My parents live in a small town near Fresno, and I try to call them at least once a week, though the time difference and my work schedule make it a bit of a scramble.

## Politics and this election

I didn’t vote in 2024. Between the long lines at the polling place, the endless ads that all sound the same, and the feeling that none of the candidates really cared about the everyday grind of people like me, I just stayed home. The whole process felt like a performance—politicians promising big changes while the only thing that changed for me was the price of gas and my rent creeping up a few dollars each year. My job keeps me busy enough that I never found the time to make a convincing case to get to the polls, and honestly, I was skeptical that my vote would make a dent in the national picture.

I’m sitting this one out too. Unless something blows up locally—like a major development project that threatens our neighborhood’s green space or a sudden spike in property taxes—I don’t see a reason to line up at a voting booth. My top concerns right now are the cost of living, especially rent and utilities, and the reliability of my commute on the 101, which gets snarled up with construction almost every other week. I’m also watching the grocery bills; the price of fresh produce has jumped enough that I sometimes have to skip the organic aisle.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty fed up with both. The Democrats seem to spend a lot of time on identity politics and national scandals, while the Republicans keep pushing tax cuts that never really reach middle‑class families and ignore immigration reform, which matters to my extended family back in Mexico. Neither side, in my view, offers concrete solutions for the day‑to‑day pressures that keep me up at night. I’m not out of the game entirely—I still keep an eye on the news and talk with coworkers about what’s happening, but when it comes to casting a ballot, I’m leaning toward staying home again.
