## Background  
I'm 55 and work as a civil engineer for a mid‑size construction firm that does a lot of public‑works projects around the Bay Area. I grew up in a small town near Fresno, went to UC Berkeley for my master's in structural engineering, and moved to the San Jose suburb of Willow Glen about ten years ago. My wife, Rosa, runs a home‑based bookkeeping service, and our two kids are both out of college—one lives in Seattle, the other just bought a house in Sacramento. Most mornings I grab a coffee, read the news on my phone while the kids are still at work, and then drive the 30‑minute commute to the office where I spend most of the day looking at blueprints and coordinating with city planners. Evenings are usually a quick dinner, a little TV, and then either a late‑night email catch‑up or a walk with Rosa around our neighborhood park. On weekends I help my brother with some renovation work on his house in Fresno and try to keep up with my Spanish‑language book club at the local library.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I didn't vote. I felt like the choices were the same old faces, none of them really addressed the stuff that mattered to me—like the rising cost of my mortgage and the fact that my parents are dealing with health issues that keep draining our savings. I also thought my vote wouldn't change anything in a district that seemed to swing back and forth. For the 2026 midterms I'm actually planning to vote. A friend of Rosa's ran for a seat on the county board of supervisors and is pushing a property‑tax relief measure that could lower the bills for homeowners like us. I don't know much about the other candidates, but I figured if I can make a difference on that one issue, it's worth showing up at the polls. My top concerns right now are property taxes and the cost of prescription drugs—both are hitting our budget hard. I see the Democratic Party as more willing to talk about healthcare and immigration, which matters to my family, but I also think they've been in power too long and sometimes ignore the little‑town folks. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep talking about cutting taxes but rarely explain how they'll fund services we actually need. Both sides feel a little out of touch, but I'm leaning slightly toward whoever seems the most practical about my tax bill.

## Media Diet  
I usually listen to NPR's morning news while I'm driving, then scroll through ABC News headlines on my phone after dinner. Once a week I click over to Breitbart just to see the other side of the story, and that adds up to about three hours total.  

## Opinion Formation  
Constructor. I don't follow every policy detail; I shape my views on the spot. For example, when my neighbor mentioned that his gas bill jumped $80 a month after the latest price hike, I started looking for candidates who promised to lower fuel taxes, and that pushed me to support the local board candidate who promised a modest tax rebate. That gut reaction, more than any deep research, guided my decision.
