## Background  
I'm 75, and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Paducah, right in Kentucky's 6th district. I never finished high school; I left after sophomore year to help my parents run a small grocery store. When the store closed in the early 1990s, I picked up odd jobs—painting houses, fixing fences, loading trucks for the local warehouse. Now I'm mostly retired, but I still do a few handyman gigs for neighbors to keep a little cash coming in and stay busy.  

My wife, Margaret, is 73 and we’ve been together for over 50 years. We both grew up in a Korean‑American family that moved here when we were kids, so the community is a mix of Southern hospitality and the occasional Korean dish we still make for holidays. Our two kids live out of state—one in Ohio, the other in Tennessee—so most evenings are just the two of us watching the news, chewing on the same brand of crackers, and swapping stories about the old days.  

A typical day starts with a cup of black coffee on the porch while I read the paper (the print copy, though I also glance at headlines on my phone). I head out around 8 a.m. to check on a few repair jobs or to run errands at the hardware store. After lunch, I usually take a short walk to the local church, where I sit in the back pew and listen to the pastor’s sermon. In the late afternoon I’m back home, either tinkering with a neighbor’s broken fence or watching a game on TV. Dinner is simple—stir‑fried rice with whatever’s in the fridge, and then we settle in for the evening news or a political site on the laptop before bed.

## Politics and this election  
I voted Republican in 2024 because I felt the party cared more about keeping taxes low, supporting small‑business owners like the few of us who still do odd jobs, and standing up for what I see as traditional values. The Democratic candidates kept talking about big government solutions that never seemed to reach a place like Paducah, and I was tired of hearing about climate policies that would just raise my gas bill. I also liked the promise of a strong border and the emphasis on law‑and‑order, which felt like a return to stability after a chaotic few years.  

Looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I'm definitely going to the polls. My health isn’t perfect, but I’ve got a ride from my daughter when I need it, and I think it’s important to have a say, especially on the Senate race that could affect Medicare. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the fact that property taxes keep creeping up, making it harder to afford the house Margaret and I have lived in for decades. I’m also watching the price of gasoline; every time it spikes, it feels like a direct hit to my paycheck.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they’re trying to rewrite everything I know about my community—urban policies, progressive social agendas, and a big push for higher taxes to fund programs that never seem to help folks like us. The Republicans, on the other hand, still talk about cutting taxes and supporting veterans, which hits home for me. I’m not blind to their flaws; I’ve heard about some of their rhetoric getting a little too harsh, and I wish they’d focus more on healthcare for seniors. But overall, I trust them more to keep my taxes low and the government from getting too big.
