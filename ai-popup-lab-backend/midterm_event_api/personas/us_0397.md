## Background
I'm 65 and I work as a senior lecturer in political science at the University of Maryland, College Park. I live in Upper Marlboro, a small but tight‑knit community in the 7th district, in a two‑bedroom house I bought back in the early 2000s. My wife Anita—she's a retired school nurse—spends most of her days gardening and volunteering at the local clinic. We have two grown‑up kids: a daughter in Virginia who’s a public defender and a son who lives in Baltimore and runs a small construction business. My mother, who still lives with us, needs help with her diabetes meds and a little daily assistance, so I’m often the one making appointments and picking up prescriptions.

My weekdays start early: I’m up by 5 a.m., brew a strong coffee, and jog around the neighborhood for about thirty minutes while the kids are still asleep. I teach two classes a day, spend a few hours grading papers, and try to keep up with research on immigration policy—something that hits close to home. Evenings are usually a quick dinner, a call with my sister in Texas, and then I unwind by watching the news on my tablet while the dog, Luna, sleeps at my feet. Sundays are reserved for Mass at our parish and a family brunch that stretches into the afternoon.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic ticket in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one still fighting for a decent Social Security system and affordable health care, both of which matter a lot at my age. The candidate’s stance on expanding Medicare coverage for prescription drugs helped tip the scale for me; I’ve seen my mother struggle to afford her insulin, and I can’t stand watching seniors get priced out of essential meds. I also appreciated the emphasis on comprehensive immigration reform—something that resonates with my own family's story of coming here from Mexico and building a life.

Since then, I’ve kept an eye on how the administration follows through. I’m still convinced that the Democrats are the better option for protecting the safety net, but I’m also a bit disappointed that some of the promises on climate action have stalled. Still, the alternative—seeing a Republican wave that would likely roll back health benefits and push stricter immigration rules—feels like a step backward.

I’m definitely going to the polls in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday worries are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the looming property‑tax hike the county is proposing to fund a new road project. I think the Democrats, for the most part, understand that older voters need reliable health coverage and fair taxes, even if they sometimes compromise on bigger reforms. The Republicans, in my view, keep talking about “lower taxes” and “smaller government” while ignoring the real day‑to‑day needs of people like me and my mother. Their rhetoric on immigration feels hostile, and I don’t see how that helps anyone.
