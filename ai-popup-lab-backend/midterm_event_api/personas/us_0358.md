## Background
I'm 55 and I work as a forklift operator at a big distribution center just outside New Orleans East, in the part of the city that falls in LA‑5. I’ve been doing that job for about fifteen years, after a stint as a concrete laborer when I was in my twenties. My wife, Denise, works part‑time at a local salon and we’re both taking care of my mom who lives a few blocks away; she has diabetes and needs help with meds and meals. Our only child, a daughter, graduated from college three years ago and now lives in Houston with her own family, so the house is quieter but we still get visits on holidays.

My day starts around 5:30 a.m. I grab a coffee from a corner stall, listen to the local radio for traffic, then drive the half‑hour to the warehouse. My shift runs from 6 a.m. to 2 p.m., with a short lunch break where I usually eat a sandwich and catch up on the news on my phone. After work I swing by the church for the Sunday‑morning service on Saturdays (the choir needs extra hands) and sometimes help with the community food pantry. Evenings are mostly about cooking, watching a bit of TV, and scrolling through the news feeds before I go to bed around 10 p.m. I try to keep the house tidy, pay the bills, and make sure Mom gets her insulin on time.

## Politics and this election
I voted for the Democratic ticket in 2024 because I felt the party was the only one talking about things that mattered to me: protecting Medicare, funding community health clinics, and pushing for police reform after the incidents that hit our neighborhood last year. I also liked that the Democratic candidate promised more funding for infrastructure, which would mean better roads for my commute and more jobs at the warehouse. I wasn’t thrilled with any of the candidates, but I figured a vote for the Democrats was the safest bet to keep the social safety net from getting shredded.

Since then I’ve kept an eye on how things are going. The new administration has been slow on the promised Medicaid expansion in Louisiana, and the rising cost of groceries and rent has been squeezing my budget more than ever. On the other hand, I’ve seen some progress on the police‑community dialogue panels, and a few of the pothole‑filled streets near my home finally got repaved. Those little wins make me feel like my vote mattered a bit, even if the big picture still feels stuck.

I’m definitely going to the polls for the 2026 midterms. My top everyday worries are the price of food and the rent on our modest two‑bedroom place – we’re already looking at a possible increase next year. I also worry about the reliability of the power grid after the last summer’s blackouts. When I think about the parties, the Democrats seem like the only ones trying to address those concerns, even if they’re often all talk and not enough action. The Republicans, in my view, keep pushing for tax cuts for the rich and rolling back environmental regulations that hurt our coastal neighborhoods; that just feels like they’re ignoring the everyday grind of folks like me.
