## Background  
I'm 49, and I live in a modest house on the edge of Marion, Illinois, not far from the I‑57 corridor. I work as a middle‑school social studies teacher at the local public school, a job I got after finishing my bachelor's in history at a state university. My wife works part‑time at the county health department, and we have two kids: a 15‑year‑old son who’s in his sophomore year at the regional high school, and a 12‑year‑old daughter who’s in eighth grade. Most mornings I’m up by 5:30 a.m., make coffee, and drive the 15‑minute stretch to school while the radio plays classic soul. After a full day of teaching, grading, and supervising after‑school clubs, I head home, pick up the kids from their activities, and try to squeeze in a quick dinner before we all sit down for a little TV before bed. Sundays are the only day we get a break—church in the morning, a family barbecue with a few neighbors, and then a long walk around the park. I try to keep the house in order, but between the kids’ sports gear, my wife’s rotating shifts, and the occasional car repair, the house is never truly quiet. I’m also on the local PTA board, which means a few extra meetings in the fall and spring, and I occasionally volunteer to mentor students who are struggling with reading.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was the only one paying any real attention to public schools, health care access, and criminal‑justice reform—issues that touch my daily life as a teacher and a parent. The Republican platform seemed more focused on cutting taxes for big corporations and rolling back environmental regulations, which didn’t help my kids or my community. I was also encouraged by the candidate’s promise to increase funding for early‑intervention programs, something I see the need for in my own classrooms.  

Looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I’m definitely voting. My top concerns right now are the rising cost of groceries and the property‑tax hike the county approved last year, which is squeezing our family budget. I’m also watching the local job market; the manufacturing plant that used to be a steady employer for many families in the district has been cutting shifts, and I worry about what that means for the community’s stability. I still feel the Democratic Party is more in tune with my priorities—education funding, health‑care affordability, and a criminal‑justice system that doesn’t punish people for poverty. The Republicans, in my view, keep talking about “small government” while ignoring the everyday struggles of working families like mine. I’m not a political analyst, but I’m tired of hearing promises that sound good on the campaign trail and then disappear once the election is over.  

## Media Diet  
I usually catch the morning news roundup on NPR while I’m driving, then skim the ABC News website in the evening after the kids are in bed. Altogether it’s about three hours a week.
