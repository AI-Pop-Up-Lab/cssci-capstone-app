## Background  
I'm 60, and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Cheyenne, right in the heart of Wyoming's 1st district. I never finished high school; after a few odd jobs I settled into a full‑time gig as a home health aide, helping elderly folks with daily chores, medication reminders, and a bit of companionship. My days start early—around 5 a.m., I prep a quick coffee, check the weather on my phone, and head out to the first client’s home. Most mornings I’m driving a minivan, stopping at a diner for a cheap breakfast burrito before the first visit.  

After the morning rounds I usually have a short break, where I catch up on the news on my old tablet, scrolling through a couple of sites while the kids (my two adult children who live nearby) are at work. In the afternoons I’m back in the community, often running errands for my clients—picking up groceries, dropping off prescriptions, or just listening to their stories. I come home around 6 p.m., make a simple dinner, and then spend a little time on the porch watching the sunset over the high desert. My weekend is a mix of church service on Sunday, a family barbecue with my sister’s family, and occasional trips to the local Walmart for bulk supplies. I don’t have a lot of spare cash, so I keep a close eye on every bill, especially the rising cost of utilities and medical expenses.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, the whole thing just felt like a circus. Between working long hours, dealing with clients who need help at odd times, and trying to keep the lights on, I never found the energy to get to a polling place or even fill out a mail‑in ballot. The candidates all seemed to be talking past each other, and none of the promises really touched the things that keep me up at night—like the price of my medication or the fact that the nearest clinic sometimes runs out of basic supplies. I felt like my single vote wouldn’t change anything, so I stayed home.  

Looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I’m planning to sit this one out again. Unless something dramatic hits the local community—like a hospital closure or a sudden spike in property taxes—I don’t see a reason to carve out the time. My top everyday worries are the grocery bills, which keep creeping up, and my own health care costs, especially the co‑pays for the seniors I help.  

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty fed up with both. The Democrats come across as out of touch with rural life; they keep talking about climate policies that feel like they’d hurt the ranches and farms around here. The Republicans, on the other hand, brag about cutting taxes and “standing up for America,” but I rarely see any concrete help for people like me who are struggling to pay for basic health services. Both sides seem more interested in scoring points than solving the real, day‑to‑day problems that affect folks on the ground.
