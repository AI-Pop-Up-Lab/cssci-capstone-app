## Background  
I'm 71 and I live in a modest house on a quiet street in Westborough, right in the middle of Massachusetts’s 4th district. I spent most of my working life on the assembly line at a paper mill just outside Worcester; I retired ten years ago when the plant shut down. My wife, Margaret, and I have been married for 45 years. Our three kids are grown and live in Boston, Portland, and out in New Hampshire, so the house is pretty empty most days. I still get together with a few members of the local Wampanoag community for monthly powwows and occasional tribal meetings—those gatherings keep me connected to my heritage.  

My days are a mix of routine and small chores. I get up around 6 a.m., make coffee, and read the newspaper (the print edition of the local paper) while the kettle boils. After that I tend to the garden in the backyard—tomatoes, beans, a few herbs—and check on the chickens I keep for eggs. In the afternoons I either go for a walk around the neighborhood or stop by the senior center where I play checkers and watch the weekly bingo. Evenings are usually spent watching the news on TV, listening to a couple of radio talk shows, or chatting on the phone with my daughter about her kids. I drive a reliable old sedan that I keep in the garage, and I’m careful about my health—regular check‑ups, a low‑sodium diet, and a little bit of yoga I learned from a community class.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. By the time the primaries rolled around, I was feeling like the whole process was just a circus—campaign ads everywhere, promises that never seemed to match what I see in my own town. Between the long lines at the polling place and the feeling that none of the candidates really cared about issues like the rising cost of prescription meds or the declining number of jobs in our old manufacturing corridor, I just stayed home. I told myself that my single vote wouldn’t change anything, and the cynicism stuck.  

Now, looking at the 2026 midterms, I’m still leaning toward sitting this one out. The only thing that might pull me to the booth would be if a candidate from my district actually showed up at the senior center to talk about a concrete plan for expanding broadband in rural parts of the district, or if there were a ballot measure that directly affected property tax assessments on my home. My top everyday concerns are the price of groceries—especially fresh produce and meat—and the looming increase in property taxes that the town council keeps mentioning.  

When I think about the two major parties, I’m mostly annoyed with the Democrats because they seem to focus on big‑city issues and keep raising taxes that hit retirees like me. The Republicans, on the other hand, talk a lot about cutting taxes but rarely address the reality of healthcare costs for seniors. I don’t feel a strong loyalty to either side; I just want someone who will listen to folks living on a fixed income and stop the endless back‑and‑forth that never leads to real solutions.

## Media Diet  
I watch the evening broadcasts on ABC and CBS most nights after dinner, and I catch the morning news on NBC when I’m getting ready. I also tune into NPR’s hourly updates while I’m in the garden, adding up to about twelve hours a week total.
