## Background  
I'm 20 and I live in a small apartment complex on the edge of Leesburg, right in the middle of VA‑1. My parents moved here from Maryland when I was a kid, and after I graduated high school two years ago I stayed put instead of going to college. I work full‑time as a cashier at the local grocery store; the shift starts at 4 p.m. and runs until midnight, so most of my day is spent walking the aisles, stocking shelves, and dealing with the rush of weekend shoppers.  

When I'm not at work I usually catch the bus to the nearby community center where I take a few night classes in basic computer skills—just enough to keep my résumé looking decent. I share the apartment with a roommate from the Philippines who also works part‑time; we split rent and utilities, which means every month we have to stretch what little we earn to pay the $1,200 rent, internet, and the occasional grocery run.  

My daily routine is pretty set: I wake up around 8 a.m., make a quick bowl of instant noodles, scroll through my phone for a bit, then head to the community center for a class or a job‑search workshop. After class I swing by the library to print out resumes, then head home for a brief nap before my shift. On weekends I try to meet up with a few friends from high school at the local park, but most of the time I'm just exhausted from working late and trying to save enough for a car. I don't belong to any clubs or volunteer groups; my free time is mostly spent watching short videos on TikTok or listening to music while I clean the apartment.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my vote wouldn't change anything, and between school, work, and trying to make rent, I just didn't have the energy to get to a polling place or figure out the ballot. The whole process seemed like a hassle, and I kept hearing the same old promises from both parties, so I tuned out. The news kept talking about big national issues that felt far removed from my life in a tiny town where the biggest debate is whether the grocery store will stay open on Sundays.  

Going into the 2026 midterms, I'm still sitting this one out. Unless something blows up locally—like a sudden rent hike or a major store closure that hits my paycheck directly—I don't see a reason to drag myself to the polls. My top everyday concerns are making sure I can keep up with rent and utilities, and watching grocery prices creep up; the last thing I need is another bill I can't afford.  

When I think about the parties, I see Democrats as the ones who talk a lot about immigration and student debt but never seem to address the day‑to‑day grind of low‑wage workers like me. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep pushing tax cuts for the wealthy and talking about "law and order" while ignoring the fact that many of us are trying to stay afloat on minimum wage. Both sides feel like they're speaking past each other and past people like me. I get annoyed when politicians use buzzwords instead of offering any concrete help for renters or for people working two jobs. If I ever get motivated to vote, it'll be because a candidate actually mentions protecting affordable housing or raising the minimum wage in a way that sounds realistic.
