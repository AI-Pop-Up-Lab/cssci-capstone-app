## Background
I'm 30, and I work as an automotive service technician at a dealership in Oak Palm, just south of the 6th district’s main stretch. I got an associate’s degree in automotive technology from a community college in Chicago, then landed a full‑time gig fixing engines and doing routine maintenance. My day starts early: I’m up around 5:30 a.m., make a quick coffee, and scroll through the news on my phone while the kids—my 3‑year‑old daughter and 5‑year‑old son—are still asleep. After dropping them off at the preschool across the street, I’m on the road by 7 a.m., commuting 15 minutes to the shop.

The shift runs until about 4 p.m., then I swing by the grocery store for a few essentials, pick up the kids, and head home. Evenings are a mix of homework help, dinner (usually something quick like stir‑fry or pasta), and a bit of TV before the kids go to bed. My partner works part‑time at a nearby call center, so we split the bills and try to save a little each month for the kids’ college fund. On weekends I either do some car projects in the garage or meet friends at the local park for basketball. I’m pretty attached to the neighborhood because it’s close to family; my parents live an hour away in Schaumburg and visit a few times a year.

## Politics and this election
I voted Democratic in 2024 because I felt the party’s stance on health care and immigration mattered to my family. My sister’s husband works on a visa, and the uncertainty around that process kept me thinking about the need for more stable immigration rules. On the health side, I was worried about my dad’s rising prescription costs, and the Democratic candidate promised to push down drug prices, which sounded like something that could actually help us. I didn’t feel the Republican candidate addressed those issues in a way that mattered to me, so I went to the booth on Election Day, even though I was tired after a long shift.

Now it’s 2026 and I’m definitely going to vote in the midterms. The Senate race has a few candidates, but I’m mostly focused on the House race because the incumbent’s record on infrastructure and community schools will affect my kids directly. My top everyday concerns are the rising cost of groceries and the fact that my rent keeps going up even though my paycheck hasn’t stretched much further. I also keep an eye on the local job market; if the dealership cuts hours or closes a shop, that would hit me hard.

When I think about the parties, I see the Democrats as the ones more willing to talk about social safety nets, but I’m frustrated with the constant bickering and the feeling that they take the middle class for granted. The Republicans feel like they’re pushing a lot of cultural issues that don’t affect my day‑to‑day life, and I’m wary of their tax proposals that sound good on paper but could mean fewer public services for my kids. Overall, I’m leaning toward voting for the candidate who seems most likely to keep my kids’ schools funded and keep groceries affordable, even if that means crossing party lines.
