## Background
I'm 35, and I live in a small apartment in Kakaʻako, just a few blocks from the waterfront in Honolulu. I work full‑time as a software engineer for a fintech startup that outsources a lot of its code to the mainland. My day usually starts with a quick surf session at Ala Moana Beach Park before I grab a coffee and scroll through the NPR morning news while the train rolls me into the office. After a nine‑hour shift, I often stay late to debug a build or finish a feature rollout, then head home to cook a simple dinner—usually rice, some fish, and a few veggies from the local market. My partner works part‑time at a community health clinic, and we split the rent and utilities; we don’t have kids yet, but we’re saving for a place with a little yard. On weekends I’ll hike up the Makapuʻu Trail or meet friends for a poke bowl, but most of my free time is spent on my laptop, either learning a new programming language or catching up on the latest tech podcasts. The cost of housing in Honolulu is sky‑high, so we keep a tight budget and keep an eye on any rent increase notices.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in the 2024 presidential election. Between the long commute, the late nights at work, and the feeling that neither party really addressed the housing crisis that’s squeezing us out of the city, I just didn’t see a point. The campaign ads felt repetitive, and the constant talking about national issues made me feel distant from the decisions that actually affect my daily life. I also thought my single vote wouldn’t change anything, especially when the polls kept flashing the same two names and the same talking points. That sense of cynicism stuck with me through 2025, and unless something dramatic—like a major development plan that threatens to displace thousands of renters, or a personal emergency that forces me to deal with government agencies—happens, I’m planning to sit out the 2026 midterms as well. My top concerns right now are the relentless rise in rent and the price of groceries; a sudden spike in either could force us to move off the island. As for the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats seem complacent about the cost‑of‑living crisis, assuming their progressive platform will sort itself out, while the Republicans keep talking about “fiscal responsibility” without ever acknowledging how unaffordable housing is for folks like me. I wish there were more candidates who actually talked about the local housing market, public transit improvements, and the way tourism impacts our everyday life.
