## Background  
I'm 45 and work as a computer repair technician for the community college in the little town of Moscow, Arkansas. My day starts around 5:30 a.m. with a quick coffee and a glance at the headlines on my phone before I hop on the 20‑minute drive to the campus. The job is steady but not glamorous; I spend most of my shift swapping out hard drives, troubleshooting network glitches, and teaching a few basic IT classes for non‑major students.  

I live with my wife and our two kids—14‑year‑old Maya, who’s gearing up for her first high‑school driver’s permit, and 10‑year‑old Ethan, who still asks for extra chicken nuggets at dinner. After work I usually pick Maya up from soccer practice, then we all head home for a family dinner that lasts longer than it should because we’re all scrolling through our phones at the table. Weekends are a mix of yard work, grocery runs, and the occasional church potluck. I try to keep the house in order; the roof needed a new shingle last summer, and we’re still saving up for a new fridge. I don’t travel much beyond the nearest city of Little Rock for a few errands or to see extended family once a year.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t cast a ballot in 2024. Between long work hours, the kids’ schedules, and a feeling that the candidates were more alike than different, I just never got around to it. The whole process feels like a show where the script is already written, and I’m not sure my vote changes anything. That cynicism has only deepened; the same old talk about “change” and “reform” never seemed to touch the problems we actually face in a small Arkansas town.  

I’m sitting this one out too. Unless something blows up locally—like a sudden factory closure that puts a lot of folks out of work, or a scandal that directly affects our community—I don’t see a reason to stand in line again. My biggest worries right now are the rising cost of groceries and the property tax bill that keeps creeping up each year. I also keep an eye on job security; the college where I work could see budget cuts, and there aren’t many other options around here.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they’re focused on big‑city issues and ignore the realities of rural life. The Republicans talk tough on taxes and regulation, which sounds good until I see them push for cuts that could hurt the college’s funding. Both sides have their flaws, but I’m more annoyed by the Democrats’ “talk‑down” attitude toward people like me who live in a place that isn’t a metro hub.
