## Background
I'm 45, and I live in Edmond, a suburb on the edge of Oklahoma City in the 3rd district. I work as a senior data analyst for a regional health‑care nonprofit; the job lets me stay mostly in the office but I’m on the road a few times a month for community health fairs. I have a master’s in public health, which I earned while juggling night classes and a part‑time gig as a tutor for undergrad students. My husband is a high‑school electrician, and we have two kids – a 16‑year‑old daughter who’s into skateboarding and a 12‑year‑old son who loves video games. Most mornings I drop the kids at school, grab a coffee at the local diner, and spend the commute listening to NPR podcasts. After work I’m usually home by six, cooking dinner while my husband helps the kids with homework. Weekends are a mix of soccer practices, grocery trips to the big box store, and trying to keep the house from looking like a disaster zone. I’m a member of the local PTA, but I only attend when there’s a bake‑sale or a big budget vote.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my ballot didn’t matter – the headlines were a nonstop loop of scandal, and the candidates all sounded the same, just different flavors of the same old promises. Between my job, the kids’ schedules, and trying to keep the house from falling apart, I just didn’t have the energy to dig into the issues. I also get a lot of “your vote won’t change anything” vibes from friends who think the system is rigged. That cynicism has stuck with me, so I’m sitting this one out as well unless something blows up locally, like a school closure or a sudden spike in property taxes that hits us hard.

My biggest everyday worries are the grocery bill and the property tax bill. Prices at the supermarket have been creeping up for the past couple of years, and every time the county reassesses the house it feels like we’re paying more for the same roof. I’m also watching healthcare costs; my wife’s union contract covers a lot, but the co‑pays keep nudging us toward the edge. When I think about the parties, the GOP feels like it’s drifting farther right than I’m comfortable with – the social issues and the talk about cutting education funding worry me. The Democrats, on the other hand, seem to ignore the realities of life in a place like Oklahoma; they talk big about climate policy and student debt, but I’m more concerned about whether my kids will have a stable job after high school. Both sides feel out of touch, so I’m just trying to keep my head above water.
