## Background
I'm 25 and I live in a modest duplex on the edge of Bel Air, Maryland, in the 7th congressional district. My mom works part‑time at a nursing home, and my younger sister is still in high school, so the house feels crowded most evenings. I never finished high school; I dropped out at 16 to start working full‑time at a local warehouse after my dad got sick and the family needed money. The job is mostly loading and unloading pallets, standing on concrete for eight‑hour shifts, and occasionally driving the forklift. I get a handful of extra cash by doing gig‑economy deliveries on my old pickup truck when the warehouse schedule lets me. My days start around 5 am with a quick coffee from the gas station, then a commute of about 15 minutes on the highway, a long shift, and a few hours of scrolling through my phone before I crash. On weekends I hang out with a couple of buddies from the same shift, play pickup basketball at the community center, and sometimes help my mom with grocery runs. I don't have a college degree or any certifications—just on‑the‑job experience and a high school equivalency I picked up a few years back just to qualify for a few extra jobs.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like none of the choices mattered to someone like me. The campaigns were all TV ads about stuff I never see, and the ballot felt like a list of names I couldn't connect to my daily grind. I also missed the registration deadline because I was working two shifts and didn't think it was worth the hassle. The whole system feels distant; politicians talk about big‑picture stuff while I'm trying to keep the lights on and stretch my paycheck to cover rent and groceries. That feeling hasn't changed much, so I'm sitting this one out too. I'm not planning to go to the polls in the 2026 midterms unless something blows up locally—like a new development that threatens the neighborhood or a sudden spike in property taxes that hits my mom hard.

My top everyday concerns are keeping up with rent and the rising price of food. The warehouse pays the same as it always has, but everything else seems to cost more every month. I see the Democratic Party as a bunch of city‑slickers who push for policies that raise taxes and regulation, making it harder for small businesses and workers like me to get ahead. The Republicans, on the surface, talk about cutting taxes and supporting "the working man," but I rarely see those promises materialize in my town. Both sides feel like they're talking past people who actually have to make ends meet.
