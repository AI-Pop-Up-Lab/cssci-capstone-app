## Background  
I'm 65, and I live in a modest house on the edge of Gainesville, Georgia, in the 6th district. I grew up in a family that ran a small grocery store, and after high school I took a couple of years of community college classes in computer repair before I landed a job at the local hospital doing equipment maintenance. I’ve been there for almost three decades, mostly fixing MRI machines and the occasional busted ventilator. The work is steady, but the shift schedule is a pain—most weeks I’m on a rotating 12‑hour night shift, which means I sleep during the day and try to squeeze in errands before sunrise.

I’m married to a retired schoolteacher; we’ve been together for 42 years. Our two kids moved out after college—one lives in Atlanta, the other in Charlotte—so the house is mostly quiet now. On weekends I tend the backyard garden, grow a few tomatoes, and meet up with a few other retirees at the community center for bridge. I also volunteer a couple of afternoons a month at the local senior center, helping folks with basic computer tasks. My days are a mix of checking equipment logs, grocery trips, and watching the news while I wait for the next shift to start.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between the night shifts, caring for my wife’s arthritis meds, and trying to keep the house heated when the utility bill spikes, I just never felt the time or the energy to get to a polling place. The whole process feels like a theater—candidates promise change, but the same big‑ticket issues keep coming back, and I’m left paying the price. I’m not a cynic for the sake of being contrarian; I’m just worn out by a system that seems to ignore folks like me who work the night shift and can’t afford to take a day off just to cast a ballot.

I’m sitting this one out again for the 2026 midterms. Unless there’s a local issue that hits my doorstep—like a hospital budget cut that threatens my job, or a property tax hike that would force us to sell the house—I’m not going to rearrange my schedule. My top everyday worries are the rising cost of health care (my wife’s prescriptions keep going up) and the property taxes that keep climbing even though my pension is fixed. As for the parties, I’m fed up with the Democrats acting like they’re the only ones caring about seniors; they talk a lot about climate and student debt, which feels distant from my reality. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep shouting about tax cuts but rarely address the health‑care costs that bite me every month. Both sides seem to forget the people who keep the lights on at night and the hospitals running.

## Media Diet  
I usually start my day by scrolling through NPR’s morning newsletter while I sip coffee, then I catch up on ABC News videos during my lunch break, and in the evenings I watch a half‑hour of CNBC highlights to see how the markets are doing. All together it adds up to about ten hours a week.
