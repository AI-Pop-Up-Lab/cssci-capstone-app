## Background
I'm 35 and I live in Grand Junction, right on the edge of the desert where the mountains start to climb. After getting an associate degree in automotive technology at a community college in Colorado, I took a job as a service manager at a local repair shop. The shop's owner is a family friend, so I end up pulling extra shifts when we get a rush of tourists fixing up their rental trucks. My parents moved here from Taiwan about ten years ago; they run a small grocery store on the same street, and I help out on weekends stocking shelves and dealing with the occasional language mix‑up with customers. I'm single, but I share a two‑bedroom apartment with my roommate, a fellow mechanic who loves mountain biking. Most mornings I grab a coffee at the diner across the street, read the headlines on my phone while the barista flicks on the TV, then head to the shop for the 8 a.m. shift. Evenings are usually spent scrolling through repair manuals, fixing up my own bike, or watching a game on the couch. On Sundays I drive out to the nearby mesa for a hike, then swing by the family store to help close up. Money's tight enough that I keep an eye on rent and car payments, but the work is steady and the community feels close‑knit.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for a third‑party candidate—someone from the Green Party who promised stronger action on climate and more support for small businesses like my parents'. I was fed up with the usual two‑party dance; the major candidates both sounded like they were talking past each other, and none of them seemed to care about the water shortages we face out here or the rising cost of parts for my shop. I didn't think a vote for a major party would change anything, so I went with the alternative to send a message. That decision felt like a small protest, even if I knew the chances of that candidate winning were slim.

Now, looking at the 2026 midterms, I'm planning to vote again, but I'm still leaning toward a third‑party or at least a less‑established candidate. My biggest worries are the price of gasoline and the looming shortage of skilled technicians—if wages don't go up, shops like mine will struggle to keep staff. I also worry about the local water rights issue; the river's flow has been dropping every summer, and the state hasn't done enough to protect it. I see the Democrats as more willing to talk about climate, but sometimes they push policies that feel like they ignore the practical needs of small towns. The Republicans, on the other hand, keep talking about deregulation, which sounds good for business until you realize it can mean fewer protections for workers and the environment. Honestly, I'm pretty annoyed with both parties for treating Colorado as a background market rather than a place with real people and real problems. I'm going to the polls because I want to keep the pressure on, but I'm not hopeful that either major party will give me what I need.
