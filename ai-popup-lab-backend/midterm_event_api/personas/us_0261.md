## Background
I'm 45, and I work as a public‑health analyst for the Illinois Department of Public Health. My office is in Springfield, but I actually live in the suburb of Normal, a short drive north on I‑55. I’m married to a high‑school teacher, and we have two kids—Mia, 13, and Luis, 10. Most mornings start with a quick coffee while scrolling through NPR headlines on my phone, then I drop the kids off at school, hop in the car, and spend about an hour on the highway. The commute gives me time to listen to a CNBC podcast about the latest market moves, which I try to follow because the budget cuts we see at work affect my projects. After a nine‑to‑five day of data analysis, report writing, and occasional meetings with local health officials, I pick up the kids from soccer or after‑school tutoring, help with homework, and try to squeeze in a grocery run before dinner. Weekends are a mix of family errands, a bit of yard work, and catching up on a few episodes of a streaming drama. I’m fluent in Spanish and English, which comes in handy at work and at home, but I don’t consider myself a political junkie—most of my news consumption is pragmatic, just enough to stay informed for my job and my budget.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in the 2024 presidential election. Between a demanding job, kids’ schedules, and feeling like neither party really addressed the concerns of my community—especially the rising cost of prescription meds and the lack of culturally competent health services—I just shrugged it off. The whole process felt like a circus, and I was convinced my single vote wouldn’t change anything. I also felt alienated when campaign ads ignored Hispanic families in the suburbs, focusing instead on urban issues that didn’t touch my daily life. That cynicism hasn’t faded; if anything, the endless stream of partisan attacks makes me think the system is more about scoring points than solving problems. So for the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out as well, unless something dramatic—like a major hospital closure in my district or a direct threat to my kids’ school funding—pulls me back in.

My top everyday worries are the grocery prices, which have been creeping up since the pandemic, and the cost of health insurance for my family. The former hurts our budget line by line, and the latter is a constant source of anxiety because I see gaps in coverage that could leave us vulnerable. When I think about the parties, I’m mostly annoyed with the Democrats for promising immigration reform and better health services but never delivering concrete changes at the state level. The Republicans get my eye‑roll for talking about tax cuts while ignoring the rising cost of basic necessities like food and medicine. Both sides seem to think I’m a vote to be courted, not a constituent with real needs.
