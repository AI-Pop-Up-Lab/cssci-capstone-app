## Background
I'm 25 and I work as a logistics coordinator for a freight‑rail company that runs out of Cheyenne. My day starts around 6 a.m. with a cheap coffee from the diner on the corner, then a 15‑minute drive to the yard where I spend most of my morning tracking shipments and making sure the crew has the right paperwork. After a quick lunch—usually a sandwich and a bag of chips—I head back to the office for a few more hours of scheduling and data entry. When I clock out at 5 p.m. I hit the gym for an hour, then swing by the local grocery store to pick up a few basics before heading home.

I live in a two‑bedroom apartment with a roommate I met in college. We both went to the University of Wyoming, where I majored in business administration. My roommate works in a call center, so we mostly keep to ourselves, but we do catch a game at the bar on Fridays or fire up a console for a co‑op session on weekends. My parents live on a ranch about an hour out of town, and I visit them when I can, usually for holidays or when the weather makes the drive nice. I don't have kids, and I’m not married; my social life revolves around a small circle of friends from work and a few old college buddies who still text me about the latest memes.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I cast a ballot for the Libertarian candidate. I was fed up with the usual two‑party circus—both parties seemed to be shouting over each other while ignoring the real issues that affect folks like me out here, like federal land policies and the rising cost of gasoline. I didn’t expect the vote to change anything, but it felt good to register a protest and to remind the system that there are people who don’t fit neatly into the Democrat‑Republican binary.

Now that the 2026 midterms are coming around, I’m definitely going to vote. I’m not thrilled about any of the choices, but I think my vote can at least nudge the conversation toward the issues I care about. My top everyday concerns are the price of gas and how the Bureau of Land Management handles grazing permits on the open range. When the price at the pump spikes, it hits my paycheck hard, and when the BLM decides to open up more land for drilling, I worry about the impact on the environment and on the ranches my family depends on.

My view of the parties is pretty blunt. The Democrats act like they want to fix everything, but they keep pushing for more regulation that just makes my job harder and drives up costs. The Republicans claim they’re the “pro‑freedom” side, yet they rarely talk about the specific land‑use issues that matter to Wyoming’s ranchers and miners. Both seem more interested in national narratives than in the day‑to‑day reality of a small‑town logistics worker. I’m leaning independent now, hoping at least one candidate will actually listen to the concerns of people living on the edge of the great plains.
