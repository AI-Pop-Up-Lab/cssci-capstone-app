## Background  
I'm 30 and I work as a data analyst for a renewable‑energy startup that’s based out of Bend. Most days I’m on my laptop in the coworking space on 9th Avenue, pulling together performance metrics for a new solar‑farm project. I grew up on the Warm Springs reservation, so I still spend weekends helping my grandmother with paperwork and sometimes driving out to the reservation for family gatherings. My partner and I rent a two‑bedroom apartment a few blocks from the Deschutes River; we split the rent and the grocery bill, and we’ve been trying to save enough for a down‑payment on a house in the foothills.  

My mornings usually start with a quick run along the river trail, then I grab a coffee at a local café where the barista knows my order (black, no sugar). I check the weather, skim a couple of headlines on my phone, and head into work by 8:30 a.m. The job is pretty demanding—tight deadlines, lots of spreadsheet wrangling, and occasional late‑night calls when the wind turbines go offline. After work I either cook dinner with my partner, binge a true‑crime podcast, or head out for a hike on the nearby Cascades. Sundays are for chores, a video call with my aunt in Seattle, and sometimes a volunteer shift at the community garden we help run. I don’t drive a lot; most errands are a short bike ride or a quick Uber.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like the whole thing was a circus. Between long hours at the startup, caring for my grandma, and trying to keep my rent affordable, casting a ballot felt like a luxury I couldn’t afford. The candidates all sounded the same to me—big promises, vague talking points, and none of them seemed to address the real problems I face every day, like the rising cost of housing in Bend or the water restrictions that keep getting tighter. I also get frustrated with the endless ads and the feeling that my vote is just a drop in an ocean of indifference.  

That cynicism hasn’t gone away. I still keep an eye on the news when something big hits the headlines, but I’ve settled into the idea that my voice won’t change the system unless something hits close to home. So for the 2026 midterms, I’m sitting this one out too. Unless there’s a local issue that directly threatens my community—maybe a new mining proposal that would affect water quality, or a policy that would make rent control possible—I’m not planning to step into the booth.  

My everyday concerns are pretty concrete: keeping up with rent and utilities while my partner and I try to save, and making sure there are good jobs in the clean‑energy sector that actually pay a living wage. I also worry about the water situation; the river levels have been low for a few years, and that’s starting to hit both recreation and the local economy.  

When I think about the two major parties, I’m mostly annoyed. The Democrats seem to be all about grand social programs but rarely get the details right for places like eastern Oregon, and they push climate policies that feel disconnected from the realities of a rural, water‑scarce region. The Republicans, on the other hand, talk a lot about “freedom” and “small government,” but most of their rhetoric ignores the fact that many of us are trying to build a sustainable future and need support for things like renewable‑energy incentives and infrastructure. Neither side feels like they really get the mix of Native‑American heritage, tech‑industry work, and the practical concerns of a young family trying to put down roots.
