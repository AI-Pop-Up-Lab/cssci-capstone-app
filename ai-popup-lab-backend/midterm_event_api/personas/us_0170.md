## Background  
I'm 35 and I live in a modest townhouse in Fort Myers, right in the heart of Florida's 17th district. I work full‑time as a data analyst for a health‑tech startup that partners with the local hospital. My job mostly involves cleaning up data sets, building dashboards, and answering ad‑hoc questions from the product team. I graduated with a master's in health informatics, so most of my days are spent in front of a dual‑monitor setup, sipping cold brew and listening to the latest episode of an NPR wellness podcast.

I share the place with a roommate who works as a school teacher; we split the rent and take turns cooking dinner. On weekdays I leave at 7 am, catch the commuter bus to the office, and usually work until about 5 pm. After work I hit the gym for a quick spin class or run along the river trail. Evenings are a mix of grocery runs, scrolling through the news on my phone, and catching up on a Netflix series when I have the energy. Weekends are a bit more relaxed—I often help my mom, who lives a short drive away in Punta Gorda, with errands and sometimes pick up her prescriptions. When the weather's nice we grill on the patio or take short trips to the beach. I don’t have kids, but I do have a dog, a rescued Labrador named Maya, who keeps the house lively.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in the 2024 presidential election. Between a demanding work schedule, the constant stream of hurricane warnings, and a feeling that the two major parties were both talking past each other, I just never got around to filling out a ballot. The candidates seemed like they were promising the same things—lower taxes, better infrastructure—without any real plan that resonated with my day‑to‑day concerns. I also felt a bit cynical after seeing a lot of misinformation online and hearing friends say their votes didn't matter. So I stayed home, and I haven't been particularly motivated to change that habit.

For the 2026 midterms, I'm sitting this one out as well. Unless something dramatic happens—like a major misstep by my congressional representative on flood‑relief funding, or a local scandal that directly affects my community—I don't see a compelling reason to cast a ballot. My top everyday worries are the rising property taxes on my townhouse and the ever‑increasing cost of health insurance premiums. Both of those feel like they eat into my paycheck no matter what the national conversation is about. I also worry about flood insurance rates going up as sea levels rise; the threat of another big storm looms over every decision I make about home maintenance.

When I think about the parties, I'm pretty frustrated with both. The Republicans in Florida have pushed for deregulation that I think hurts the environment, and their stance on climate change feels out of touch with the reality of living on the Gulf Coast. The Democrats, on the other hand, seem more focused on identity politics and national narratives than on concrete solutions for small business owners and middle‑class families like mine. I don't feel like either side is giving me a clear answer to the problems that matter most in my daily life, which is why I keep my vote on the shelf.
