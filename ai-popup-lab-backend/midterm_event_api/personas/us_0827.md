## Background
I'm 60, and I live in Sevierville, right near the foothills of the Smokies in Tennessee's 5th District. I work full‑time as a floor supervisor at the local Walmart, mostly handling the grocery and household‑goods sections. My husband retired from the railroad a few years ago, so most of the money we bring home comes from my paycheck. We have two grown kids—one lives in Atlanta and the other in Nashville—so the house is pretty quiet during the day. I also help my mother, who lives a short drive away, with a few errands and occasional meals because her health has been slipping.

My mornings start with a strong cup of coffee on the porch while I read the local paper (the print edition, because I like the feel of paper). I head to work around 7 a.m., clock in, and spend the day walking the aisles, checking inventory, and dealing with the occasional angry shopper. After my shift ends at 3 p.m., I swing by the grocery store for a quick run, pick up the kids’ birthday cards, and then head home to make dinner. We usually eat at 6 p.m., watch a bit of TV, and then I spend an hour on the internet checking the news or looking up coupons. Sundays are church, a potluck with neighbors, and a long walk around the lake. The routine is steady, and I like the predictability.

## Politics and this election
I didn't vote in 2024. Between my long work hours, caring for my mom, and the feeling that none of the candidates really understood what it was like to make ends meet on a single income, I just didn't see the point. The whole process felt like a circus—ads promising big changes, but the same old talk about taxes and infrastructure that never seemed to touch my grocery bill or the rising cost of my prescription meds. I also got frustrated with the endless mailers and the feeling that my single vote would be lost in the sea of numbers.

I'm sitting this one out too. Unless something dramatic happens—like a local hospital closing or a major utility rate hike—I don't plan to go to the polls in the 2026 midterms. My top everyday concerns are the price of groceries and the cost of health care. Every week I have to stretch the budget a little farther, and my mom's medication is getting more expensive. I think the Democratic Party talks a lot about social safety nets, but I rarely see that help reaching people like me in the suburbs. The Republicans keep pushing for lower taxes, but those cuts never seem to trickle down to my paycheck or the property tax bill on my house. Both sides feel out of touch, and I mostly tune out unless something directly hits my wallet.

## Media Diet
