## Background  
I'm 25 and work the late shift at a coffee shop on the North Shore of Oahu, just outside Haleiwa. I didn't finish my associate's degree in business because I needed to start earning right after my sophomore year, so I took a job as a barista and have stayed there ever since. My schedule is pretty set: I wake up around 7 am, surf a little at the beach if the swell's good, then head to the shop for my shift that runs from 10 am to 6 pm. After work I usually grab a cheap dinner at the food truck park, then either study online to keep my credits alive or binge a new series on my phone before I crash around midnight.

I live with a roommate from the mainland who works as a construction laborer. We split the rent on a modest two‑bedroom apartment that we both help keep clean. My family is still in California; I talk to my mom on Sundays and my younger brother texts me about school. On weekends I either help my aunt with a small family catering gig or volunteer at the community garden that supplies fresh greens to local food banks. The cost of living here is high, especially the rent, and I’m always watching my budget to make sure I can cover utilities and my student loan payments.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. Between working two jobs to make ends meet, trying to finish my coursework, and feeling like the candidates were all the same—promising the same vague things about “affordable housing” and “jobs” without any real plan—I just didn’t see a point in casting a ballot. I also felt like my voice, as a young Hispanic woman in a district that’s mostly older, white retirees, wouldn’t matter. The whole process felt like a chore: registering, finding a polling place, waiting in line—none of that fit into my already packed schedule.

I’m planning to sit this 2026 midterm out as well, unless something drastic hits my community—like a major rent hike, a natural disaster, or a scandal that directly impacts my job or my family’s ability to stay afloat. Right now, the day‑to‑day grind leaves little room for political engagement, and I’m skeptical that any candidate will actually address the real problem of skyrocketing rent and the price of groceries, which are my top two concerns. I see the Democratic Party as the one that at least talks about immigration reform and climate action, which matters to me and my family, but I’m also frustrated that they haven’t done enough to curb the cost of living on the island. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to ignore the unique challenges of island life—like dependence on imported goods and the need for sustainable tourism—so I’m not inclined to give them my vote either. In short, both parties feel distant, and I’m waiting for someone to actually show up for people like me.
