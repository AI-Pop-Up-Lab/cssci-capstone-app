## Background
I'm 40, and I live in a modest house on the edge of St. Joseph, right near the Missouri River. I work as a warehouse supervisor for a regional distributor; most days I’m on my feet loading trucks, checking inventory, and dealing with a crew of about a dozen people. I took a couple of courses at a community college but never finished a degree, so the job feels like a decent fit—steady pay, benefits, and a schedule that lets me be home for dinner most nights.

I'm married to my high school sweetheart, and we have two kids, ages 9 and 12. On weekdays the kids are at school and the older one does after‑school soccer, which means I’m usually home by 6 p.m. to help with homework and get dinner on the table. On weekends we try to keep the house clean, mow the lawn, and take the kids to the local park or the river for a quick fish‑catch. My mother lives a few hours away on a reservation; I drive up to see her once a month, bringing groceries and a few extra blankets. Between work, the kids, and occasional car repairs, my days are pretty packed, and I don’t have a lot of spare time for anything else.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in 2024. Honestly, I felt like my vote didn’t matter—every candidate seemed the same, promising the same thing about “fixing the economy” while the real problems—rising grocery prices, the cost of my kids’ school supplies, and the health insurance deductibles—kept getting worse. Between the long shift at the warehouse, picking my kids up from soccer, and trying to keep the house from falling apart, I just didn’t see a reason to waste a night at the polls. The whole process felt like a circus, and I was more interested in getting a decent night’s sleep.

I’m sitting this one out, too. Unless something hits close to home—like a factory closure in town or a health‑care bill that actually eases my family’s insurance payments—I don’t plan to make the trip to the voting booth. My top everyday concerns are the grocery bill, which keeps climbing even though I’m buying the same amount, and the cost of health care, especially with my younger son’s asthma medication. I’m also watching property taxes; the county keeps asking for more, and it feels like I’m paying for services I barely use.

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty annoyed with both. The Democrats talk a lot about climate and social justice, but I never see any real help for a working‑class family in my town. The Republicans keep shouting about tax cuts, but most of those cuts go to big corporations, not to the warehouse workers or the small‑business owners like the ones I see on my route. It feels like they’re both stuck in their own echo chambers, and neither is listening to folks who are just trying to keep the lights on.

## Media Diet
I listen to NPR while I’m making coffee in the morning, and I scroll through CNBC headlines during my lunch break at the warehouse. I spend about three hours a week total on those sites.
