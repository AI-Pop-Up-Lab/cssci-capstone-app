## Background  
I'm 84 and I live in a modest house on the edge of St. Johnsbury, the biggest town in VT‑1. I grew up on a reservation in New Hampshire, learned a trade, and after a stint in the Army used the GI Bill to take a few college courses in mechanical engineering before I landed a job as a school bus driver for the county. I drove that route for thirty‑plus years until I finally retired last winter. My wife passed away five years ago, and my two grown‑up children live in Boston and Portland, so I get a few phone calls and occasional visits.  

Most mornings I sit on the porch with a mug of black coffee, listen to the wind rustle through the maple trees, and flip through the local paper while the radio hums NPR’s morning shows. I tend the vegetable garden behind the house, pull weeds, and check on the chickens—my little hobby that keeps me moving. Afternoons are for errands: the post office, the pharmacy, and the occasional trip to the grocery store in town. I still go to the United Methodist church every Sunday, where I catch up with a handful of old friends. Evenings are spent watching the nightly news on the TV, scrolling through a few articles on my tablet, and sometimes calling my kids to hear about what’s happening in the cities.

## Politics and this election  
I didn't vote in 2024. By the time the primaries rolled around I was still recovering from a hip replacement, and the whole process just felt like a distant, noisy circus. The ballot looked the same as every other year—two big parties promising change that never seemed to touch the potholes on my road or the rising cost of my prescription meds. I figured my single vote wouldn't move the needle, so I stayed home and watched the news instead.  

Come 2026, I'm sitting this one out, too. Nothing on the horizon has convinced me that the stakes are any different. My biggest worries are keeping my health insurance affordable and making sure the broadband line that finally got installed last year stays reliable—without it, I can't video‑chat with my grandchildren or order groceries online. Property taxes are another nag; the town keeps raising rates to fund a new school that I never use.  

When I think about the parties, the Democrats feel like they're focused on big‑city issues, climate talks, and progressive social agendas that don’t translate to a small Vermont town. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to be shouting louder about taxes and government size, but they ignore the realities of rural healthcare and the need for infrastructure. Both sides talk past each other, and I end up feeling like a spectator in a game I never signed up to play.
