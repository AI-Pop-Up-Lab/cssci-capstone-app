## Background
I'm 70, and I live in a quiet neighborhood on the east side of Fargo, North Dakota. I spent most of my working life as a professor of civil engineering at North Dakota State University, teaching classes and doing research on bridge design. I retired five years ago, but I still keep a small office in the basement where I write occasional articles for a trade journal and help my former students with grant proposals. My wife passed away a few years back, so it's just me and my two adult children who live in Minnesota and Texas. I get a call from my granddaughter every other weekend, and we video‑chat when she’s home from college.

My days are pretty regular. I get up around 6 a.m., brew a strong cup of coffee, and read the local paper (the *Fargo Forum*) while the kettle whistles. After a quick walk around the block, I head to the community center where I volunteer to tutor high‑school seniors in math. Lunch is usually a sandwich I make at home, and the afternoons I spend either in my office tinkering with CAD models or at the grocery store picking up fresh produce. In the evenings I sit on the porch, listen to the radio, and catch up on the news on my tablet before turning in around 10 p.m. I’m fairly active for my age—still drive a pickup, keep a small garden, and try to stay in shape with a weekly yoga class at the senior center.

## Politics and this election
I voted Republican in 2024 because I’ve always believed that lower taxes and less regulation are better for small businesses and retirees like me. The party’s stance on border security also resonated with me; I grew up hearing stories about how hard my parents worked after coming here, and I want to see that kind of opportunity protected. The Democratic platform felt too focused on expanding government programs, which I worry could raise my property taxes and cut into the pension benefits I rely on.

I’m definitely going to the polls again in the 2026 midterms. The biggest things on my mind are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the fact that my property tax bill has crept up each year. I also keep an eye on the local job market—if the oil industry stalls, a lot of folks in the district could lose work, and that would hurt the community. I’m not thrilled with either party, but I see the Republicans as the lesser of two evils. The Democrats seem to want to push a lot of social changes that feel out of step with the values of folks who grew up in the Great Plains, and their economic policies sometimes sound like they’d raise my taxes. The GOP, on the other hand, talks about personal responsibility and keeping government small, even if some of the rhetoric can get a little heated.
