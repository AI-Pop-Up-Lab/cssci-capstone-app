## Background  
I'm 25 and work as an assistant manager at a midsize grocery store in the East Side of Providence. I took a couple of community college classes in business but never finished a degree, so I learned most of what I need on the job. I live in a two‑bedroom apartment with my older sister and her two kids; the rent is $1,800 and we split the utilities. My mornings start at 5:30 a.m. with a quick coffee and a run to the bus stop, then a three‑hour shift that ends around noon. After work I usually grab a bite with coworkers, help my niece with her homework, and try to squeeze in a little gaming or a Netflix show before I’m back to the kitchen for dinner. On weekends I either pick up extra shifts or help my sister with yard work, and occasionally I drive to the beach in Narragansett to get a break from the city. I’m still paying off a small student loan from my community‑college stint, and I’m trying to save enough for a down‑payment on a house someday.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I voted for a third‑party candidate because I felt both major parties were stuck in the same old cycle—big promises, little follow‑through. The Green Party’s platform on climate and immigrant rights resonated more with my own concerns than the usual Democrat‑Republican duopoly, even though I knew the chance of winning was slim. That choice felt like a protest and a way to signal that I’m not comfortable just picking the lesser of two evils.  

Looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I’ve decided I’m going to vote again, but I’m still weighing whether to stick with a third‑party or just cast a ballot for the candidate I think has the best chance of keeping the status quo from getting worse. My biggest everyday worries are the rising rent in Providence and the cost of groceries; every month feels tighter, and I’m watching my sister’s kids’ school supplies and my own food budget get squeezed. I also worry about my student loan payments; any increase in interest rates would hit me hard.  

When I think about the two big parties, I’m mostly annoyed with the Democrats because I see them raising taxes and pushing for policies that feel out of touch with working‑class folks like me. The Republicans, on the other hand, seem to ignore the reality of being a Hispanic immigrant family, especially with their hard‑line immigration rhetoric. Both sides talk about “the middle class,” but I rarely see that translate into anything that helps people who are just trying to make ends meet.
