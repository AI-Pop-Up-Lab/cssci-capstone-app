## Background  
I'm 55 and I work as a professor of Spanish literature at the university in my town, Morehead, Kentucky. I grew up in a Mexican‑American family that moved here when I was a kid, so I’m fluent in both English and Spanish and I still help out at the local community center teaching kids to read. My wife runs a small bakery downtown, and we have two kids—Jenna, 17, who’s finishing high school, and Luis, 15, who’s into video games and soccer. My mother lives with us; she’s in her late 70s and needs help with medication and occasional trips to the doctor.  

My weekdays start early: I’m up by 5:30 a.m., make coffee, and skim the morning headlines on my phone while the coffee brews. I drive the 20‑minute commute to campus, where I teach two classes, hold office hours, and spend a few hours grading papers. Lunch is usually a quick sandwich in the faculty lounge, and I try to catch a short segment of business news on my laptop. After work I swing by the bakery to pick up dinner, then help my kids with homework before we all sit down for a family dinner. Evenings are low‑key—sometimes I watch a documentary, other times I read a novel in Spanish. Weekends are a mix of grocery runs, a church service on Sunday, and occasional trips to my mother’s doctor appointments. Money’s tight enough that we watch the budget, but we’re not living paycheck to paycheck.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t cast a ballot in 2024. Between long hours at the university, caring for my mother, and feeling like the candidates didn’t speak to the mix of issues that matter to me—immigration, education funding, and rising health‑care costs—I just shrugged it off. The whole process felt like a circus, and I was convinced my vote wouldn’t change anything, especially when the local media kept talking about national drama instead of the potholes on our roads or the funding cuts to the community college.  

I’m sitting this one out again in 2026. Nothing dramatic has happened locally to pull me in, and the same sense of cynicism lingers. My top everyday worries are the grocery prices that keep climbing and the property taxes that make it harder to keep the house in good shape. I also worry about my mom’s health bills and whether I’ll be able to afford a decent plan next year.  

When I think about the parties, I’m pretty fed up. The Republicans push a lot of pro‑business rhetoric that sounds good for the mining and timber companies around here, but they rarely talk about immigrant families or the language programs that keep our kids bilingual. The Democrats keep promising better health care and education, yet their national candidates seem out of touch with rural Kentucky life—talking about climate policies that feel like a distant concern while ignoring the real need for a decent road to get to the hospital. Both sides feel like they’re playing a game that leaves people like me on the sidelines.

## Media Diet  
I listen to NPR first thing in the morning while I’m making coffee, check CNBC headlines during lunch, and watch the evening wrap‑up on ABC News after dinner. All together it’s about nine hours a week.
