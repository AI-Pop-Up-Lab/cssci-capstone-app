## Background  
I'm 35 and work as a maintenance technician at a midsize manufacturing plant just outside Durham. I finished an associate’s program in industrial maintenance at a community college, then landed a full‑time job that pays enough to keep a modest apartment in the Southpoint area. I share the place with my partner, Maya, and our two‑year‑old daughter, Luna. Most mornings I’m up by 5:30 a.m. to get Luna to daycare, then I catch the bus to the plant where I spend eight hours troubleshooting equipment, changing filters, and keeping the production line humming. After my shift I usually swing by the grocery store, grab a quick dinner, and try to squeeze in a little TV before bedtime. On weekends I help Maya with chores, take Luna to the park, and sometimes work a second shift if the plant needs overtime. I’m a fan of barbecues on the patio and I try to keep my car fuel‑efficient because gas prices bite hard on my budget. I don’t belong to any clubs or churches, but I do hang out with a few coworkers at the local sports bar after work when we can.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in 2024. To be honest, the whole thing felt like a circus—candidates yelling at each other on TV, ads promising everything, and none of them really speaking to people like me who are juggling a job, a kid, and rising rent. I felt like my single vote wouldn’t change anything, and between long shifts and caring for Luna, I just didn’t make time to get to a polling place. The news kept talking about “the battle for the soul of the nation,” but I was more worried about whether the paycheck would cover the electric bill. That cynicism hasn’t gone away, so I’m sitting this one out too. Unless there’s a local issue that hits my pocket directly—like a proposal to raise property taxes on our building or a sudden cut to Medicaid—I’m not planning to go to the polls in the 2026 midterms.

My biggest everyday worries are rent and grocery prices. The landlord just raised the lease by $150, and the price of fresh produce keeps climbing, which makes budgeting a constant juggling act. When I think about the parties, I’m pretty fed up with both. The Democrats seem to care more about identity politics and big‑city issues than the struggles of working‑class families in places like Durham. The Republicans keep talking about tax cuts and cultural battles, but they don’t really address the cost of living or the need for decent health coverage. It feels like both sides are more interested in scoring points than solving the problems that keep me up at night.
