## Background
I'm 25 and work as a dental hygienist at a clinic in Charlottesville, which sits in Virginia's 3rd district. I grew up in a mixed‑heritage household in Richmond, but after high school I moved to a two‑bedroom apartment near the university to be closer to work and the occasional art shows downtown. My parents still live in a small town about an hour away, so I visit them on holidays and sometimes help out with yard work. I share the apartment with a roommate who does freelance graphic design; we split rent, utilities, and the occasional grocery run. My days start early—by 6 am I’m on a bus heading to the clinic, where I spend eight hours cleaning teeth, taking x‑rays, and chatting with patients about their oral health. After work I either hit the gym or catch a quick coffee at a local café before heading home to cook dinner, usually something simple like pasta or a stir‑fry. Weekends are a mix of laundry, catching a movie at the downtown theater, and volunteering a few hours at a community garden that supplies fresh produce to a local food bank. I don’t own a car, so I rely on public transit and occasional ridesharing.

## Politics and this election
I didn’t vote in the 2024 election because I felt like my vote wouldn’t change anything, and honestly I was swamped with extra shifts at the clinic when the primaries rolled around. The whole process seemed like a circus—ads screaming about the “greatest” candidate, endless rallies, and then the same old promises that never materialized. I’ve got a lot on my plate with rent, student loans, and trying to keep up with grocery prices, so casting a ballot felt like another chore I could skip. I’m still sitting this one out for the 2026 midterms; unless something blows up locally—like a major hospital closure or a sudden spike in property taxes—I can’t see myself making the time to vote.

My biggest everyday worries are paying rent on time and keeping food on the table without breaking the bank. The cost of groceries keeps creeping up, and my landlord’s occasional rent hikes make budgeting a nightmare. When I think about the two major parties, I’m mostly annoyed. The Democrats seem to focus on identity politics and big‑government solutions that feel out of touch with folks like me who just want stable jobs and affordable housing. The Republicans talk a lot about tax cuts and “law and order,” but they ignore the rising cost of living and the fact that many of us rely on public services. Both sides feel like they’re talking past each other, and I’m left wondering whether any candidate actually cares about the day‑to‑day grind.
