## Background
I’m 30 and I work as a data analyst for a midsize health‑tech startup in Allentown. I graduated with a master’s in biostatistics a couple of years ago, and the job lets me play with numbers all day, but I still have to commute about 20 minutes each way on the bus because I can’t afford a car. I live in a two‑bedroom apartment with a roommate who’s in his late twenties, also in tech. We split rent and utilities, and we take turns cooking dinner—mostly quick meals like pasta or stir‑fry because we’re both busy. My mornings start with a quick run around the city park, then I grab a coffee from a corner shop and scroll through the news on my phone before the bus. After work I sometimes stop by the community center to help run a coding workshop for high‑school kids; it’s a way to give back and stay connected to the neighborhood. Weekends are a mix of catching a game with friends, visiting family in Philadelphia, and doing laundry. I’m not married, but I keep in touch with my parents who live in the suburbs and my younger sister who’s still in college.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted for the Democratic candidate because I felt the party was more committed to expanding health coverage and investing in STEM education—things that matter to me personally and to the community I work in. I also appreciated the candidate’s stance on criminal‑justice reform, which resonates with my own experiences and the conversations I have with friends and family about policing. Since then I’ve kept an eye on how the administration handles the rising cost of living; I still think the Democrats are the better bet for protecting programs that keep rent affordable and for pushing for more federal funding for public schools. That said, I get frustrated when politicians promise big changes and then get stuck in partisan fights. I’m planning to go to the polls for the 2026 midterms; I don’t feel like I can sit this one out because the Senate races could affect health‑care policy and the House will decide on the next round of infrastructure spending. My top everyday worries are keeping up with rent and the price of groceries—everything feels like it’s getting more expensive, and I’m juggling a student loan that I’m still paying off. I see the Democratic Party as the one that at least talks about these issues, even if they’re not perfect, while the Republicans seem more focused on tax cuts for the wealthy and less on the day‑to‑day struggles of people like me. I’m annoyed by the constant gridlock, but I’m still leaning toward voting for the candidates who promise concrete support for working‑class families.
