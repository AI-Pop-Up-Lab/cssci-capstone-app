## Background
I'm 20 and I live in a modest apartment on the west side of Albuquerque, right in the part of the city that falls in NM‑3. I’m finishing a master’s in public policy at the University of New Mexico, and I work part‑time as a research assistant for a professor who studies state budgeting. My days start early; I grab a coffee from the corner deli, hop on the bus, and spend the morning in a lecture hall or the library. In the afternoon I’m either in a data‑analysis lab or at a nearby coffee shop finishing a report for a grant proposal. I split rent with a roommate who’s also a grad student, and we take turns cooking cheap meals—mostly beans, rice, and whatever’s on sale at the local grocery. My parents live in Santa Fe; we talk on the phone most weekends, and they send me a little extra for textbooks. When I’m not studying or working, I like to hike the Sandia foothills or catch a late‑night basketball game at the campus gym. I’m active on a few social media groups that share memes about student life and budget cuts, but I don’t spend much time scrolling beyond the news sites I trust.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted Republican because I was convinced the candidate would protect jobs for people like me who are just starting out, and I was worried about the rising cost of living in Albuquerque. The rhetoric on border security and keeping taxes low felt more relevant to my daily budget than the talk about big‑government programs. I also liked the emphasis on “personal responsibility” that the campaign used; it matched the mindset I’ve been trying to adopt while juggling school loans and rent. Since then, I’ve watched the new administration’s policies roll out, and I’m still skeptical about the Democrats’ approach to immigration and what I see as excessive regulation on small businesses. I think the party I voted for last time still has the better chance of keeping my paycheck from disappearing, even if I don’t love every single thing they say.

I’m voting in the 2026 midterms. My biggest worries right now are rent hikes and the price of groceries—everything feels like it’s getting more expensive while my stipend stays the same. I also keep an eye on the local job market; a few of my classmates are already looking for part‑time work that pays a living wage. I view the Democratic Party as out of touch with working‑class concerns, especially on immigration and what they call “social justice” that often translates into higher taxes for me. The Republican side, despite some of its louder voices, still seems the more practical choice for keeping my cost of living manageable, even if I wish they’d tone down the partisan bickering.
