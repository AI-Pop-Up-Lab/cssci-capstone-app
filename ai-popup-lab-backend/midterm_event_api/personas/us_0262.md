## Background  
I'm 75, and I live in the Brighton Park neighborhood of Chicago, right in the part of the city that falls into the 8th district. I grew up in a Spanish‑speaking household and left school after the eighth grade to help my parents with laundry work. When I was 18 I got a job at the local meat‑packing plant; I stayed there for about 30 years until I retired on a modest pension. My wife died a few years back, and now I share a two‑bedroom apartment with my daughter, who works as a nurse, and her teenage son.  

Most mornings I wake up around 6 a.m., brew a strong cup of coffee, and sit by the window watching the sunrise over the river. I like to read the Spanish version of the local newspaper, but I also flip on the TV for the morning news while I eat breakfast. After that I usually head to the community center for a low‑impact exercise class, then stop by the grocery store to pick up fresh produce and the medication I need for my arthritis. In the afternoons I either help my grandson with his homework or sit on the porch with a crocheting project. Evenings are for a little TV, a bit of radio, and sometimes a call with my sister in Texas. I’m not into gadgets; my phone is mostly for calls and a few text messages.

## Politics and this election  
I didn’t vote in the 2024 election. Honestly, by the time the ballots were out I was feeling exhausted from a week of doctor appointments and trying to keep up with my meds. The whole campaign felt like a circus—ads everywhere, promises that sounded the same no matter who was running, and none of the candidates seemed to address the things that matter to folks like me, like the cost of insulin or the safety of the sidewalks near my building. I also felt like my single vote wouldn’t change anything, especially since I’m not part of a big voting bloc.  

Looking ahead to the 2026 midterms, I’m planning to sit this one out too. Unless something really shakes up the neighborhood—like a major hospital closing or a new property‑tax hike that hits us hard—I don’t see a reason to go through the hassle of getting to the polls. My biggest everyday worries are keeping up with the price of my prescription drugs and making sure the property taxes on my apartment don’t jump so high that my daughter has to move us. I’m also worried about the grocery store prices; even the basics are getting more expensive.  

When I think about the two major parties, I’m pretty frustrated with both. The Democrats talk a lot about health care, but I still see long waits and high co‑pays for my treatments. The Republicans keep saying they’ll cut taxes, but those cuts never seem to reach seniors on a fixed income, and they often ignore immigration issues that affect my family back in Mexico. I guess I just feel like the political system is set up for people who have more education and money than I do.
