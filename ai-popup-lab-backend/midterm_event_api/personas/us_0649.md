## Background  
I'm 30 and I work the swing shift as a front‑desk clerk at a casino‑hotel in Sparks, right on the edge of Reno. The job is steady enough to cover my rent on a modest two‑bedroom apartment that I share with my younger sister, who’s still in community college. My mom lives about an hour away on the reservation, so I drive up on weekends to help with groceries and to check on the house.  

Mornings start at 5 a.m. with a quick coffee from the corner diner, then a half‑hour commute while the desert light is still pink. I spend most of the day behind a desk, logging reservations, answering calls, and dealing with the occasional upset guest. After my shift ends around 2 a.m., I’m usually home by 3, grab a snack, and try to catch a few hours of sleep before the next day. On my days off I like to hike the nearby foothills, but the heat in summer makes that a rare treat.  

I don’t have a college degree; after high school I went straight into the service industry to help support my family. I’m proud of the fact that I can fix a broken dishwasher and that I can speak a little Diné (Navajo) with my grandparents, even though most of my life has been in English. My social circle is a mix of coworkers, a few friends from the reservation, and some neighbors I met at the local gym. We trade recipes, talk about the rising cost of gas, and sometimes argue about the best way to vote.

## Politics and this election  
In 2024 I didn’t vote for Democrat or Republican; I cast my ballot for the Green Party’s presidential nominee. I felt both major parties were more interested in fundraising than in actually fixing the water shortages that hit my community every summer, and I was tired of the endless back‑and‑forth on immigration that never seemed to help my extended family back on the reservation. The Green ticket promised stronger environmental protections and more respect for tribal sovereignty, which resonated with my own worries about the desert drying up and the loss of cultural sites.  

That same sense of disillusionment still colors how I look at politics. I’m definitely going to the polls for the 2026 midterms, though I’m not sure which candidate will earn my vote. I’m watching the Senate race closely because the incumbent has been vague about water policy, and the House race matters for the federal funding that could go toward tribal infrastructure. My top everyday concerns are keeping my rent affordable and making sure the grocery store down the street doesn’t raise prices on fresh produce again. I also worry about the state’s push to legalize more gambling venues, which I think will only drive up traffic and strain water supplies.  

When I think about the Democratic Party, I see a lot of talk about climate change but not enough action that reaches my community. The Republican Party feels like it’s more about tax cuts for the wealthy and less about protecting the land my ancestors have lived on. Both sides seem to forget the small‑town folks and the tribal members who just want reliable water and decent jobs. I’m not a fan of the political theater, but I’m willing to give a candidate a chance if they actually lay out a plan that includes tribal input and real funding for drought mitigation.
