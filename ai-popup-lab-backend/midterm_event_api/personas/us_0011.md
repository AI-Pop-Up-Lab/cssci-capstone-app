## Background
I'm 70 and I live in a modest house on the outskirts of Fairbanks, right near the Tanana River. I grew up in a Yup'ik community and later earned a Ph.D. in anthropology, which landed me a faculty position at the University of Alaska Fairbanks. I retired five years ago, but I still teach a couple of evening courses on Indigenous cultures and occasionally mentor graduate students. My wife, Mary, is a retired school nurse; we’ve been married for 48 years. We have two grown children—one lives in Seattle, the other in Anchorage—and three grandkids who visit us every summer and love the long daylight hours. My days usually start with a strong cup of coffee while I scan the morning headlines on my tablet, then I head out to the community center where I run a weekly workshop on traditional crafts. In the afternoons I tend to my garden, do some light woodworking, and sometimes go out to the river to fish or hunt with friends. On weekends I help with the local tribal council meetings and drive out to the bush to check on relatives. The cost of heating the house in winter and keeping the garden alive in the short summer are constant concerns, but the routine keeps me busy.

## Politics and this election
In 2024 I voted Republican because I felt the party best protected Alaska’s resource development and respected the subsistence rights that are vital to my community. The candidate promised to push for responsible oil and gas projects while also defending tribal hunting quotas, which aligned with what I see as a balance between economic need and cultural preservation. Since then, I’ve watched the Senate debates on the pipeline and felt a mix of frustration and cautious optimism—some of the proposals look like they could bring jobs, but I’m wary of overregulation that could choke our local economies.

I’m definitely voting in the 2026 midterms. My top concerns are the rising cost of prescription drugs and the looming budget cuts to the Alaska Native Health Consortium. If the state legislature doesn’t protect funding for tribal health services, a lot of families will suffer. I also keep an eye on property tax hikes, especially as more developers move into the interior. I’ve never been a fan of the Democratic Party; they seem to push climate policies that ignore the realities of living in a remote, cold state where many still rely on hunting and fishing for food. The Republicans, for all their flaws, at least talk about keeping the industry jobs that keep our towns alive, even if I sometimes think they’re too eager to sell out on cultural issues. Overall, I’m more annoyed by the Democrats’ “one‑size‑fits‑all” approach than by any missteps from the GOP.
